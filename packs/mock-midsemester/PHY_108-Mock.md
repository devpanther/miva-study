## Marking scheme

**1. B** — Week 1, *x-intercept from y = mx + c*: x-intercept = −c/m = −(−2)/5 = 0.4. (A comes from using −m/c; C from dropping the negative sign; D from using m/c.)

**2. B** — Week 2, *ammeter placement and design*: a very low resistance in series means the ammeter barely alters the current it is measuring.

**3. B** — Week 3, *procedure, non-ohmic experiment*: the conductor material is chosen from a drop-down list before each run begins.

**4. A** — Week 4, *apparatus, KCL experiment*: three branch currents meet at the junction, and each must be read by its own ammeter.

**5. B** — Week 1, *systematic vs random error*: scatter in both directions that averages out over repeats is the signature of random error, not a fixed instrument bias.

**6. C** — Week 2, *ohmic vs non-ohmic*: a filament lamp heats up and its resistance changes with current, curving its V–I graph; the other three are ohmic under constant conditions.

**7. B** — Week 3, *why the non-ohmic graph curves*: current causes Joule heating (P = I²R), which raises temperature, which changes resistance, so V stops being simply proportional to I.

**8. C** — Week 4, *sign convention on ammeter readings*: a negative reading shows the actual current direction is opposite to the assumed reference, not a fault or zero current.

**9. B** — Week 1, *percentage difference*: (9.65 − 9.81)/9.81 × 100% = −1.63%. (C comes from wrongly dividing by 9.65 instead of the reference value 9.81.)

**10. C** — Week 2, *parallel resistors*: 1/R = 1/4 + 1/12 = 4/12, so R = 3 Ω.

**11. B** — Week 3, *gradient of P vs I²*: (6.4 − 1.6)/(0.10 − 0.02) = 4.8/0.08 = 60, and since P = R·I², the gradient equals R in ohms. (A comes from swapping the axes.)

**12. B** — Week 4, *KCL applied to signed currents*: I3 = I1 + I2 = 2.6 + (−0.9) = 1.7 mA. (A adds the magnitudes and ignores the sign.)

**13. B** — Week 1, *generalised logarithmic linearisation*: taking ln of y = a·e^(bx) gives ln(y) = bx + ln(a), linear in x with gradient b — the same technique used for exponential decay, applied to growth.

**14. B** — Week 2, *non-ohmic behaviour, asymmetric case*: a non-constant resistance and asymmetric response to reversed current is exactly what "non-ohmic" describes; it is not a law violation or a wiring fault.

**15. B** — Week 3, *choosing the right graph*: P vs I² is straight through the origin with gradient R; the raw I–V graph is curved once self-heating is significant, so it has no single constant gradient.

**16. C** — Week 4, *KCL vs KVL*: this experiment verifies current conservation at a node (KCL); the sum of voltage drops around a loop is KVL, a separate law it does not test.

**17. C** — Week 1, *gradient of T² vs L, two-point form*: slope = (3.16 − 0.79)/(0.80 − 0.20) = 2.37/0.60 = 3.95 s²/m; since slope = 4π²/g, g = 4π²/3.95 ≈ 9.99 m/s². (A mistakes the slope itself for g.)

**18. B** — Weeks 2 & 3 (combined), *detecting non-ohmic behaviour*: self-heating effects only become significant over a wide current range, so a narrow low-voltage test can look linear even for a sample that is genuinely non-ohmic at higher power; only the wide-range test with both graphs reliably exposes this.

**19. A** — Weeks 1 & 4 (combined), *percentage error applied to I3-computed vs I3-measured*: |0.92 − 0.89|/0.89 × 100% ≈ 3.4%, small enough to count as experimental tolerance and so to support KCL, using the directly measured value as the reference.

**20. C** — Week 2, *combined series–parallel resistors*: the 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors in parallel give R = (6×3)/(6+3) = 2 Ω; total circuit resistance = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω; I = V/R = 12/6 = 2.00 A. (A treats all three resistors as one series chain; B ignores the 4 Ω resistor; D treats all three as one parallel group.)

**21.** — Week 2 (b, c), with a Week 1 calculation in (a); *Ohm's law, its circuit, and percentage error*
(a) Actual error = |44.6 − 47.0| = 2.4 Ω **[1]**; percentage error = 2.4/47.0 × 100% = 5.11% **[1]**.
(b) V = IR: the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature (and other physical conditions) remain constant. **[1]**
(c) Full credit requires: DC power supply, key/switch, rheostat and resistor R connected in one series loop; the ammeter in series in that same loop (low resistance, so it barely alters the current it measures); the voltmeter connected in parallel across R only (high resistance, so it draws almost no current from R); and the reasoning that series components share the same current while parallel components share the same potential difference. **[2]**

**22.** — Week 3 in (a); Week 4 in (b); *analysing non-ohmic readings, then stating and applying KCL*
(a) Row 1: P = 5.0 × 0.25 = 1.25 W, I² = 0.0625 A². Row 2: P = 9.0 × 0.36 = 3.24 W, I² = 0.1296 A². **[2]** (½ mark per correct value, to a maximum of 2.)
(b)(i) In words: the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving it (conservation of charge). Algebraically, with entering currents positive and leaving currents negative: I1 + I2 − I3 = 0. **[1]**
(ii) Full credit requires: a breadboard carrying the resistor network, two DC power supplies driving the network, three ammeters positioned to read the three branch currents I1, I2 and I3 that meet at the junction, and the statement that I1 and I2 enter the junction while I3 leaves it. **[1]**
(iii) I3 = I1 + I2 = 1.10 + (−0.30) = 0.80 mA. **[1]**

**23.** — *Week 1 (a), Week 2 (b), Week 3 (c)*
(a) 16 = 3.5 × 4 + c ⟹ 16 = 14 + c ⟹ c = 2. **[2]** (1 mark for correct substitution, 1 for the correct final value.)
(b) Current I is plotted on the x-axis; the gradient equals the resistance R. **[1]**
(c) I² (the square of the current) is plotted on the x-axis, because power dissipated by Joule heating is P = I²R — a straight line through the origin only appears when P is plotted against I², not against I itself, since plotting against I would leave the underlying relationship quadratic (curved). **[2]**

## How to read your score

- **30–35 marks**: Strong across all four weeks. Spot-check any single MCQ you missed against its marking-scheme note — it usually points to one specific mix-up (an axis, a sign, a formula) rather than a gap in understanding.
- **24–29 marks**: Solid overall, but check which *type* of question cost you marks. Missed numeric questions (1, 9–12, 17, 19–20, or Section B calculations) mean you should redo the worked examples in the Week 1–4 summaries by hand, not just re-read them. Missed concept questions mean you should re-read the "Commonly confused" section of the matching week's summary.
- **16–23 marks**: Revisit the weeks behind your weakest section. If Section A questions 1, 5, 9, 13 and 17 (Week 1: linearisation and error formulas) gave the most trouble, redo the Week 1 summary's worked examples first. If questions 2, 6, 10, 14, 20 (Week 2: Ohm's law) or 3, 7, 11, 15 (Week 3: non-ohmic/Joule heating) or 4, 8, 12, 16 (Week 4: KCL) were the weak spot, redo that week's apparatus-and-circuit description from memory, then check it against the summary.
- **Below 16 marks**: Go back to all four week summaries and, for each, redraw the circuit from memory, restate the governing law, and redo every worked numeric example before attempting this mock again. Don't move on to Week 5 material until this range closes.

This mock deliberately did not repeat any question from the Week 1–4 question sets — if a question here felt unfamiliar even though the topic is familiar, that is expected; go back to the relevant week's summary rather than the old question set.

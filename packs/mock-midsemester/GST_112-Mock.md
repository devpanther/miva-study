## Section B — Structured questions (3 questions, 5 marks each)

**21.** Using two named examples — one of conflict and one of trade or religious borrowing — explain why this week's material insists that conflict and cooperation among pre-colonial Nigerian peoples were not opposites, but two kinds of evidence for the same underlying intergroup relations. *(5 marks)*

**22.** Explain how the Royal Niger Company's own conduct as both trading company and administrator, combined with external pressures between 1891 and 1896, brought about the revocation of its Charter. Name at least three actors or events in your explanation. *(5 marks)*

**23.** Answer all three parts.
(a) Define nation-building and national integration, and state which one is the "effort" and which is the "outcome." *(2 marks)*
(b) Name the constitution that first introduced the elective principle in Nigeria, and state the criticism the source says "sowed the seed of disintegration." *(2 marks)*
(c) In one or two sentences, explain how the criticism you named in (b) illustrates the national-integration challenge you defined in (a). *(1 mark)*

## Marking scheme

**1. B** — **Week 1** — *the three components of culture*: technological, sociological, ideological. "Political" and "legal" are plausible but not the named components — sociological covers institutions/norms (which includes governance-adjacent norms) without being a fourth "political" category.

**2. C** — **Week 2** — *traditions of origin*: both the Bayajidda (Hausa) and Saif (Kanuri) legends trace back to **Baghdad**.

**3. A** — **Week 3** — *origins of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade*: Portugal began it in the **15th century** after developing maritime capability.

**4. B** — **Week 4** — *the Nigerian Council*: set up by Lugard in **1914**, 36 members, advisory only, but foundational for later constitutional development.

**5. C** — **Week 1** — *functionalism*: society as an organism in which each institution plays a role contributing to the survival of the whole.

**6. B** — **Week 2** — *conflict as an indicator*: Kano fought Katsina for **control of trade routes**.

**7. B** — **Week 3** — *Buxton's 1833 bill*: argued the trade had to be stopped **"from Africa" itself** — the reasoning the source names as the chain leading to the 1861 annexation of Lagos. (Option D wrongly borrows Beecroft's 1849 appointment, a separate, earlier development.)

**8. B** — **Week 4** — *the elective principle*: the Clifford Constitution's introduction of it triggered the **formation of political parties and newspapers**.

**9. B** — **Week 1** — *nation-building vs national integration*: nation-building is the active effort (mobilising resources, forging identity); national integration is the resulting unification of diverse groups — effort vs outcome.

**10. A** — **Week 2** — *religious borrowings*: **Ekpe and Ekpo** are Efik in origin; **Ibini Ukpabi** is Ibibio in origin. Both became popular among Igbo subgroups, which is why they get swapped.

**11. B** — **Week 3** — *the Royal Charter*: refused in 1881, capital £1m in 1882, charter finally granted in **1886**, creating the Royal Niger Company. (1884 is Hewett's treaty tour/the Oil River Protectorate; 1887 is when RNC came to control the whole Niger basin; 1893 is the Niger Coast Protectorate rename — all real but wrong for this question.)

**12. C** — **Week 4** — *Donald Cameron (1930–1936)*: abolished the Lieutenant Governor's office and the provincial court, and introduced separation of powers. Bourdillon, his successor from 1935, instead prepared the ground for the next constitution.

**13. A** — **Week 1** — *colonialism's impacts*: governance, culture, and family structures.

**14. B** — **Week 2** — *conqueror lists*: Benin's conquests reached **Auchi, Idah, Itsekiri, Urhobo, Isoko, Okpe** (plus an expedition to Eko/Lagos) — a separate list from Jukun's.

**15. A** — **Week 3** — *RNC's decline, dated*: **Ralph Moor** was appointed Consul of the ORP in **1891**; Chamberlain's appointment and the Brass raid on Akassa both belong to **1895**, four years later — different stages, don't merge them.

**16. B** — **Week 4** — *the three constitutions' defining features*: Richards (1947) — regionalism; Macpherson (1951) — the House of Representatives; Lyttleton (1954) — the federal system.

**17. B** — **Weeks 1–2** — *scale of Nigeria's diversity*: **374** is the ethnic-group figure (the larger of the two); 348 is the language-group figure, offered here as the direct neighbouring-number distractor.

**18. A** — **Weeks 2–3** — *trans-Saharan vs trans-Atlantic trade*: the pre-colonial trans-Saharan network (Kanem Bornu, Yoruba/Nupe participation) is a different system from, and was overshadowed by, the trans-Atlantic slave trade that Portugal began in the 15th century.

**19. A** — **Weeks 3–4** — *chronological ordering*: Beecroft's appointment (1849) → annexation of Lagos (1861) → the Nigerian Council (1914) → the Clifford Constitution (1922). Option D keeps the right start but swaps the middle two — check dates, not just "sounds plausible."

**20. A** — **Weeks 1&4** — *the 1914 Amalgamation debate*: one school holds it merely formalised pre-existing inter-group relations; the other that it created a deeply divided nation — directly bearing on Week 1's national-integration challenge.

**21.** — **Week 2** — *conflict and trade as joint evidence of intergroup relations*. Full marks require: (i) one correctly named example of conflict that produced contact rather than separation — e.g. the Igala–Nsukka Igbo wars, evidenced by the Illah people, who speak Igbo but whose masquerades speak Igala; or the Emowa/Isoko sub-groups' gods still located at Patani, an Ijaw town, as cultural diffusion driven by war (2 marks); (ii) one correctly named example of trade or religious borrowing showing the same peoples in contact through cooperation — e.g. Oyo and Nupe fought repeated wars while also trading (shea butter, bangles, brasswork, potash, straw hats), or the spread of Ekpe/Ekpo (Efik) and Ibini Ukpabi (Ibibio) among the Igbo (2 marks); (iii) an explicit statement that conflict and cooperation are treated as two indicators of the *same* underlying relations, not opposites (1 mark).

**22.** — **Week 3** — *the Royal Niger Company's fall*. Full marks require, in a connected explanation rather than a list: RNC's own conduct — running as both trading company and administrator, paying dividends only from trade profits, buying out rivals, taxing competitors, monopolising imports and manipulating produce prices — created enemies (2 marks); **Sir Ralph Moor's** appointment as Consul of the Oil River Protectorate in **1891** put a rival authority on the ground (1 mark); a price war and smuggling further weakened RNC's position (1 mark); in **1895**, **Joseph Chamberlain's** appointment as Colonial Secretary and the **Brass people's raid on Akassa** combined to expose RNC, leading to the **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** and the revocation of its Charter (1 mark). At least three named actors/events must appear for full marks.

**23.** — **Weeks 1 & 4** (multi-part).
(a) *[2 marks]* **Week 1** — 1 mark for defining nation-building (mobilising resources for socio-economic/political development + constructing a national identity) and national integration (unifying diverse groups into a cohesive nation); 1 mark for correctly stating nation-building = the effort, national integration = the outcome.
(b) *[2 marks]* **Week 4** — 1 mark for naming the **Clifford Constitution (1922)**; 1 mark for the criticism that the **North was unrepresented**, which the source says sowed the seed of disintegration.
(c) *[1 mark]* **Weeks 1–4 bridge** — 1 mark for linking the North's exclusion from Clifford's 45-member council directly to the difficulty of forging one shared national identity across Nigeria's ethnic groups — i.e. the constitutional starting point of the national-integration problem defined in (a).

## How to read your score

- **30–35 (86%+)**: Exam-ready across all four weeks. Spend remaining revision time on the cross-week questions (17–20) and Section B mark breakdowns — that is where marks are lost even by strong students.
- **23–29 (66–83%)**: Solid but uneven. Check which week's questions you missed — if it clusters in one week, re-read that week's Summary and "Commonly confused" section before moving on. Named-particulars slips (dates, actors) are the most common cause at this band.
- **14–22 (40–63%)**: Not yet exam-ready. Re-sit each week's own Question Set (packs/week-0N) before attempting another mock — you likely need to rebuild the named-particulars layer (dates, founders, treaties) before the cause-and-effect and cross-week questions will click.
- **Below 14 (<40%)**: Go back to the four Week 1–4 Summaries in full. Do not attempt another mock until you can reproduce, unaided, the four constitutions' years and features, the five parties and their founders, and the 1851–1861–1886 date chains from Weeks 3–4 — those alone account for a large share of this paper's marks.

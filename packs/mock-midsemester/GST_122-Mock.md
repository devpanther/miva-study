# GST_122 — Mid-semester Mock (Weeks 1–4)

*Communication in English II · Weeks 1-4 · **40 minutes** · Closed book · 35 marks total · Sit this in one unbroken sitting and mark it only when you finish.*

## Section A — Multiple choice (20 questions, 1 mark each)

**1.** During the seminar, Ade keeps his phone away, jots down the presenter's key claims, and prepares a question challenging one of them. This demonstrates:
A. Passive listening, since Ade is quiet throughout
B. Hearing, since Ade is merely present in the room
C. Active listening, since Ade is fully engaged, processing and evaluating what is said
D. Descriptive writing, since Ade is recording what he hears

**2.** Chiamaka was in the room when the announcement played over the intercom, but she could not later say what it said. This is best described as:
A. Active listening, because she was physically present
B. Passive listening, because some processing occurred
C. Hearing, because the sound registered without comprehension or retention
D. Reading comprehension failure, because she did not take notes

**3.** "Slender" and "skinny" both denote a thin body, but "skinny" often carries a more negative, unflattering ____ than "slender."
A. Denotation
B. Referent
C. Signified
D. Connotation

**4.** Bola reads a novel slowly, pausing to consider the narrator's tone and the meaning behind a character's silence. This best illustrates:
A. Passive listening, because Bola is not speaking aloud
B. The receptive skill of reading, since Bola is interpreting subtext in written language
C. The productive skill of writing, because Bola is engaging critically with the text
D. Active listening, because Bola is fully engaged with the material

**5.** A pavement is still wet, and a passer-by concludes it must have rained recently. This is best classified as which type of sign?
A. Icon, because the pavement resembles rain
B. Index, because the wet pavement is physically caused by, and therefore connected to, the rain
C. Symbol, because wetness conventionally means rain
D. Referent, because the pavement is the actual object being discussed

**6.** "The proposal was rejected, the board offered no explanation." What is the best way to fix this sentence?
A. Leave it as is; a comma alone may join two independent clauses
B. Insert "so" after the comma, or replace the comma with a semicolon
C. Replace the comma with a colon, since the second clause explains the first
D. Add a hyphen between "rejected" and "the"

**7.** "The Lagos sky burned orange as market women packed away their wares, the smell of roasting corn thick in the evening air." This passage is best classified as:
A. Narrative writing, because it recounts a market woman's actions in the order they happened
B. Descriptive writing, because it uses rich sensory detail to paint a single moment, without developing a sequence of events
C. Expository writing, because it explains how markets close for the day
D. Persuasive writing, because it argues that Lagos markets should stay open later

**8.** In the sentence "The exhausted athlete finally crossed the finish line," which word is a function word rather than a content word?
A. Exhausted — because it modifies the noun
B. Finally — because adverbs are always function words
C. The — because articles are a closed class that carries grammatical structure rather than independent meaning
D. Crossed — because verbs are always function words

**9.** For the word "Kano," the printed letters K-A-N-O are the ____, the mental concept of the city they evoke is the ____, and the actual city in northern Nigeria is the ____.
A. Signified; signifier; referent
B. Signifier; signified; referent
C. Referent; signifier; signified
D. Signifier; referent; signified

**10.** "There is only one thing left to do ____ apologise." Which punctuation mark correctly fills the gap, and why?
A. A semicolon, because both sides of the gap are independent clauses
B. A colon, because "apologise" explains or completes what "the one thing" is, and need not be a full clause
C. A comma, because short additions only need a comma
D. A hyphen, because "to do apologise" functions as a compound phrase

**11.** A lecturer says, "It's getting a bit warm in here," and a student gets up to open the window. The lecturer's literal statement about temperature is its ____ meaning; the student's understanding of it as a request is its ____ meaning.
A. Connotative; denotative
B. Semantic; pragmatic
C. Denotative; connotative
D. Pragmatic; semantic

**12.** "Tunde finished his assignment, submitted it online, and went to bed early." By structure, this sentence is:
A. Compound, because three actions are joined by "and"
B. Simple, because "Tunde" is a single subject governing one compound verb, making it one independent clause
C. Complex, because "and" is functioning here as a subordinating conjunction
D. Compound-complex, because the sentence contains three verbs

**13.** "Renewable energy reduces long-term costs and cuts carbon emissions. Nigeria should adopt solar incentives now, before the window to lead the region closes." This passage is best classified as:
A. Expository, because it presents facts about renewable energy
B. Persuasive, because, despite opening with factual claims, it goes on to urge a specific action ("should adopt... now")
C. Narrative, because it describes a sequence of policy events
D. Descriptive, because it uses vivid sensory language

**14.** "The three students' essay's were graded by two lecturers." What is the punctuation error?
A. No error
B. "students'" should be "student's," because only one student is meant
C. "essay's" should be "essays" — a plain plural takes no apostrophe; it is neither a possessive nor a contraction here
D. "were" should be "was," for subject-verb agreement

**15.** "The committee agreed to review the proposal submitted by the department with the new budget." Why is this sentence ambiguous?
A. Because "budget" is a homophone
B. Because it is unclear whether "with the new budget" describes the department or the proposal
C. Because "committee" is a collective noun with an unstable connotation
D. Because "agreed" is a function word being misused as a content word

**16.** Read the paragraph:

*"Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants convert sunlight into chemical energy. Chlorophyll in the leaves absorbs light, which is then used to combine carbon dioxide and water into glucose. Oxygen is released as a by-product of this reaction."*

Which option correctly identifies this paragraph's writing style and the role of its first sentence?
A. Narrative style; the first sentence is a supporting sentence giving an example
B. Expository style; the first sentence is the topic sentence, stating the paragraph's main idea
C. Persuasive style; the first sentence is a transition word signalling contrast
D. Descriptive style; the first sentence uses sensory detail to set a scene

**17.** "The range of solutions offered by the students ____ impressive." Which verb correctly completes this sentence, and why?
A. Are — because "students" is the nearest plural noun
B. Is — because the true subject is "range" (singular); "of solutions offered by the students" is a modifying phrase, not the subject
C. Were — because collective subjects default to plural in formal writing
D. Have been — because multiple solutions were offered

**18.** "Never underestimate the power of a single vote." Which option correctly identifies both this sentence's writing style and its sentence function?
A. Descriptive style; declarative function
B. Persuasive style; imperative function
C. Expository style; exclamatory function
D. Narrative style; interrogative function

**19.** "Facing a single rival in the primary, the senator called his opponents plan reckless and irresponsible, a stark contrast to his own careful, measured proposal." Which option correctly identifies both the connotative effect of the senator's word choice and the punctuation error in the sentence?
A. "Reckless" and "irresponsible" carry strongly negative connotations chosen to sway opinion against a single named rival; "opponents" should be "opponent's," showing that one opponent possesses the plan
B. "Reckless" and "irresponsible" are neutral, purely denotative terms; "opponents" should become "opponents'," since there are several rivals
C. The words connote calm approval; the sentence needs a colon after "reckless" to introduce a list
D. The words carry no connotation at all; "opponents" is already correctly punctuated

**20.** "Neither the interns nor the supervisor were told to send the file to the design head in Enugu." This sentence contains two separate errors, each tested in a different week. Which option correctly identifies both?
A. A concord error ("were" should be "was," agreeing with the nearer subject, "supervisor") and an ambiguity error (it is unclear whether "in Enugu" describes the design head's location or the destination for sending the file)
B. A homophone error ("told" vs "toll") and a punctuation error (a missing comma after "interns")
C. A collective-noun error ("interns" should take a singular verb) and a denotation error (the wrong dictionary meaning of "design")
D. A proximity-agreement error requiring "are" instead of "were," and a semantic error (the sentence has no literal meaning at all)

## Section B — Structured questions (3 questions, 5 marks each)

**21.** Read the paragraph:

*"Every year, our city sends thousands of tonnes of waste to landfill instead of investing in recycling infrastructure. This is not simply inefficient — it is a betrayal of future generations. We must demand that our leaders fund proper recycling programmes now, before the damage becomes irreversible."*

(a) Identify the writing style used in this paragraph. Justify your answer with one piece of textual evidence. (2 marks)

(b) The paragraph uses the word "waste" rather than a neutral synonym such as "discarded material." State the denotation of "waste," explain its connotation, and explain why the writer likely chose it over a neutral alternative. (3 marks)

**22.** Read the passage:

*"The number of applications for the scholarship have increased this year. Either the coordinator or the reviewers is expected to shortlist candidates by Friday, although the final list depend on the dean's approval."*

(a) By structure, classify the second sentence (the one beginning "Either the coordinator..."). (1 mark)

(b) By function, classify the second sentence. (1 mark)

(c) This passage contains three separate subject-verb agreement errors. Identify and correct each one, and state which concord rule applies in each case. (3 marks)

**23.** Read the passage:

*"Its essential to plan an essay before writing it. Skipping this step is risky, it often produces weak, poorly organised paragraphs. A well known technique is to draft a topic sentence first and then list supporting points that connect to it directly."*

(a) Identify and correct the three punctuation errors in this passage, naming the rule broken in each case. (3 marks)

(b) Identify the topic sentence of this paragraph and explain, using the definition of a topic sentence, why it qualifies. (2 marks)

## Marking scheme

**1. C** — *Week 1, active listening*: Ade takes notes, evaluates claims and prepares a challenge — full engagement, not mere presence.

**2. C** — *Week 1, hearing vs listening*: the sound registered but nothing was understood or retained — this is hearing, not listening.

**3. D** — *Week 2, connotation*: "skinny" and "slender" share a denotation (thin) but "skinny" carries a harsher, more negative connotation.

**4. B** — *Week 1, reading (receptive skill)*: Bola is interpreting subtext in a written text — the definition of reading, not listening or writing.

**5. B** — *Week 2, index*: the wet pavement is physically/causally connected to the rain that caused it.

**6. B** — *Week 4, comma splice*: a comma alone cannot join two independent clauses; fix with a coordinating conjunction or a semicolon.

**7. B** — *Week 1, descriptive writing*: sensory detail paints one moment, with no developing sequence of events (which would make it narrative).

**8. C** — *Week 3, function vs content words*: "the" is an article, a closed-class function word; adverbs and verbs are open-class content words, not "always" function words as B and D wrongly claim.

**9. B** — *Week 2, signifier/signified/referent*: the letters are the signifier, the mental concept is the signified, the real city is the referent.

**10. B** — *Week 4, colon*: "apologise" explains what "the one thing" is; it need not be a full independent clause, so a colon (not a semicolon) is correct.

**11. B** — *Week 2, semantic vs pragmatic meaning*: the literal statement about temperature is semantic; its use as a request is pragmatic.

**12. B** — *Week 3, simple sentence with a compound verb*: one subject ("Tunde") governs a compound verb; still one independent clause, hence simple, not compound.

**13. B** — *Week 1, persuasive vs expository*: the passage opens with facts but exists to urge a specific action ("should adopt... now"), which makes it persuasive.

**14. C** — *Week 4, apostrophe in plurals*: "essay's" is a plain plural here (no possession, no contraction) and should be "essays"; "students'" is already correctly formed.

**15. B** — *Week 2, ambiguity*: "with the new budget" could modify either "department" or "proposal" — the sentence permits more than one reading.

**16. B** — *Week 1 + Week 4, writing style and topic sentence*: the paragraph explains a process (expository), and its first sentence states the main idea and previews the paragraph — the topic sentence.

**17. B** — *Week 3, concord (prepositional phrase decoy)*: the true subject is "range" (singular); "of solutions offered by the students" is a modifying phrase, so the verb must agree with "range."

**18. B** — *Week 1 + Week 3, writing style and sentence function*: the sentence urges the reader to act (persuasive) and is a command with an understood "you" (imperative), regardless of having no exclamation mark.

**19. A** — *Week 2 + Week 4, connotation and apostrophe*: "reckless"/"irresponsible" are chosen for negative emotional force against a named single rival; "opponents" should be "opponent's" (one opponent, shown by "a single rival").

**20. A** — *Week 2 + Week 3, concord and ambiguity*: proximity agreement requires "was" (agreeing with the nearer, singular subject "supervisor"), and "in Enugu" is genuinely ambiguous between modifying "design head" and "the file."

**21.** — *Week 1 + Week 2, writing style and denotation/connotation* (5 marks)
(a) 2 marks: style = persuasive (1 mark) with valid textual justification, e.g. "We must demand..." (1 mark).
(b) 3 marks: denotation of "waste" = discarded/unwanted material (1 mark); connotation = carries negative, careless/shameful associations beyond the neutral "discarded material" (1 mark); explains the rhetorical effect — the word choice makes the reader feel the practice is blameworthy, reinforcing the persuasive aim (1 mark).

**22.** — *Week 3, sentence structure, function and concord* (5 marks)
(a) 1 mark: complex (one independent clause + a dependent clause introduced by "although").
(b) 1 mark: declarative (states information; no command, question, or exclamation).
(c) 3 marks (1 each): "have increased" → "has increased" (true subject "number," singular; "of applications..." is a decoy prepositional phrase); "is expected" → "are expected" (Rule of Proximity Agreement: with "either...or," the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "reviewers," plural); "depend" → "depends" (true subject "list," singular).

**23.** — *Week 4, punctuation and topic sentence* (5 marks)
(a) 3 marks (1 each): "Its essential" → "It's essential" (contraction of "it is," not the possessive "its"); "risky, it often produces" → comma splice, fix with a semicolon ("risky; it often produces...") or a coordinating conjunction; "well known technique" → "well-known technique" (hyphenated compound adjective before a noun).
(b) 2 marks: topic sentence = "It's essential to plan an essay before writing it" (1 mark, correct identification) — it states the paragraph's main idea and is developed by the sentences that follow (1 mark, justification tied to the definition of a topic sentence).

## How to read your score

Total this paper by week, not just overall — the marking scheme labels every question by the week it tests, so tally your marks per week to find your actual weak spot rather than guessing from the overall percentage.

- **30–35 (86%+):** Strong across all four weeks. Skim your weakest week's summary once more and move on.
- **24–29 (69–85%):** Solid, but check which week's questions you lost marks on. If it's Week 3 or Week 4, redo the concord and punctuation "commonly confused" sections — those are the exam's favourite traps.
- **18–23 (51–68%):** Patchy. Add up your marks for Weeks 1–4 separately. Whichever week scores under half its share, re-read that week's Summary in full before your next mock, not just the "commonly confused" list.
- **Below 18 (under 51%):** Re-sit the four weekly Question Sets first (Weeks 1–4), then this mock again in a week. Prioritise Week 3 (concord, sentence structure) and Week 4 (punctuation) — they carry the most trap-style questions and the most marks in Section B.

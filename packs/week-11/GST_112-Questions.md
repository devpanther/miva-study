## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Set out the three nationalist politicians the lesson opens with — Herbert Macaulay, Obafemi Awolowo and Nnamdi Azikiwe. For each give the dates, the office or party attached to him, and what the lesson says he contributed. Add what the Zikist movement was and who inspired it.

**14.** Give a full account of Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti: her dates, her moniker and why she earned it, what she organised and what it produced, and the sequence of events that led to her death. Say how she is connected to another figure on the same list.

**15.** Three of the makers criticised the Nigerian polity from outside government — Chinua Achebe, Wole Soyinka and Gani Fawehinmi. For each, state the dates, the vocation, the concrete acts of criticism the lesson records, and the words or cases by which each is remembered.

## Answers

**1. b** — *Macaulay's tag*: he is "**tagged the father of Nigerian nationalism**", **one of the key figures who helped lay the foundation of modern Nigeria**. Option (a) is **Awolowo**; option (c) is **Azikiwe**, who was **last governor-general 1960–1963**; option (d) is again **Azikiwe**, who **inspired the Zikist movement**. Macaulay's own creation is a **party**, not a movement.

**2. d** — *the NNDP, 1923*: the **Nigerian National Democratic Party**, **Nigeria's first political party**, **whose members were the first to sit in the legislative council** and which **held sway in Nigeria's political theatre until the late 1930s**. Option (a) misattributes the legislative-council fact to the **Nigerian Youth Movement**, which in this deck is the body the NNDP **later merged with**. Option (b) gives Macaulay **Awolowo's** party. Option (c) gives him the **NCNC**, which on the deck's account came **after** the merger, not in 1923.

**3. a** — *the deck's merger story*: the NNDP **joined forces with the Nigerian Youth Movement** in the **late 1930s** to form the **NCNC**, "**a nationalist organisation designed to advocate for Nigeria's independence**". Option (b) invents a merger and misnames Balewa's party as the deck does elsewhere. Option (c) confuses the **Zikist movement** — a **youth body**, not a party — with the NCNC. Option (d) gets the PRP wrong: it is **Achebe's** post-civil-war party, unconnected to the Action Group. (See the source note in the summary: the deck's own Azikiwe slide dates his politics to **1944**, which sits awkwardly with the late-1930s merger.)

**4. b** — *Awolowo's office*: **first premier of the western region from 1954 to 1960**. Option (a) is **Azikiwe's** presidency. Option (c) is **Azikiwe's** governor-generalship. Option (d) keeps the right dates but moves him to the **Northern Region**; Awolowo's base and his development record are **Western**, and it was the **Balewa-led government** in the north he opposed.

**5. c** — *the 1954 Lyttelton Constitution* (spelled "Lyleton" in the deck): while he **led the Action Group**, Awolowo was **at the forefront of the campaign for a federal constitution, which was introduced in the 1954 Lyttelton Constitution**. The Richards, Macpherson and Independence constitutions are real instruments but the lesson names **only** the 1954 one, and it is the one that carries **federalism**, the principle Awolowo called **the only basis for equitable national integration**.

**6. a** — *Azikiwe's two offices, in the right order*: **last governor-general of Nigeria from 1960 to 1963**, **then** the **country's first elected president from 1963 to 1966 during the first republic**. Option (b) reverses the two offices and their dates — the presidency came **second**, and it was the governor-generalship he held at independence. Option (c) backdates the governor-generalship to 1954 and leaves the presidency undated. Option (d) makes him the **leader of the opposition**, which in this deck is **Awolowo's** description.

**7. b** — *the Lioness of Lisabi*: **as a revolutionary, she was known to have used her influence to mobilise women and organise resistance against colonialism at the time**, "**earning her the moniker**". Options (a) and (c) invent a court case and a party that the lesson nowhere gives her; her organisational achievement is the **FNWS**, a **women's group**, not a political party. Option (d) borrows the **market women** — whom she did mobilise, for **picnics and festivals** — and wrongly attaches them to the **NCNC**.

**8. a** — *the 1953 Abeokuta conference*: she **organised a conference in Abeokuta to discuss women's welfare**, and "**the gathering gave birth to**" the **Federation of Nigerian Women's Societies (FNWS)**, which **set out to fight for women's rights against all odds**. Option (b) takes the **picnics and festivals**, which the lesson lists as events she mobilised market women for, not as the FNWS's origin. Option (c) inverts the chronology — the raid is **1977**, twenty-four years later. Option (d) puts her inside **Macaulay's** party, a link the lesson never makes.

**9. c** — *the raid, the fall, and the two dates*: on **18 February 1977** hundreds of soldiers **raided the Lagos residence of Fela Kuti, her son**; the **76-year-old** was **thrown from a second-storey window**, **sustained injuries** and died on **13 April 1978**. Option (b) collapses injury and death into one date — the whole point is that they are **fourteen months apart**. Option (a) moves her death to the year of the **conference**. Option (d) attaches her to the **civil war** and misstates her age; she was **76** at the raid and her dates are **1900–1978**.

**10. a** — *Afrobeat*: Fela is "**widely credited as the creator of Afrobeat in the early 1970s, which rapidly became the most avidly followed African style of music**", and he **used words as a form of protest and resistance**. Option (b) belongs to **Achebe**, who headed the **Biafra Broadcasting Service**. Option (c) belongs to **Soyinka**, the first African **Nobel laureate in Literature (1986)**. Option (d) substitutes a different genre and a decade the lesson does not give — the deck's decade is the **1970s**, through which he **rose to prominence**.

**11. b** — *Achebe's landmark facts*: **Things Fall Apart (1958)**, "**often considered his masterpiece**" and **the most widely read book in modern African literature**; he **openly supported the secessionist Biafra movement** and **took a position as head of the Biafra Broadcasting Service at great risk to his life and career**; after the war he was active in the **left-leaning People's Redemption Party**, becoming its **deputy national vice president in 1983**. Option (a) misdates the novel to **1938** and swaps both the broadcasting post and the party. Option (c) misdates **The Trouble with Nigeria** to 1958 — that is the novel's year — and hands him **Azikiwe's** movement and **Awolowo's** party. Option (d) grafts **Soyinka's** imprisonment and Nobel onto Achebe.

**12. a** — *Soyinka's dates kept apart*: **as a 33-year-old graduate in 1967 he was imprisoned by the Nigerian military government for 22 months for speaking out against the Nigerian civil war**; **in 1994 he fled the country** after being **charged with treason** for criticising the military junta; and in **1986** he received the **Nobel Prize for Literature**, **the first African to do so**, using the acceptance speech to focus attention on **white rule in South Africa** and **dedicating the award to the imprisoned Nelson Mandela**. Option (b) moves the imprisonment to **1994**, which is the year he **fled**, and redirects the dedication. Option (c) turns the **22 months** into 33 (his age), reverses his stance on the war — he **opposed** it, while **Achebe** supported **Biafra** — and moves the prize to **1999**, the year of the **return to civilian rule**. Option (d) makes it the **Peace** Prize and says "first Nigerian" where the deck says **first African**.

**13.** *The three nationalist politicians.* **Herbert Macaulay (1864–1946)** is **tagged the father of Nigerian nationalism** and was **one of the key figures who helped lay the foundation of modern Nigeria**. During the **1920s**, when Nigeria began to witness **political agitation for self-rule**, he was among the **first generation of Nigerian nationalists** protesting British colonial policies including **water rates, land issues, and the management of railway finances**. In **1923** he founded the **Nigerian National Democratic Party (NNDP)**, **Nigeria's first political party**, **whose members were the first to sit in the legislative council**; it **held sway in Nigeria's political theatre until the late 1930s**, when it **joined forces with the Nigerian Youth Movement to form the National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons (NCNC)**, "**a nationalist organisation designed to advocate for Nigeria's independence**". **Obafemi Awolowo (1909–1987)** was the **first premier of the western region from 1954 to 1960** and **another nationalist who played an active role in the struggle for Nigeria's independence**; for some he was **Nigeria's foremost federalist**, advocating **federalism as the only basis for equitable national integration**. Leading the **Action Group (AG)** he was **at the forefront of the campaign for a federal constitution, which was introduced in the 1954 Lyttelton Constitution**, and as **leader of the opposition** he **frequently challenged the policies of the Balewa-led government**. He was **one of the country's leading social democratic politicians**, standing in his agitation for self-rule for **economic and social development, especially in the Western Region**. **Nnamdi Azikiwe (1904–1996)** was **a leading figure of modern Nigerian nationalism** who **spent the better part of his life working to end British control of Nigeria, both as a journalist and a politician**. He served as the **last governor-general of Nigeria from 1960 to 1963** and as the **country's first elected president from 1963 to 1966 during the first republic**. He **joined politics in 1944**, after a **successful stint as a journalist**, and **co-founded the NCNC**. **The Zikist movement**, which **Azikiwe inspired**, was **a radical revolutionary and multi-ethnic youth body which championed the Nigerian struggle against imperialism and the belief that Nigerians and indeed Africans should manage their own affairs.**

**14.** *Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti (1900–1978).* She **stood against British colonial rule, demanding the rights of citizens in the pre-independence era**. **As a revolutionary, she was known to have used her influence to mobilise women and organise resistance against colonialism at the time, earning her the moniker "Lioness of Lisabi".** She **mobilised market women for events such as picnics and festivals**, and in **1953** she **organised a conference in Abeokuta to discuss women's welfare**; **the gathering gave birth to a women's group known as the Federation of Nigerian Women's Societies (FNWS)**, which **set out to fight for women's rights against all odds**. The **most memorable point of her life, which unfortunately resulted in her death**, came on **18 February 1977**, when **hundreds of soldiers raided the Lagos residence of Fela Kuti, her son, who was also a known activist** — this is her connection to another maker on the list, **Fela Anikulapo-Kuti (1938–1997)**, the creator of **Afrobeat**. **The 76-year-old Ransome-Kuti was thrown from a second-storey window, during which she sustained injuries that eventually led to her death on 13 April 1978.** **Cheryl Johnson-Odim described her as "the only Nigerian woman of her time to rank as such a national figure or have the international exposure and connections of Ransome-Kuti."** The three dates to keep apart are **1953** (the conference and the FNWS), **18 February 1977** (the raid and the fall) and **13 April 1978** (her death from those injuries).

**15.** *Three critics of the polity.* **Chinua Achebe (1930–2013)** was **one of the most acclaimed novelists of his generation**, famous for his **unsentimental depictions of the social and psychological disorientation that follows the imposition of Western customs and values upon traditional African society**. His first novel, **Things Fall Apart (1958)**, **often considered his masterpiece**, is **the most widely read book in modern African literature**. He **openly supported the secessionist Biafra movement** and **took a position as head of the Biafra Broadcasting Service at great risk to his life and career**. After the civil war he became active with the **left-leaning People's Redemption Party (PRP)**, and **in 1983 became the party's deputy national vice president**, continuing to **pull discourse towards Nigeria's corrupt polity**, notably in **The Trouble with Nigeria**, whose first page reads: **"The Nigerian problem is the unwillingness or inability of its leaders to rise to the responsibility and to the challenge of personal example, which are the hallmarks of true leadership."** **Wole Soyinka (1934–present)** is remembered for **achievements in literature and political activism**: **as a 33-year-old graduate in 1967 he was imprisoned by the Nigerian military government for 22 months for speaking out against the Nigerian civil war**, and **in 1994 he fled the country after the dictatorship in power charged him with treason for criticising the military junta**. **In both instances he continued to criticise the undemocratic governments of the day and call out the oppression of Nigerians**, and **since the return to civilian rule in 1999 he has continued to activate political discourse around the needs of the average Nigerian and the corruption of the polity**. Receiving the **Nobel Prize for Literature in 1986 — the first African to do so** — he used the acceptance speech to **focus the world's attention on the continuing injustice of white rule in South Africa** and **dedicated the award to the imprisoned Nelson Mandela**; he is **a symbol of Nigerian resilience and a true generational impact leader**. **Gani Fawehinmi (1938–2009)**, in full **Ganiyu "Gani" Oyesola Fawehinmi**, wanted only to be **a successful lawyer** until he **started practising in Lagos in 1965** and realised he **also wanted to affect the lives of everyday Nigerians and leave a legacy of impact**. **Often representing those who couldn't afford his services**, he became known as **"the people's lawyer"**, taking **a great number of pro bono cases**. He **played the role of human rights campaigner for 40 years, constantly challenging military rulers and defending their victims**; his **brushes with the authorities earned him frequent detentions and beatings, and on one occasion his books were seized and his library set on fire**. He **believed in media freedom, contributed avidly to newspapers, and often took on journalists' cases** — **the most notable being the still unsolved case of Dele Giwa, a magazine editor who died in a parcel bombing in 1986**. He **sought to re-open the Dele Giwa case and called on the federal attorney general to be removed for his indifference to corruption, all from his sick bed**, and **died in 2009 after a long battle with cancer, still as resilient as ever**.

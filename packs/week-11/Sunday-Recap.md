## Fallback topic 1 — MTH_102: Every radius in a volume of revolution is a DISTANCE FROM THE AXIS rather than a value of the function, and the integrand is the AREA built from those radii - so a washer subtracts squared radii, pi*(R^2 - r^2), instead of squaring a difference of radii, pi*(R - r)^2, and moving the axis to y = k shifts every radius by k without changing the formula at all.

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** Week 10 trained one reflex for a whole week: the integrand of an area is (top - bottom), a difference of the two boundary functions. Week 11 keeps the same region, the same limits and the same sketch, and changes only the integrand - and the reflex carries straight over, producing pi*(integral of (f - g)^2 dx) instead of pi*(integral of (f^2 - g^2) dx). The error is almost invisible because the working looks right: the limits are correct, the top-minus-bottom test was done, a pi has been attached, and the answer is a plausible positive multiple of pi. Nothing in the arithmetic complains. It survives self-checking because it is not an arithmetic slip but a wrong picture of the slice: the student believes the cross-section is a circle whose radius is the gap between the curves, when in fact it is an annulus, a big disc with a small disc removed. The identity R^2 - r^2 = (R - r)(R + r) makes the two look related, which reassures rather than corrects. The same wrong picture produces the second failure: because the radius is thought of as 'the function' rather than 'the distance from the axis', a rotation about y = -1 or x = 3 is set up with f(x) or x as the radius and the shift is simply dropped. The tell in both cases is that the wrong answer does not change when the region is moved further from the axis - which is impossible for a real solid.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why is the cross-section of the solid at position x an annulus rather than a circle, and what exactly is the circle that has been removed from it?
2. Why, then, is the area of that annulus pi*R^2 - pi*r^2 rather than pi*(R - r)^2 - what shape does pi*(R - r)^2 actually measure?
3. Why does the identity R^2 - r^2 = (R - r)(R + r) NOT rescue the wrong version, and under what single condition do the two expressions coincide?
4. Why must the radius be defined as the distance from the AXIS rather than as the value of f(x), and what does that force you to write when the axis is the line y = -1 instead of y = 0?
5. Why is 'the answer did not change when I moved the region further from the axis' a proof that the set-up is wrong, and what does the factor (R + r) tell you about how fast the true volume grows as the axis moves away?

**What a good answer contains:**

- It must say that revolving the region carries every point at a given x right around the axis, so the plane cut there is a full circle of radius R = (distance from the axis to the far boundary) with a concentric circle of radius r = (distance from the axis to the near boundary) removed, and that this annulus has area pi*R^2 - pi*r^2 = pi*(R^2 - r^2).
- It must state that pi*(R - r)^2 is the area of a circle whose radius is the WIDTH of the gap between the curves, a different shape entirely, and that R^2 - r^2 factors as (R - r)(R + r), not as (R - r)^2, the two agreeing only when r = 0, which is exactly the disc case where there is no hole.
- It must state that a radius is a distance from the axis, so about y = k it is |f(x) - k| and about x = h it is |x - h|, and that a set-up whose answer is unchanged when the region is moved further from the axis must be wrong, because R + r grows and every annulus gains area.

**Worked example.**

Find the volume generated by revolving the region between y = x and y = x^2 on [0, 1] about the x-axis.
Step 1. Sketch and fix the limits. The curves meet where x = x^2, so x = 0 and x = 1. Test x = 1/2: the line gives 1/2 and the parabola gives 1/4, so the LINE is above the parabola on the whole open interval.
Step 2. Identify the axis and the slice. The axis is y = 0 and it touches the region only at the two endpoints, so for every interior x there is a gap: the cut perpendicular to the axis is an ANNULUS, not a disc.
Step 3. Write the radii as distances from the axis. Distance from y = 0 to the far boundary y = x is R = x; distance to the near boundary y = x^2 is r = x^2. Both are non-negative on [0, 1], and R >= r there.
Step 4. Area of the slice. A(x) = pi*R^2 - pi*r^2 = pi*(x^2 - x^4). Square each radius FIRST, then subtract.
Step 5. Integrate. V = pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of (x^2 - x^4) dx) = pi*[x^3/3 - x^5/5] from 0 to 1 = pi*(1/3 - 1/5) = 2*pi/15, about 0.419.
Step 6. Check by shells about the same axis. Shells about a horizontal axis run in dy: radius y, height sqrt(y) - y, y from 0 to 1, giving 2*pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of y*(sqrt(y) - y) dy) = 2*pi*(2/5 - 1/3) = 2*pi/15. The two methods agree.
The wrong approach: the student carries Week 10's (top - bottom) into the integrand and writes V = pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of (x - x^2)^2 dx) = pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of (x^2 - 2x^3 + x^4) dx) = pi*(1/3 - 1/2 + 1/5) = pi/30, about 0.105 - four times too small, with no step that looks wrong. What that number actually measures is the volume of a solid whose cross-section at x is a small disc of radius (x - x^2), i.e. a circle whose radius is the GAP between the curves; no such solid is produced by revolving anything. The decisive test: translate both curves upward by 5, so the region is far from the axis and the true volume is enormous. The correct integrand pi*((x + 5)^2 - (x^2 + 5)^2) grows accordingly, while pi*(x - x^2)^2 is completely unchanged - the wrong set-up cannot see the axis at all.
The same wrong picture, applied to a moved axis: revolving that region about y = -1 needs R = x + 1 and r = x^2 + 1, giving pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of ((x + 1)^2 - (x^2 + 1)^2) dx) = pi*(integral from 0 to 1 of (2x - x^2 - x^4) dx) = 7*pi/15. A student who takes R = x and r = x^2 reports 2*pi/15 again, having computed the solid about the wrong line.

## Fallback topic 2 — COS_102: That cohesion and coupling are two independent readings taken in two different places - cohesion inside a single module against its one ultimate aim, coupling across the boundary between two modules against what one must know of the other - so that 'high cohesion, low coupling' is a pair of separate tests rather than one sliding scale, and neither can be improved by merging or splitting modules on its own

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** The phrase 'high cohesion, low coupling' is short and easy to recite, so it is learned as a slogan long before it is learned as two measurements. Because the two words always appear together and point in opposite directions, students infer that they are the two ends of one scale. That produces three errors. First, they believe the two trade off, so a gain in one must cost the other, and they stop looking once one improves. Second, they judge both by counting calls: heavy traffic between modules is read as high coupling, heavy traffic inside one as high cohesion. Neither is the definition. Coupling is the extent to which a module depends on another before it can perform its function, so one line that reads another module's field order is tighter coupling than two hundred calls through a published interface; cohesion is whether the functions serve the module's one ultimate aim, so two functions that never call each other are cohesive if both serve it. Third, and worst in practice, they treat the metric as the goal and improve it by moving boundaries. Merging two modules sends the coupling between them to zero because no boundary is left to measure across, while the dependency survives inside, ungoverned by any interface; repeat the move and you arrive at the five-thousand-line function the course opened by condemning. The error survives because the number moved as the slogan asked, and nothing in the vocabulary says it moved for the wrong reason.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why does the course state two separate properties, cohesion and coupling, rather than a single measure of how well a program is divided?
2. Why is cohesion measured inside one module while coupling is measured between modules, and what would go wrong if you tried to read either one in the other's place?
3. Why is a module that calls another two hundred times through published functions more loosely coupled than one that reads a single field of that module's internal table?
4. Why does merging two modules drive the coupling between them to zero without removing any dependency at all, and what has happened to cohesion at the same moment?
5. Why does splitting a low-cohesion component into four raise cohesion while splitting a high-cohesion component into four lowers it, when the operation performed is the same in both cases?

**What a good answer contains:**

- That cohesion and coupling are two different readings taken in two different places - cohesion inside one module, asking whether every function in it works towards the module's single ultimate aim, and coupling across a boundary, asking what one module must know about another before it can perform its function - which is why they are not two ends of one scale and why a design can score badly on both at once.
- That neither is measured by counting calls: one line that depends on another module's data structure or field order is tighter coupling than two hundred calls through a published interface, and two functions that never call each other are highly cohesive whenever both serve the module's aim, so the real test in each case is what would have to change and why.
- That moving a boundary changes the reading without changing the design - merging two modules sends their coupling to zero by deleting the boundary rather than the dependency, and costs cohesion because the result now carries two responsibilities - so the correct operation is to keep the boundary and narrow the interface, making the other module's data structures and processing details inaccessible, which is information hiding and is what actually produces low coupling.

**Worked example.**

Task: a module 'orders' calls a module 'stock' in fourteen places, and the team is told to reduce coupling.

Step 1 - look at what the fourteen calls actually depend on, not at their number. Twelve of them call published functions: stock.quantity(sku) and stock.reserve(sku, n). Two of them read stock.LEVELS[sku][2] directly, where field 2 happens to be the reorder threshold. The coupling of this pair of modules is set almost entirely by those two lines, because they are the only ones that depend on how 'stock' stores anything.

Step 2 - test each dependency by asking what would break it. If 'stock' were reorganised internally - LEVELS moved into a database, or a field inserted at position 2 - the twelve calls would be unaffected and the two direct reads would either fail loudly or, worse, keep running and return the wrong number. That asymmetry is the whole of the coupling reading.

Step 3 - repair by narrowing the interface, not by removing the boundary. Add stock.reorder_threshold(sku) to the published interface and rewrite the two offending lines to call it. Make LEVELS inaccessible to other modules. 'orders' now depends on three questions that 'stock' promises to answer, and on nothing about how 'stock' answers them.

Step 4 - check cohesion afterwards, because it is a separate reading. 'orders' still has one aim (placing and tracking orders) and 'stock' still has one aim (knowing what is held). Neither module gained a responsibility, so cohesion is unchanged and high. Both readings now point the right way, and they were checked separately.

Step 5 - state the test that will be used from now on: if 'stock' is reorganised internally, must 'orders' change? After the repair the answer is no, which is what low coupling means.

What the wrong approach does. The tempting move is to merge 'orders' and 'stock' into one module, 'sales', because a merged module cannot be coupled to itself. Measured coupling between them falls immediately to zero - and nothing has been fixed. The two direct reads of LEVELS still exist and still depend on field 2; they have simply moved to where no interface governs them, no review notices them, and nothing stops more of them appearing. Meanwhile 'sales' now has two responsibilities, so cohesion has fallen and the single responsibility principle is broken. Repeat the move on the next coupled pair and the next, and the endpoint is one module containing the whole program - precisely the five-thousand-line function the course opens by condemning, and a program with a perfect coupling score. The lesson is that a boundary is what makes a dependency visible and narrow; deleting it deletes the measurement, not the problem.

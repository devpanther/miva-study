# Monday — MTH_102 nightly check

*Volumes of solids of revolution as concepts: what the disc, washer and shell methods each are and where their integrands come from, what feature of the geometry forces one rather than another, why a washer subtracts areas and not radii, how the axis of revolution together with the direction of slicing fixes the variable of integration, and how rotating about y = k or x = h changes the radius without changing the formula.*
*Sit cold, notes closed, about 15 minutes. Score out of 12.*

**1.** The formula V = π∫ₐᵇ[f(x)]²dx is obtained by arguing that:
a) the region has area ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx and it is swept through 2π radians, so the volume is 2π times that area
b) a plane cut perpendicular to the x-axis meets the solid in a circle of radius f(x), whose area is π[f(x)]²; a slab of thickness dx therefore has volume π[f(x)]²dx
c) the area ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx is squared and multiplied by π, since a circle's area is π times a squared length
d) the strip at x has area f(x)dx and travels a distance 2πx as it revolves, so its swept volume is 2πx·f(x)dx

**2.** In exactly one of the following, the cross-sections perpendicular to the axis of revolution are **discs** rather than washers. Which?
a) the region between y = x and y = x² on [0, 1], revolved about the x-axis
b) the region under y = √x on [0, 4] (above the x-axis), revolved about the x-axis
c) the region bounded by y = x², y = 0 and x = 2, revolved about the y-axis
d) the region bounded by y = x², y = 0, x = 0 and x = 2, revolved about the line y = −1

**3.** The deck's first step says that identifying the axis of revolution "determines the variable of integration". Stated precisely, this is because:
a) the variable is the one the axis is named after — an x-axis rotation is integrated in x, a y-axis rotation in y
b) the slices must always be taken perpendicular to the axis, and the variable of integration measures position along the axis
c) the slice thickness lies in a direction fixed jointly by the axis and by whether the slices are perpendicular or parallel to it, and the variable of integration is the coordinate measured in that direction
d) the variable is whichever one the boundary curve has already been written in terms of

**4.** In the shell integrand 2πx·h(x)dx, the factor 2πx is there because:
a) 2πx is the area of the circle of radius x that the strip sweeps out
b) the shell of radius x, height h(x) and thickness dx can be slit and unrolled into a slab of dimensions 2πx by h(x) by dx, so 2πx is its length
c) the strip has two ends, each contributing a circle of circumference πx
d) a full revolution is 2π radians, so every volume of revolution carries a factor 2π

**5.** The region bounded by y = f(x) (with f(x) > 0 throughout), the x-axis, x = a and x = b is revolved about the line **y = −2**. The correct radii are:
a) R = f(x), r = 0
b) R = f(x) + 2, r = 2
c) R = f(x) − 2, r = −2
d) R = f(x) + 2, r = 0

**6.** A washer's cross-sectional area is π(R² − r²) and not π(R − r)² because:
a) π(R − r)² would be negative whenever r > R
b) R² − r² = (R − r)(R + r), so the two expressions are equal and either may be used
c) π(R − r)² is the area of a circle whose radius is the washer's *width*, which is a different region; the annulus is the outer disc with the inner disc removed, so its area is πR² − πr²
d) R and r are functions of x, and the square of a difference of two functions is not defined

**7.** A region lying above the x-axis between x = a and x = b, with 0 < a < b, is to be revolved about the **y-axis**. The correct pairing of method with variable is:
a) washers in dx, shells in dy
b) washers in dy, shells in dx
c) both in dx, since the region's boundaries are given as functions of x
d) both in dy, since the axis of revolution is the y-axis

**8.** The region bounded by y = 2x² − x³ and the x-axis (0 ≤ x ≤ 2) is revolved about the y-axis, and the shell method is the only practical route. The reason is:
a) a cubic cannot be integrated, so no method requiring ∫(2x² − x³)dx can be used
b) a washer setup about a vertical axis needs both boundaries written as x = (function of y), and y = 2x² − x³ cannot be solved for x
c) the region touches the y-axis at the origin, so the inner radius of a washer would be zero
d) whenever the axis of revolution is the y-axis, shells must be used

**9. (explain why)** Every method this week is a special case of V = ∫ₐᵇA(x)dx. State what A(x) is, what has to be true of the slices for the formula to hold, and show how the disc and the washer are both instances of it. Then explain why a cylindrical shell is *not* a cross-section of the solid, and why the shell method is nevertheless exact.

**10. (explain why)** A student computes the volume generated by revolving the region between y = x and y = x² on [0, 1] about the x-axis as π∫₀¹(x − x²)²dx. Identify the error precisely, evaluate both that integral and the correct one, and say what solid, if any, the student's number describes.

**11. (explain why)** Explain why the radius in every one of these formulas is a *distance from the axis*, and use that to write down the outer radius for a curve y = f(x) > 0 revolved about (i) the x-axis, (ii) the line y = k with k < 0, and (iii) the line x = h with the region entirely to the left of it. Then say what goes wrong if the region straddles the axis of revolution, and what you do instead.

**12. (explain why)** The region bounded by y = x², y = 0 and x = 2 can be revolved about the y-axis by washers or by shells. Explain what the slices look like in each case, which variable each forces, and exactly what work each method demands of you before the integral can even be written. Do not evaluate either integral.

## Answers

**1. b** — *A solid of revolution is integrated by slicing it perpendicular to its axis; the integrand is the area of one slice.* Every point of the region at abscissa x has been carried right round the axis, so the plane cut at x is a full circle of radius f(x); its area is π[f(x)]² and the slab of thickness dx contributes π[f(x)]²dx. (a) is the "swept through 2π radians" argument, which would give 2π∫f dx — dimensionally it is an area times an angle, not a volume, and it produces the wrong answer for every disc problem (for y = √x on [0, 4] it gives 32π/3 instead of 8π); (c) squares after integrating instead of before, and π[∫f dx]² ≠ π∫f²dx — for y = √x on [0, 4] it gives π(16/3)² = 256π/9; (d) is the **shell** integrand, correct for revolution about the *y*-axis and wrong here, because the strip at x is parallel to the x-axis's own direction and does not travel a distance 2πx about it. The confusion between (a), (d) and (b) is the whole of this week's method selection in miniature.

**2. b** — *Cross-sections are discs precisely when the axis of revolution is a boundary of the region, so no hole is punched.* Under y = √x the region reaches down to y = 0 at every x in (0, 4], so the cut at x is a solid circle of radius √x. (a) has two curves that meet only at the endpoints x = 0 and x = 1; strictly between them the region is bounded below by y = x² > 0, so every interior cut is a washer with hole radius x² — touching the axis at isolated points does not close the hole. (c) is the case that kills the popular rule "one curve means disc": there is one curve, but the axis is *vertical*, and the horizontal cut at height y runs from x = √y out to x = 2, a washer with inner radius √y. (d) has a gap of exactly 1 between the region's lowest edge (y = 0) and the axis y = −1, so every cut is a washer with inner radius 1. The criterion is never "how many curves" — it is whether the region touches the axis all along the interval.

**3. c** — *The variable of integration is the direction in which the slices are stacked, and that depends on the axis and on the slicing direction together.* Disc and washer slices are perpendicular to the axis, so they stack along it: about a horizontal axis, dx; about a vertical axis, dy. Shell slices are parallel to the axis, so they stack across it: about a vertical axis, dx; about a horizontal axis, dy. (a) is true for discs and washers only and is exactly backwards for shells — it is the reason students write 2π∫y·(…)dy for a rotation about the y-axis; (b) states the perpendicular rule as though it were universal, which quietly outlaws the shell method; (d) is the refusal to invert: how a curve happens to be written is a fact about the page, not about the solid, and a washer about the y-axis requires x = g(y) whether or not you were given it that way.

**4. b** — *A shell is a thin tube; unrolled, it is a rectangular slab whose length is the circumference at its radius.* Slitting the tube of radius x, height h and thickness dx and flattening it gives dimensions 2πx (circumference) × h (height) × dx (thickness), so dV = 2πx·h·dx. Exactly, the tube is π(x + dx)²h − πx²h = πh(2x·dx + dx²), and the dx² term is negligible in the limit — so the formula is not an approximation in the finished integral. (a) confuses circumference with area: the area of the circle of radius x is πx², and using it would give a solid cylinder, not a tube; (c) invents a geometry — the two ends of a shell are annuli of negligible area, and πx is not a circumference anyway; (d) is the same "2π radians" error as in question 1 — the 2π here is a length, the circumference, and that is why it is multiplied by a radius that varies from strip to strip rather than pulled out as a constant angle.

**5. b** — *The radius is the distance from the axis to the boundary, so it is (boundary coordinate) − (axis coordinate).* With the axis at y = −2, the far boundary y = f(x) is at distance f(x) − (−2) = f(x) + 2, and the near boundary y = 0 is at distance 0 − (−2) = 2. (a) ignores the shift entirely and computes the solid about y = 0, a completely different solid; (c) writes f(x) − 2 by subtracting the *magnitude* of k instead of k itself — the sign of k is the whole point, and it also produces the absurd negative inner radius −2; (d) is the half-correction that is much the most common: the outer radius is shifted correctly, but the student forgets that the shift has opened a cylindrical hole of radius 2 down the middle, and so adds a solid core that is not part of the solid.

**6. c** — *The washer is a difference of two discs, so its area is a difference of two areas.* Remove the disc of radius r from the disc of radius R: πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²). (a) is irrelevant — a square is never negative, and in a correctly set up washer R ≥ r anyway; (b) is the near-miss that catches the most people: R² − r² does factor, but as (R − r)(R + r), **not** as (R − r)². The two agree only when R + r = R − r, i.e. r = 0, which is precisely the disc case where there is no hole to argue about. (d) is fabricated: (f − g)² is perfectly well defined; it simply computes the area of a circle whose radius is the *gap*, which is not the shape the slice has.

**7. b** — *Perpendicular slices stack along the axis, parallel slices stack across it, so the two families take opposite variables.* About the y-axis, a washer is a horizontal annulus at height y whose radii are x-values, so the stacking direction is y and the integral is π∫(R(y)² − r(y)²)dy; a shell is a vertical tube at abscissa x, so the stacking direction is x and the integral is 2π∫x·h(x)dx. (a) is the exact swap, and it is what produces the notorious "2π∫y·(…)dy about the y-axis"; (c) lets the notation of the question dictate the variable and so refuses to invert the curve, which makes the washer integral unwritable; (d) applies the disc/washer rule to both families, which would forbid the shell method's single greatest advantage — that it needs no inversion.

**8. b** — *Discs and washers about a vertical axis need the boundaries as functions of y; shells about a vertical axis need them as functions of x.* The washer at height y would require the two x-values on the curve at that height, i.e. the roots of x³ − 2x² + y = 0 — a cubic in x with no usable closed form, and in fact two branches. Shells need only y = 2x² − x³ as given, with radius x and height 2x² − x³. (a) is false and confuses two different operations: ∫(2x² − x³)dx is elementary; what cannot be done is *inverting* the relation; (c) inverts the logic — an inner radius of zero is not an obstacle, it is exactly the condition that makes a washer degenerate into a disc, which is easier, not harder; (d) is a blanket rule that is simply untrue — worked from y-boundaries, x = √y about the y-axis is a routine disc problem.

**9.** *Concept: all three methods are the single formula V = ∫A(x)dx applied to different decompositions; the disc and washer supply cross-sectional areas, while the shell supplies a nested-tube decomposition that is exact in the limit.* If a solid lies between the planes x = a and x = b, and A(x) denotes the **area of the plane cross-section at x, taken perpendicular to the x-axis**, then a slab between x and x + dx has volume approximately A(x)dx, and summing and taking the limit gives **V = ∫ₐᵇA(x)dx**. The requirement is only that A(x) be a genuine cross-sectional area and that it vary continuously, so that the slab's volume is A(x)dx to first order. For a solid of revolution about the x-axis every cross-section is a circle or an annulus, because the region has been carried right round the axis: if the region reaches the axis, A(x) = π[f(x)]², which is the **disc** method; if it does not, the same cut has a concentric hole of radius r(x), so A(x) = π([R(x)]² − [r(x)]²), which is the **washer** method. The two are one formula with r = 0 or r ≠ 0. A cylindrical shell is different in kind: it is not a plane cut of the solid at all, but one of a family of **nested tubes** filling the solid from the axis outwards, so its "thickness" dx is measured radially, across the axis rather than along it. It is still exact, because the tube of radius x, height h(x) and thickness dx has volume π(x + dx)²h − πx²h = 2πx·h·dx + πh·dx², and the dx² term contributes nothing in the limit; the sum of the tubes is therefore ∫2πx h(x)dx. Both decompositions fill the same solid without overlap, so both must give the same number — which is why one is the best available check on the other.

**10.** *Concept: a washer's integrand is a difference of areas, π(R² − r²), and (R − r)² is not R² − r².* The error is squaring the *difference of the radii* instead of differencing the *squares*. The strip at x runs from y = x² up to y = x, and when it is revolved about the x-axis it sweeps an annulus whose outer radius is R = x (distance from the axis to the upper curve) and whose inner radius is r = x² — so its area is π(x² − x⁴), not π(x − x²)².
Correct: V = π∫₀¹(x² − x⁴)dx = π[x³/3 − x⁵/5]₀¹ = π(1/3 − 1/5) = **2π/15 ≈ 0.419.**
The student's: π∫₀¹(x − x²)²dx = π∫₀¹(x² − 2x³ + x⁴)dx = π(1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5) = π(10 − 15 + 6)/30 = **π/30 ≈ 0.105.**
The two are not related by any constant, and the student's is far too small — as it must be, since (R − r)² < R² − r² whenever 0 < r < R. Geometrically, π(x − x²)² is the area of a **circle whose radius is the width of the gap between the curves**, so the student's number is the volume of the solid obtained by revolving nothing at all: it is the volume of a solid whose cross-section at x is a small disc of radius x − x², a shape that has no relation to the annulus actually produced. The tell is that the answer does not depend on how far the region sits from the axis: shift both curves up by 5 and π(R − r)² is unchanged, while the true volume grows enormously.

**11.** *Concept: a radius is a distance from the axis of revolution, so it is always (coordinate of the boundary) − (coordinate of the axis), taken so as to be non-negative.* When a point is revolved about a line, the circle it traces has radius equal to its perpendicular distance from that line — this is the only geometric fact behind every formula this week, and it is why the formulas themselves never change when the axis moves. Hence: **(i)** about the x-axis (y = 0), R = f(x) − 0 = **f(x)**; **(ii)** about y = k with k < 0, R = f(x) − k = **f(x) + |k|**, and the region no longer touches the axis, so an inner radius r = 0 − k = |k| appears and the problem becomes a washer; **(iii)** about the vertical line x = h with the region entirely to the left, every point of the region has x ≤ h, so the distance is **h − x**, and writing x − h instead makes every radius negative and the final volume negative. If the region **straddles** the axis — part of it on each side — the two parts sweep into the *same* space, and adding their contributions counts the overlap twice. The correct treatment is to revolve only the part that is further from the axis on each cut, i.e. to take R as the largest distance and r as the smallest distance *achieved on that side*; in the common symmetric case (a region symmetric about the axis, such as −1 ≤ y ≤ 1 revolved about y = 0) the solid generated is exactly the one generated by either half, so you integrate one half only and do **not** double it. Reporting twice the volume because "there are two halves" is the standard casualty here.

**12.** *Concept: the two families slice the same solid in perpendicular directions, so they force opposite variables and impose opposite demands on how the boundary curves must be written.* The region is bounded above by y = x², below by y = 0, and on the right by x = 2; the axis is the y-axis. **Washers.** The slices are perpendicular to the axis, so they are horizontal annuli at height y, and they stack in the y-direction: the variable is **dy** and the limits are y-values, from y = 0 to y = 4 (the height of the corner (2, 4)). At height y the solid runs from the parabola out to the line x = 2, so the inner radius is the parabola expressed as **x = √y** and the outer radius is the constant 2, giving π∫₀⁴(2² − (√y)²)dy. The work demanded *before* integrating is therefore **inverting the curve** (y = x² ⟹ x = √y), converting the corner point into a y-limit, and recognising that the parabola supplies the *inner* radius, since it lies nearer the axis. **Shells.** The slices are parallel to the axis, so they are vertical tubes at abscissa x, and they stack in the x-direction: the variable is **dx** and the limits are x-values, from x = 0 to x = 2. The tube at x has radius x (its distance from the y-axis) and height (top − bottom) = x² − 0, giving 2π∫₀²x·x²dx. Here nothing needs inverting — the curve is already y = f(x) — but you must identify the radius as the distance x rather than the function value, and the height as a Week 10 "top minus bottom", not simply as the upper curve. The two integrals look nothing alike and are in different variables; both are exact, and their agreement is the check.

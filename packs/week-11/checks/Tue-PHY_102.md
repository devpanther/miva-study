# Tuesday — PHY_102 nightly check

*Faraday's and Lenz's laws as concepts: what Faraday's law actually asserts and why it is the rate of change that matters, what the 1831 magnet-and-ammeter experiment proves line by line, what the minus sign is doing and what sign convention gives it meaning, Lenz's law as conservation of energy rather than an extra rule, determining the direction of the induced current in named geometries, and motional emf as the magnetic force on the carriers.*
*Sit cold, notes closed, about 15 minutes. Score out of 12.*

**1.** In Faraday's experiment the ammeter deflects when the **magnet** is moved towards the loop, and deflects in exactly the same way when the **loop** is moved towards the magnet. This shows that:
a) only the **relative** motion matters, because either version changes the flux through the loop at the same rate
b) the magnet drags its field along with it, so a moving magnet and a moving loop are physically the same event
c) two different laws are at work in the two cases, and it is a coincidence that they give the same reading
d) the two deflections agree only if the two speeds are equal, which the experimenter must arrange

**2.** You take the positive normal of a loop to point **out of** the page, then change your mind and take it **into** the page instead. As a result:
a) the sign of Φ reverses, the positive sense of circulation reverses with it, and the **physical direction** of the induced current is unchanged
b) the sign of Φ reverses, so the induced current now flows the opposite way round the loop
c) nothing at all changes, since Φ = BA cos θ is a magnitude and has no sign
d) Faraday's law acquires a plus sign, since the minus sign is there only for the standard choice of normal

**3.** The external field threading a loop is getting **weaker**. The magnetic field produced by the induced current, inside the loop, points:
a) opposite to the external field, since an induced field always opposes the field that produces it
b) in the **same** direction as the external field, replacing the flux that is being lost
c) nowhere — a weakening field cannot drive a current, only a strengthening one can
d) in the same direction at first, then reverses once the external field has fallen to zero

**4.** A horizontal circular loop lies on a bench. A bar magnet is held above it with its **south pole pointing down** at the loop, and is then **lifted away**. Seen from above, the induced current in the loop flows:
a) clockwise, since the induced field must always oppose the magnet's field
b) anticlockwise, so that the loop's upper face becomes a north pole and attracts the departing south pole
c) clockwise, because a south pole always induces a clockwise current when viewed from above
d) not at all, since the magnet is receding and so cannot increase the flux through the loop

**5.** A magnet dropped down a long vertical copper pipe settles to a constant terminal speed **v**. While it descends at that speed, the rate at which heat is produced in the pipe is:
a) zero, since the magnet is no longer accelerating and so does no net work
b) ½mv², the kinetic energy the magnet carries
c) **mgv**, the rate at which the magnet loses gravitational potential energy
d) not determinable from these data, since it depends on the resistivity of the copper

**6.** A magnetic flux changes with time through a region of **empty space**, with no conductor anywhere near it. In that region there is:
a) nothing, since an emf can exist only where there are charges for it to act on
b) a circulating **electric** field, whose line integral round any closed path equals −dΦ/dt whether or not a wire occupies the path
c) a magnetic force but no electric field, magnetic effects being unable to produce electric ones
d) an emf that springs into existence only at the moment a conductor is introduced

**7.** A straight metal rod is moved at right angles to a uniform magnetic field, with **no circuit** connected to it. Between its ends there is:
a) nothing at all, since an emf requires a closed circuit
b) a steadily **growing** potential difference, since charge keeps accumulating at the ends for as long as the motion lasts
c) a steady potential difference **BLv**, charge having separated until the electrostatic field it sets up balances the magnetic force qv × B
d) a steady **current**, since the free electrons are continuously pushed along the rod by the field

**8.** Two geometrically identical single-turn loops, one of copper and one of much higher-resistance nichrome, are exposed to the **same** changing flux. Compared with the copper loop, the nichrome loop has:
a) a smaller induced emf, because emf is the product of current and resistance
b) the same induced emf, a smaller induced current, and a smaller force opposing the change
c) the same induced current, since Faraday's law fixes the current directly
d) a larger induced emf, since a larger resistance means a larger opposition to the change

## Answers

**1. a** — *Faraday's law depends on dΦ/dt, and only relative motion changes it.* The loop has no way of telling which body is moving; all it responds to is the rate at which the flux threading it changes, and moving the magnet towards the loop or the loop towards the magnet at the same relative speed produces the same dΦ/dt and hence the same emf. This is the deck's fourth observation and it is what makes ε = −dΦ/dt a *rule about the circuit* rather than a rule about magnets. (b) invents a mechanism — a field is not a substance carried along, and no "dragging" is needed to explain anything here. (c) is the subtlest wrong answer and worth understanding: it is true that the **microscopic mechanisms differ** (a moving rod has its carriers pushed by qv × B; a stationary loop in a changing field has them pushed by an induced electric field), but the agreement is **not a coincidence** — the flux rule ε = −dΦ/dt covers both cases as one law, and the fact that it must, for consistency between observers, is precisely the observation Einstein opened special relativity with. (d) confuses a controlled comparison with a physical requirement: the deflections have the same *character* in both versions regardless of speed, and the speeds only need to match if you want the same numerical reading.

**2. a** — *The sign convention is a bookkeeping choice; Lenz's law is not.* The positive normal fixes both the sign of Φ and, through the right hand, which way round the loop counts as the positive circulation. Reverse the normal and both flip together, so ε = −dΦ/dt returns a current in the same physical place on the bench. (b) flips one half of the convention and not the other — the classic error of a student who "gets a negative answer" and reverses the current on that basis alone, without noticing that the definition of "positive circulation" reversed at the same moment. (c) is wrong on the definition: Φ = **B·A** is a **signed** scalar, positive when B has a component along the chosen normal and negative when it opposes it; the sign is what makes a coil flipped through 180° register ΔΦ = 2BA rather than zero. (d) misplaces the minus sign in the convention: it encodes Lenz's law, a physical fact about direction, and does not depend on which normal you picked.

**3. b** — *Lenz's law opposes the **change**, not the field.* Flux is being lost, so the induced current acts to **replace** it: its own field inside the loop points the **same** way as the external field. The deck states this in exactly these words — "if the external field gets weaker, the induced current tries to replace the missing external field". (a) is the single most common Lenz error; it is correct only when the flux is **increasing**, i.e. half the time, and it is wrong in this half. (c) confuses a decrease with an absence: dΦ/dt is non-zero whether the flux is rising or falling, and a falling flux drives just as real a current, merely in the opposite sense. (d) invents a reversal: once the external field has settled at zero, dΦ/dt = 0 and the induced current stops — it does not reverse, because Lenz's law resists a change, it does not overshoot and undo it.

**4. b** — *The pole form of Lenz's law: a receding pole is attracted.* Field lines enter a south pole, so just below the downward-facing S pole the field points **upward**, and the flux through the loop is upward. Lifting the magnet **reduces** that upward flux, so the induced current must **maintain** it: its field inside the loop points up, which by the right-hand loop rule (thumb up, fingers curl) is **anticlockwise seen from above**. The loop's upper face is then a north pole, which **attracts** the departing south pole — resisting the withdrawal, exactly as Lenz requires. (a) is "oppose the field" instead of "oppose the change"; it would have the loop **helping** the magnet leave, which is the perpetual-motion direction. (c) attaches the answer to the pole's name alone, which would make the deflection identical whether the magnet approached or receded — precisely the observation Faraday's experiment refutes. (d) confuses "no increase" with "no change": a decrease is a change, and a decreasing flux induces just as large an emf as an increase at the same rate.

**5. c** — *Lenz's law is energy conservation, and terminal speed makes the accounting exact.* At terminal speed the magnet's kinetic energy is constant, so every joule of gravitational potential energy it loses must be appearing somewhere else; the only sink in the system is I²R heating by the eddy currents in the pipe wall. The rate of loss of potential energy is mgv, so the heating rate is **mgv**. (a) confuses zero **acceleration** with zero **energy transfer** — the magnet is still descending, still losing height, and the magnetic force is doing negative work on it at exactly the rate mgv. (b) quotes an **energy** in joules where a **power** in watts was asked for; the units alone condemn it. (d) is the most respectable wrong answer: the resistivity does determine what terminal speed is reached, but **once v is given** the energy balance fixes the heating rate completely, with no material property needed. That is the mark of an argument from conservation rather than from mechanism.

**6. b** — *Faraday's law in its general form, ∮E·dl = −dΦ/dt, makes no mention of wires.* A changing magnetic flux creates a **circulating electric field** in the space around it; a conducting loop placed there merely provides charges for that field to push, which is what we then measure as an emf. The field is there whether or not the loop is. This is also why a ring encircling a solenoid has an emf even though B ≈ 0 at the wire itself. (a) inverts cause and detector: the charges reveal the field, they do not create it — by the same logic a gravitational field would not exist until a mass was dropped into it. (c) asserts an independence between the two fields that Faraday's law exists to deny; a changing **B** produces an **E**, and (from Maxwell's other equation) a changing **E** produces a **B**, which is why light exists. (d) makes the physics depend on the experimenter's decision to introduce a wire, and gives no account of how the field would "know" to appear.

**7. c** — *Motional emf comes from the force qv × B on the carriers, and needs no circuit.* Each free charge, carried along with the rod, feels a magnetic force along the rod's length; charge therefore separates towards the ends until the **electrostatic** field it builds up exactly cancels that magnetic push. Equilibrium is reached almost instantly, and the resulting potential difference across the length L is **BLv**, steady for as long as the motion continues. The rod is a **source of emf** — a battery of internal emf BLv — with the v × B end positive. (a) confuses emf with current: Faraday's law contains no resistance and says nothing about closed paths; a cut coil moved past a magnet has exactly the same emf as an intact one. (b) forgets the equilibrium: accumulation stops the moment the electrostatic field balances the magnetic force, which is why the potential difference is constant rather than growing without limit. (d) mistakes the initial transient for a steady state — there is no return path, so a steady current is impossible, and the momentary redistribution of charge lasts a vanishingly short time.

**8. b** — *Faraday's law gives the emf; Ohm's law then gives the current.* The emf depends only on N and dΦ/dt, both identical for the two loops, so **both have the same emf**. The nichrome loop's larger resistance gives it a **smaller current** by I = ε/R, hence a smaller induced magnetic moment and a **smaller opposing force** — which is exactly why an aluminium ring jumps off a solenoid and a plastic one does not, and why slotting a copper pipe (raising the resistance of the current path to effectively infinite) removes the magnetic braking. (a) inverts the causal chain: ε is not defined as IR; IR is what the emf *drives*, and here it is the current that adjusts, not the emf. (c) makes Faraday's law give a current, which would leave R with no role at all and predict the same braking from copper and from a poor conductor. (d) confuses electrical resistance with mechanical opposition — a bigger R means **less** induced current and therefore **less** opposition to the change, which is the opposite of what it claims.

**9.** *Concept: the exact statement of Faraday's law, and why the rate of change — not the flux — is the cause.* **Statement.** *The emf induced in a circuit equals minus the rate of change of the magnetic flux linking that circuit*: **ε = −N dΦ/dt**, or **ε = −N ΔΦ/Δt** for a uniform rate. **ε** is the induced **emf** in **volts** — energy given to unit charge by a non-electrostatic agency; **N** is the number of turns linked (a pure number, present because the turns are in series and their emfs add); **Φ = B·A = BA cos θ** is the **flux** through one turn, in **webers** (1 Wb = 1 T·m² = 1 V·s), with **B** in **teslas**, **A** in **m²** and **θ** the angle between B and the **normal** to the surface; **t** is in **seconds**. Since 1 Wb/s = 1 V, the two sides balance dimensionally. **Why the rate of change.** The quantity on the right is dΦ/dt, so a circuit responds to how fast the flux threading it is changing and to nothing else — not to B, not to Φ, not to the presence or absence of motion. A coil at rest in the strongest steady field obtainable produces exactly zero volts; a coil in a weak but rapidly changing field can produce a large emf. **Why Faraday's sentence is the pivot.** Every wrong answer in this topic comes from substituting a *state* where a *rate* is required. Once "the flux must be changing" is fixed in mind, the correct question at the start of every problem becomes: **which of B, A or θ is changing, and how fast?** — and the three ways of changing Φ = BA cos θ organise the whole subject. **The four observations, and what each proves.** **(i) Magnet moved towards the loop — deflection.** A changing flux induces a current, and it does so with **no battery anywhere in the circuit**: an emf can be produced by a field alone. **(ii) Magnet moved away — deflection the opposite way.** The **sign** of dΦ/dt determines the direction of the current; the direction is therefore fixed by the *change*, not by the direction of B, which was the same in both cases. This observation alone forces something like Lenz's law. **(iii) Magnet held stationary inside the loop — no deflection.** The decisive one: the flux is at its **largest** in the whole experiment, and the emf is zero. Flux does not cause emf; changing flux does. **(iv) Loop moved instead of the magnet — the same deflection.** Only the **relative** motion enters, because only it changes the flux; the loop cannot detect which body moved. Faraday's law is a statement about the circuit, not about magnets.

**10.** *Concept: the sign convention, and Lenz's law as the first law of thermodynamics applied to induction.* **What the minus sign does.** It is **Lenz's law written into Faraday's law**. It is a statement about **direction** — the sense in which the induced emf drives current — and it never means "the emf is a negative number of volts". **The convention.** Choose a positive **normal n̂** for the loop's surface. Φ then counts as **positive** when **B** has a component along **n̂** and negative when it opposes it. The positive sense of **circulation** round the loop is fixed by the **right hand**: curl the fingers the positive way round the loop and the thumb must point along **n̂**. With that pairing, ε = −dΦ/dt is the emf driving current in the **positive circulation sense**; a negative result simply means the current runs the other way round. **If the opposite normal is chosen**, the sign of Φ reverses **and** the positive circulation sense reverses with it, so the two changes cancel exactly and the **physical direction of the current is unaltered**. The convention is bookkeeping; **Lenz's law is a physical fact that survives any choice of bookkeeping.** **The energy argument.** Push a magnet north-pole-first towards a coil closed through a resistor. The induced current dissipates **I²R** as heat. That energy has exactly one source: **the work you do pushing the magnet against the repulsion** of the induced north pole the coil presents. Now suppose the current ran the other way. The coil's near face would be a **south** pole, so the magnet would be **pulled in**; it would accelerate, raising dΦ/dt, raising the current, raising the attraction, accelerating it further. The magnet would gain unbounded kinetic energy **while** the resistor gained unbounded heat, with **nothing supplying either**. That is a perpetual-motion machine of the first kind. So the induced current cannot run that way, and Lenz's law is not an extra empirical rule bolted on to Faraday's law — it is **conservation of energy**, which is why the minus sign could have been predicted before it was measured. **The dynamo.** A bicycle dynamo with its lamp switched **off** delivers no current, so there is no induced field, no retarding torque, and the wheel spins nearly freely. Switch the lamp **on** and current flows; the induced magnetic moment now opposes the rotation, and the rider immediately feels the wheel become **harder to turn**. The extra pedalling effort is precisely the electrical power lighting the lamp — mechanical work in, electrical energy out, with the retarding force as the channel between them.

**11.** *Concept: the four-step direction algorithm, and the difference between opposing the change and opposing the field.* **The procedure.** **(1)** Establish the direction of the external **B** through the loop and whether the flux is **rising or falling**. **(2)** Decide the direction the **induced field inside the loop** must take: **against** B if the flux is rising, **along** B if it is falling, and **none at all** if the flux is constant. **(3)** Convert that field into a current sense with the **right hand** — *loop version*: point the **thumb** along the required induced field **inside** the loop, and the **fingers curl** the way the current flows. (*Straight-wire version*, which the deck uses: grip one side of the loop so that the **fingers**, where they pass through the interior, point along the required induced field; the **thumb** then gives the current in that side. Both are correct; mixing them is not.) **(4) Check mechanically** that the resulting force or pole opposes whatever is being done. **(i) Field out of the page, decreasing to zero.** Flux out of the page is being **lost**, so the induced field inside the loop must point **out of** the page to replace it. Right thumb out of the page, fingers curl **anticlockwise** — so the induced current is **anticlockwise**, exactly as the deck's figure shows. **(ii) No field, rising to a field out of the page.** Flux out of the page is being **gained**, so the induced field inside the loop must point **into** the page to oppose the increase. Right thumb into the page, fingers curl **clockwise** — the induced current is **clockwise**, again as the deck shows. **(iii) A north pole pushed into a coil.** The flux through the coil is **rising**, so the induced current opposes the increase: the coil's near face becomes a **north** pole and **repels** the magnet. Looking at the coil from the magnet's side, the current must run in the sense whose right-hand thumb points **back towards you** (out of the coil's near face), i.e. **anticlockwise as seen from the magnet**. The mechanical check passes: you must push against a repulsion, and the work you do is the heat produced. **Why "always opposes the applied field" is wrong.** Case **(i)** demonstrates it: there the applied field is out of the page and the induced field is **also out of the page**. What is opposed is the **change** — the loss of flux — and opposing a loss means **supplying more of the same**, not the opposite. The wrong rule agrees with the right one only when the flux happens to be increasing, so it is wrong in half of all problems, and it fails in the very first case the deck illustrates.

**12.** *Concept: motional emf from two independent routes, and why they must agree.* **Route 1 — Faraday, from the swept area.** A rod of length L slides at speed v on rails, with **B** perpendicular to the plane of the circuit. The enclosed area is A = Lx, so Φ = BLx and **|ε| = dΦ/dt = BL(dx/dt) = BLv**. **Route 2 — the force on the carriers.** Each free charge inside the rod is carried along at v and therefore feels **F = qv × B**, directed **along the rod**. Charge separates towards the ends until the **electrostatic** field E it creates exactly balances the magnetic push: qE = qvB, so E = vB, and the potential difference over the length L is **ε = EL = BLv**. **Why they must agree.** They are two descriptions of the same experiment, and if they disagreed you could build a circuit whose energy books did not balance; more deeply, the flux rule ε = −dΦ/dt is the single law that covers both the "conductor moves" and the "field changes" cases, and its consistency across the two is what makes it a law rather than a recipe. **Which end is positive.** The **v × B end**. Evaluate the cross product for the actual geometry — never guess — and remember that the *conventional* positive charge is pushed towards that end, so it becomes the **+ terminal**; the electrons pile up at the other. **Why the rod is a source with or without a circuit.** An emf is energy per unit charge supplied by a **non-electrostatic** agency, here the magnetic force; it exists in the rod whether or not there is anywhere for charge to go. Disconnected, the rod behaves as a **battery on open circuit**: full emf BLv across its ends, **zero** current. Complete the circuit through resistance R and the same emf drives **I = BLv/R**, which then feels **F = BIL = B²L²v/R** against the motion — the retarding force that makes the mechanical power Fv equal to the electrical power I²R exactly. **Rotating rod.** A rod pivoted at one end and turning at angular speed ω in a field B parallel to the rotation axis has **ε = ½BL²ω**. The factor ½ appears because the speed is **not uniform along the rod**: it is zero at the pivot and Lω at the tip, so integrating the element contributions B(ωr)dr from 0 to L gives ½BL²ω — equivalently BLv̄ with **v̄ = ½Lω**, the mean speed. Using the tip speed gives exactly twice the correct answer. **Two cases with no emf.** **(i)** The rod moves **along its own length**: it then sweeps no area, and equivalently v × B is perpendicular to the rod so no charge is driven from end to end. **(ii)** The rod moves **parallel to B**: v × B = 0, and the flux swept is zero. (A third, worth knowing: a closed loop translating entirely **inside** a large uniform field, where B, A and θ are all constant, so dΦ/dt = 0 despite the motion.)

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** State the lesson's distinction between hearing and listening, then name all six of the deck's headings under "Importance of Listening" and give one line on each.

**14.** Name six of the listening strategies the week's summary sheet lists, and explain the difference between paraphrasing and summarising, and between a clarifying question and a leading question.

**15.** A course rep sits in a meeting where two students are in open disagreement about a group project. Using the deck's own points under **Conflict Resolution** and **Avoiding Miscommunication**, explain what effective listening should achieve here, and name three practical strategies from the case study she could apply.

## Answers

**1. b** — *hearing vs listening*: the summary sheet's exact wording is "Hearing is passive and involuntary." (a) and (c) are the definition of **listening**, the other half of the contrast being tested; (d) names two listening strategies, neither of which describes hearing at all — hearing requires no attitude of any kind.

**2. c** — *definition of listening*: "an active and voluntary process requiring a conscious effort to **interpret and understand spoken messages**." (a) makes replying the goal, which is the classic error the whole topic exists to correct; (b) is rote recall, not interpretation; (d) is hearing plus transcription, with the understanding step removed.

**3. c** — *Leadership Effectiveness*: the deck's own frame is a leader "actively listening to the concerns and ideas of team members" in order to "make informed decisions, boost morale, and create an inclusive and collaborative work environment." (a) needs a dispute between parties, and there is none — one person is raising a complaint; (b) would be the answer if the emphasis were on trust and rapport rather than a decision; (d) would need the listener to be acquiring knowledge or skill.

**4. d** — *Effective Communication*: the deck lists exactly three — respond appropriately, ask relevant questions, provide thoughtful feedback. Written summarising is a real strategy from the **summary sheet's** list, which is why it is tempting; it is not part of this trio. The question tests whether you learned the deck's three as a closed set.

**5. b** — *Conflict Resolution*: "express their concerns, feelings, and viewpoints **without interruption**", which is what "facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of the issues at hand". (a), (c) and (d) are all plausible conflict-management measures but appear nowhere in the lesson; only the non-interruption condition is stated.

**6. c** — *the six headings*: Effective Communication, Building Relationships, Conflict Resolution, Learning and Growth, Leadership Effectiveness, Avoiding Miscommunication. Cultural Awareness is genuine lesson content, but it belongs to the **summary sheet's case-study methodology**, not to the deck's list of six — the distractor tests whether you keep the two strands apart.

**7. a** — *learning objectives*: the deck says to enhance active listening "by practicing techniques such as **paraphrasing, summarising, and asking clarifying questions**." (b) are engagement behaviours from the case study's active-listening bullet, not the objectives; (c) describes critical listening, which the objectives do not name; (d) are practical strategies from the case study — real content, wrong list.

**8. b** — *paraphrasing vs summarising*: paraphrasing restates **one point in your own words at comparable length** to confirm accuracy. (a) would compress the whole into main points; (c) would put a question to the speaker rather than a restatement; (d) records the point without testing understanding of it.

**9. c** — *Cultural Awareness*: the sheet's wording is "recognise and respect communication differences across cultures", and it calls this a demonstration of cultural competence. (a) is self-assessment after the fact; (b) is understanding the speaker's emotions; (d) is reading body language and facial expressions. All four are case-study headings — the question tests which heading owns this description.

**10. c** — *critical (evaluative) listening*: listening to judge soundness, weigh evidence and detect bias. (a) listens for enjoyment; (b) listens to absorb and retain content, which is the lecture mode; (d) attends to sounds and tone rather than meaning. The lesson names critical listening among the skills acquired.

**11. b** — *listening-practice passage*: "two particles become interconnected, influencing each other's properties **regardless of distance**" — precisely the feature that defies classical notions of independent behaviour. (a), (c) and (d) all reintroduce a physical limit that the passage's definition explicitly removes; (d) in particular inverts the point.

**12. c** — *Reflective Practice*: the sheet defines it as "regular self-assessment to identify areas for improvement in listening abilities." (a) is reflective *listening*, a different thing the sheet does not list; (b) is note-taking; (d) is avoiding prejudgment. Each distractor is a real strategy, so the error tested is confusing reflective practice with the similarly named or similarly placed items.

**13.** *Hearing vs listening, and the six headings*: **Hearing is passive and involuntary; listening is an active and voluntary process requiring a conscious effort to interpret and understand spoken messages** — the deck puts it as "not just hearing words but actively paying attention to and understanding the message being conveyed." **Effective Communication** — a fundamental component of communicating well; good listeners respond appropriately, ask relevant questions and give thoughtful feedback, producing clearer and more meaningful conversations. **Building Relationships** — active listening fosters deeper connections; when people feel heard and understood, trust and rapport strengthen, and it demonstrates empathy and respect for others' perspectives. **Conflict Resolution** — parties express concerns, feelings and viewpoints without interruption, so the issues are comprehensively understood, common ground is identified and misunderstandings are prevented. **Learning and Growth** — attentive listening lets individuals absorb information, gain insights and acquire new knowledge and skills, in education and professional development alike, and is especially valuable in collaborative environments where diverse perspectives drive innovation and problem-solving. **Leadership Effectiveness** — leaders who listen to team members' concerns and ideas make informed decisions, boost morale and build an inclusive, collaborative workplace; they are seen as approachable and inspire trust and loyalty. **Avoiding Miscommunication** — poor listening causes misunderstandings, misinterpretations and conflicts with serious personal and professional consequences; careful listening ensures the intended message is accurately received.

**14.** *Strategies and two contrasts*: any six of — **active listening** (full attention, eye contact, nodding, verbal cues), **nonverbal communication** (attending to body language and facial expressions), **empathetic listening** (understanding the speaker's emotions and expressing empathy), **paraphrasing**, **summarizing**, **asking clarifying questions**, **minimizing distractions**, **avoiding prejudgment**, **note-taking**, **cultural awareness**, **reflective practice**. *Paraphrasing vs summarising*: paraphrasing restates a single point in your own words at roughly the same length, to verify that you have understood it correctly; summarising compresses a whole stretch of talk into its main points, to consolidate and retain it. Both are named techniques and they are not interchangeable — one checks accuracy, the other condenses. *Clarifying vs leading question*: a clarifying question asks the speaker to supply information you missed or to make a vague term precise ("when you say 'soon', do you mean this week?") and keeps the speaker's meaning intact; a leading question embeds the answer you expect and pushes the speaker towards it, which substitutes your interpretation for theirs and is the opposite of listening.

**15.** *Applying Conflict Resolution and Avoiding Miscommunication*: under **Conflict Resolution**, effective listening should first let each student **express her concerns, feelings and viewpoints without interruption**, so that the rep gains a **comprehensive understanding of the issues at hand** rather than of one side only; from there the aim is to **identify common ground**, **find solutions**, and **prevent the misunderstandings that often arise from poor communication**. Under **Avoiding Miscommunication**, the risk is that the dispute is not really about the project at all but about a message that was misheard or misinterpreted — so the rep listens carefully to check that **the intended message was accurately received** on both sides, remembering that miscommunication carries serious consequences personally and professionally. *Three practical strategies from the case study* (any three): **paraphrasing** each student's position back to her so she can confirm or correct it; **asking clarifying questions** where a claim is vague; **avoiding prejudgment**, i.e. withholding a verdict until both have spoken; **minimizing distractions** so full attention is available; **summarizing** the two positions to expose where they actually agree; **empathetic listening**, acknowledging the frustration each feels; **note-taking**, so no point raised is lost.

# MTH_102 Week 11 — what the slide images contained that the extracted text had lost

Deck: `Definite-Integrals-Volume-of-Solids-of-Revolution-PDF`, **19 pages**, course branded "Elementary Math (calculus)". **The deck does not name its lecturer anywhere** — there is no presenter line on the title slide and none in the text extraction. The course's other decks (e.g. Week 7) name **Danjuma Rose Uwanassara, MSc Mathematics**, and this deck is from the same course and template, so she is the presumptive author, but it is not stated on these slides and should not be asserted as if it were.

**Pages 1–4 and 18–19 extracted as text**: title, LEARNING OBJECTIVES, INTRODUCTION, the four-step VOLUMES BY INTEGRATION procedure, Further Reading, Thank You. **Pages 5–17 are images.** `pdftotext` recovered only their headings, in this order:

> DISC METHOD · DISC METHOD · DISC METHOD · TASK · WASHER METHOD · **DISC METHOD** · WASHER METHOD · TASK · SHELL METHOD · SHELL METHOD · SHELL METHOD · TASK · Summary

Thirteen headings, and nothing else — no formula, no example, no number. Everything below is what was inside those thirteen pictures. (The rendered PNG set contains p001, p005–p017 and p019: the four text-only body pages were not re-rendered, and p019 is the Thank You card.)

## 1. What DID extract (pages 2–4), reproduced for completeness

**Slide 2 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES.** "At the end of this lesson, you should be able to: identify problems associated with volumes of objects by means of integration; obtain the volumes of objects by integration, using disc method; obtain the volumes of objects by integration, using washer method; and obtain the volumes of objects by integration, using shell method."

**Slide 3 — INTRODUCTION.** "In this note, the applications of definite integrals to calculating volumes of solid shapes are considered. The idea will be to dissect the three dimensional objects into pieces that resemble discs or shells, whose volumes can be approximated. The volume of the object is obtained in the form of disks or a set of embedded shells, integrated over given intervals."

**Slide 4 — VOLUMES BY INTEGRATION.**
> "If a region in the plane is revolved about a given line, the resulting solid is a solid of revolution, and the line is called the axis of revolution. When calculating the volume of a solid generated by revolving a region bounded by a given function about an axis, follow the steps below:
> 1. Sketch the area and determine the axis of revolution, (this determines the variable of integration).
> 2. Sketch the cross-section, (disc, shell, washer) and determine the appropriate formula.
> 3. Determine the boundaries of the solid.
> 4. Set up the definite integral, and integrate."

## 2. Formulas and definitions that were invisible

**Slide 5 — the disc method, verbatim.**
> "The volume of a solid of revolution is given by
> **V = π∫ₐᵇ [R(x)]² dx**
> where R(x) is the region bounded by y = f(x) revolved the x-axis, and
> **V = π∫ₐᵇ [R(y)]² dy**
> where R(y) is the region bounded by x = f(y) revolved the y-axis."

**Slide 9 — the washer method, verbatim.**
> "If a solid of revolution has any hollow spots in it, we use the washer method. It is of the form of a circular washer of outer radius R and inner radius r. In this case, we have
> **V = π∫ₐᵇ ([R(x)]² − [r(x)]²) dx**
> and
> **V = π∫ₐᵇ ([R(y)]² − [r(y)]²) dy**,
> accordingly."

**Slide 13 — the shell method, verbatim.**
> "The shell method is used when integrating along an axis parallel to the axis of revolution. In this case, the volume is given by
> **V = 2π∫ₐᵇ x f(x) dx**
> where x is the radius of revolution, and
> **V = 2π∫ₐᵇ y f(y) dy**
> where y is the radius of revolution."

**Slide 17 — the SUMMARY slide, verbatim.**
> "The applications of definite integrals to calculating volumes of solid shapes are considered.
> The volume of a solid is obtained by integration using the disc, washer, and shell methods.
> Using the disc method, the volume is given by V = π∫ₐᵇ[R(x)]²dx.
> Using the washer method, the volume is given by V = π∫ₐᵇ([R(x)]² − [r(x)]²)dx.
> Using the shell method, the volume is given by V = 2π∫ₐᵇ x f(x)dx."

**That is the entire formula inventory of the deck — three formulas, each given in x and in y, and no others.** No slicing/Riemann derivation, no general V = ∫A(x)dx, no Pappus, no arc length, no surface area.

## 3. Worked examples that were invisible (six of them)

**Disc, Example 1 (slide 6).** "Let R be the region bounded by the curve y = f(x) = (−x + 1), the x-axis, and the lines x = 0 and x = 1. Find the volume of the solid of revolution obtained by revolving R about the x-axis."
> Solution. "Using the formula V = π∫ₐᵇ[R(x)]²dx where R(x) = (−x + 1) over a = 0, and b = 1, we have"
> V = π∫₀¹[(−x + 1)]²dx = π[ −(−x + 1)³/3 ]₀¹
> = π( (−(−1 + 1)³)/3 − ( (−(−0 + 1)³)/3 ) ) = **π/3**
> "Therefore, the volume is π/3 cubic units."

Note the antiderivative: she uses **−(−x + 1)³/3**, a reversed chain rule (divide by 3 for the new power, and again by the inner derivative −1). The deck never teaches substitution, so this step arrives unexplained. Expanding (1 − x)² = 1 − 2x + x² and using only the Week 7 power rule gives [x − x² + x³/3]₀¹ = 1/3, the same result.

**Disc, Example 2 (slide 7).** "Find the volume of the solid of revolution obtained by revolving the region between the parabola x = y² + 1, and the lines x = 3 about the line."
> Solution. "Setting y² + 1 = 3, we have x = R(y) = 2 − y², so that a = −√2 and b = √2. Therefore,"
> V = π∫₋√2^√2 [(2 − y²)]²dy = π∫₋√2^√2 [(4 − 4y² + y⁴)]²dy
> = π[ 4y − 4y³/3 + y⁵/5 ]₋√2^√2 = **64√2π/15**
> "Hence, the volume is 64√2π/15 cubic units."

Two defects on this slide, both recorded in section 6 below: the problem statement never says which line, and the expanded integrand keeps a stray outer square. The answer 64√2π/15 is correct and has been verified independently.

**Washer, Example 1 (slide 10 — headed "DISC METHOD", see section 6).** "Find the volume of the solid formed by revolving the region bounded by the graphs of y = x², y = x⁵, x = 0, and x = 1 about the x-axis."
> Solution. "Here, R(x) = x², and r(x) = x⁵, so that"
> V = π∫₀¹ ([x²]² − [x⁵]²)dx = π∫₀¹ (x⁴ − x¹⁰)dx
> = π[ x⁵/5 − x¹¹/11 ]₀¹ = **6π/55**
> "Hence, the volume is 6π/55 cubic units."

**Washer, Example 2 (slide 11).** "Find the volume of the solid formed by revolving the region bounded by the graphs of x = y², x = 3, and x = 0 about the y-axis."
> Solution. "Here, R(y) = 3, and r(y) = y², so that"
> V = π∫₀^√3 (3² − [y²]²)dy = π∫₀^√3 (9 − y⁴)dy
> = π[ 9y − y⁵/5 ]₀^√3 = **36√3π/5**
> "Hence, the volume is 36√3π/5 cubic units."

The unshown arithmetic: (√3)⁵ = 9√3, so 9√3 − 9√3/5 = 36√3/5.

**Shell, Example 1 (slide 14).** "Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating about the y-axis the region bounded by y = 2x² − x³ and y = 0."
> Solution. "Given that f(x) = 2x² − x³, then setting f(x) = 0, we have x = 0 and x = 2. Therefore, the volume is"
> V = 2π∫₀² x(2x² − x³)dx = 2π∫₀² (2x³ − x⁴)dx
> = 2π[ x⁴/2 − x⁵/5 ]₀² = 2π( 8 − 32/5 ) = **16π/5**
> "Hence, the volume is 16π/5 cubic units."

**Shell, Example 2 (slide 15).** "Use the shell method to find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating about the x-axis the region under the curve y = √x from 0 to 1."
> Solution. "Given that y = √x, we have x = y² so that y = 0, y = 1 and f(y) = 1 − y². Therefore, the volume is"
> V = 2π∫₀¹ y(1 − y²)dy = 2π∫₀¹ (y − y³)dy = 2π[ y²/2 − y⁴/4 ]₀¹
> = 2π( 1/2 − 1/4 ) = **π/2**
> "Hence, the volume is π/2 cubic units."

This is the deck's only solid that can be done by two of its own methods, and they agree: by discs, π∫₀¹[√x]²dx = π∫₀¹x dx = π/2. That cross-check is the most useful thing in the deck and appears nowhere in the extracted text.

**All six answers were re-derived symbolically before being written into the new material. All six are correct as printed.**

## 4. Tasks that were invisible (three, with no answers given)

- **Slide 8, TASK.** "Find the volume of the solid of revolution obtained by revolving f(x) = √x, the x-axis, and the lines x = 1 and x = 4 about the x-axis." → V = π∫₁⁴ x dx = π[x²/2]₁⁴ = **15π/2**.
- **Slide 12, TASK.** "Find the volume of the solid formed when the region under the curve y = x² over the interval [0,2] is rotated about the line y = −1." → washer with R = x² + 1, r = 1: V = π∫₀²(x⁴ + 2x²)dx = π(32/5 + 16/3) = **176π/15**.
- **Slide 16, TASK.** "Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating about the y-axis the region between y = x and y = x²." → shells with radius x, height x − x², limits 0 and 1: V = 2π∫₀¹(x² − x³)dx = **π/6**.

The deck sets all three and answers none. Slide 12 is the only place in the entire deck where the axis of revolution is a line other than a coordinate axis, and slide 16 is the only place where a shell's height is a difference of two curves stated as such in the question — so these are the two most likely exam templates, and neither has a model answer on the slides.

## 5. Notation the extraction lost

- **Square-bracket evaluation, [F(x)]ₐᵇ**, on every worked example (slides 6, 7, 10, 11, 14, 15). This is a change from the Week 7 deck, which used the bar notation F(x)|ₐᵇ. Same meaning, F(b) − F(a); different symbol.
- **R and r for outer and inner radius**, and **R(y), r(y)** when integrating in y — but the disc slide calls R "the region", which every example contradicts by using it as a length.
- **Radii written with the bracket squared**: [R(x)]², [x²]², [x⁵]², [r(y)]² — she keeps the bracket and puts the exponent outside, which is what makes the difference between π∫(R² − r²) and π∫(R − r)² visible on the page.
- **f as the height of the shell strip**, not as the original curve: slide 15's f(y) = 1 − y² is the distance from x = y² across to x = 1, even though the curve in the question is y = √x.
- **Limits obtained by solving an equation, shown explicitly**: "Setting y² + 1 = 3", "setting f(x) = 0, we have x = 0 and x = 2". She never reads a limit off a picture.
- **Answers left in exact surd form** — 64√2π/15, 36√3π/5 — never decimalised, and always closed with "cubic units".
- **Every integrand in the deck is a polynomial** once the squaring is done. There is not one trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic integrand in the whole 19 pages.

## 6. Errors and defects on the slides

1. **Slide 13 (shell method), substantive and worth flagging loudly.** The slide reads: "The shell method is used when integrating along an axis **parallel** to the axis of revolution." This is backwards, and her own two examples disprove it. Shell Example 1 revolves about the **y-axis** and integrates **dx**; Shell Example 2 revolves about the **x-axis** and integrates **dy**. Both integrate **perpendicular** to the axis of revolution — which is precisely what distinguishes shells from discs and washers, where the variable runs *along* the axis. The formulas on the slide are correct; only the describing sentence is wrong, so no computed answer in the deck is affected. **The corrected wording is what has been taught in the new material, with the slide's version named**, because it will be quoted back in an exam otherwise.
2. **Slide 10, heading.** Headed "VOLUMES BY INTEGRATION: DISC METHOD", but the working is a washer: R(x) = x², r(x) = x⁵ and the integrand ([R]² − [r]²). It sits between the washer formula slide (9) and Washer Example 2 (11), and the heading was evidently carried over from the previous section. Confirmed independently in the text extraction, where "DISC METHOD" appears sandwiched between two "WASHER METHOD" headings. **The new material presents it as Washer Example 1 and states the mislabelling**, so that a student does not come away believing the disc formula subtracts an inner radius.
3. **Slide 7, problem statement truncated.** "…revolving the region between the parabola x = y² + 1, and the lines x = 3 **about the line.**" The axis is never named. Her own working settles it — R(y) = 2 − y² is the distance 3 − (y² + 1), so the axis is the line **x = 3**. Note also "the lines" is plural where there is one line.
4. **Slide 7, stray exponent.** The middle expression is printed as π∫₋√2^√2 **[(4 − 4y² + y⁴)]²** dy. The square must be dropped once (2 − y²)² has been expanded; as printed it would be a degree-8 integrand and would not produce the antiderivative on the next line. The following line and the answer 64√2π/15 are both correct, so the defect is confined to that one expression.
5. **Slide 11, third boundary.** The region is stated as bounded by "x = y², x = 3, and x = 0", but x = 0 is the y-axis, which is the axis of revolution rather than a cutting edge. Her limits 0 to √3 show she means the first-quadrant half, whose lower edge is **y = 0**. Taken literally, the region between x = y² and x = 3 runs from y = −√3 to √3 and the volume would be 72√3π/5, twice her answer. Her limits are the ones to use; the boundary should read y = 0.
6. **Slide 5, imprecise wording (twice over).** "R(x) is the **region** bounded by y = f(x) **revolved the** x-axis" — R is a radius, not a region, and the word "about" is missing. The same sentence is repeated for R(y). Every example uses R as a length, so the slide is self-correcting two pages later, and the new summary says so as an aside rather than reproducing the loose phrasing as teaching.

Nothing else on the deck is wrong. All six worked answers and all three TASK answers have been checked symbolically.

## 7. Was the old summary wrong, incomplete, or basically fine?

**Its mathematics was largely sound, but it was not this lecturer's week, and it is being replaced rather than patched.** The old page carried an honest note at the top admitting the slide bodies were images and that "the formulas and worked examples below are the standard ones for the three methods the deck names, supplied here so the scope is usable." That note was accurate — and it is exactly the bug. Four problems:

**a) It contained none of her six worked examples.** Not one. The old page's seven invented examples were y = x² on [0,2] about the x-axis; y = x against y = x² about the x-axis; the same about the y-axis; about x = 3; about y = −1; y = x² with y = 4 about the y-axis; and a split triangle. The deck's actual examples — **(−x + 1)² on [0,1]; x = y² + 1 against x = 3 about x = 3; x² against x⁵ on [0,1]; x = y² against x = 3 about the y-axis; 2x² − x³ about the y-axis; √x on [0,1] about the x-axis** — appear nowhere on it. Two of the deck's three TASKs did coincidentally surface as invented worked examples (176π/15 and π/6), but unlabelled and unattributed, so a student had no way to know those two were the lecturer's own set questions; the third TASK, **√x from x = 1 to x = 4 giving 15π/2**, was absent entirely.

**b) A large amount of out-of-scope material.** The old page taught a general slicing framework V = ∫ₐᵇ A(s)ds with A(s) as cross-sectional area; a "Validity" paragraph about the axis lying outside the region's interior; splitting a region at a crossover and adding integrals; absolute-value radii |f(x) − k|; a Pappus check V = 2π·d̄·A using the centroid; sanity checks against the sphere and cone formulas; and a rule for choosing the variable when a boundary cannot be inverted. **None of that is on any of the 19 pages.** The deck gives three formulas and applies them; it never derives them, never discusses when a method fails, and never splits a region.

**c) The question set went far outside the deck's arithmetic.** Every integrand in the deck is a polynomial. The old question set asked for **π∫₀^π sin²x dx** (needs a double-angle identity, never taught), **π∫₀¹e^(2x)dx** (needs substitution, never taught), **π∫₁²x⁻²dx** with a ln-based distractor, and **π∫₋ᵣʳ(r² − x²)dx** with symbolic r and h to derive the sphere and cone formulas. It also asked for the region between y = x² and y = 2x about y = −2, and a triangle about the x-axis "explaining why a single disk integral will not do" — a splitting problem the deck never poses. A student who drilled that set spent most of the hour on techniques from later in a standard calculus course.

**d) Presentation.** Options were lettered a) b) c) d) on a single run-on line rather than A. B. C. D. on separate lines, which is unreadable on a phone. Answer letters happened to be reasonably spread, so that part was fine.

**What the old page got right**, and what has been kept: the four-step procedure (it extracted as text, so it was genuinely from slide 4); the three formulas in their basic shape; and the warnings about (R − r)² versus R² − r², about π versus 2π, and about squaring the shell integrand. Those survive into the new material — now attached to her numbers instead of invented ones.

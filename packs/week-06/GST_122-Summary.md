# GST_122 — Week 6 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 6 (12–18 Oct 2026) · Topic: Steps to Writing — Summary, Letters, Report*

## The 7 most examinable things this week

1. **Summary** = a *condensed and concise version of a larger text or document, highlighting the main ideas, key points and essential information*, giving a brief overview **without unnecessary details** so readers grasp the core content quickly. Learn that definition close to verbatim.
2. **Letter** = a *written message addressed to a specific person or group*, following a **formal or informal** format, serving to convey information, express thoughts or feelings, make requests, or establish and maintain connections — in personal, professional or business contexts.
3. **Report** = a *formal document presenting detailed information, analysis, findings and conclusions on a specific topic or issue*, in a structured format of **introduction, methodology, findings, conclusions, recommendations**; used in academic, business and professional settings to communicate research, investigations or evaluations.
4. The **eight ordered steps of summary writing**: read carefully → identify the thesis → highlight key points → condense in your own words → maintain structure → eliminate unnecessary details → check coherence → review and revise.
5. The **nine ordered steps of letter writing**: identify purpose and audience → choose the format → greeting → introduction → body → politeness and tone → closing → closing salutation → review and edit.
6. The **eleven ordered steps of report writing**: define purpose → research and gather → outline the structure → introduction → methodology → analyse and present findings → draw conclusions → make recommendations (if applicable) → write a summary → references and appendices → review and revise.
7. All three procedures **end with the same step**: a final review/edit for clarity, coherence and accuracy that also checks the piece achieves its intended purpose. Whatever else you forget, that step is common to all three.

## Steps to writing a summary — what each step actually requires

| # | Step | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read the text carefully | Understand the main ideas and key points before writing anything |
| 2 | Identify the thesis or main idea | Determine the **primary argument or message** — everything else is subordinate to it |
| 3 | Highlight key points | Pick out the supporting details and examples that **reinforce the main idea** |
| 4 | Condense information | Restate the key points **in your own words**, aiming at brevity and clarity |
| 5 | Maintain structure | **Retain the logical order of the original** while condensing — do not re-argue it |
| 6 | Eliminate unnecessary details | Omit minor details and examples **unless they are crucial to the main idea** (the qualifier is examinable) |
| 7 | Check for coherence | The summary must flow smoothly and keep the coherence of the original |
| 8 | Review and revise | Edit for conciseness, accuracy and clarity; confirm it **accurately represents** the original |

Standard conventions that go with these steps: a summary carries **no new material, no personal opinion, no direct quotation** and no illustration or figure of speech; it is written in **continuous prose in your own words**, in the third person, and runs to roughly **one third or less** of the original.

## Steps to writing a letter — and the parts of the finished letter

Steps in order: **identify purpose and audience** (why you write and who reads it) → **choose the right format** (formal, informal, business — determined by that purpose and audience) → **greeting**, a proper salutation addressing the recipient appropriately → **introduction**, briefly stating the purpose or reason for writing → **body**, the main content in organised paragraphs, **each paragraph addressing one specific point** → **politeness and tone**, respectful and appropriate throughout → **closing**, summarising the main points and expressing gratitude or goodwill → **closing salutation**, a suitable closing phrase plus your signature → **review and edit** for grammar, clarity and coherence, checking the letter achieves its purpose.

The lesson's sample (a letter of appreciation) names **five key parts**, in this order:

1. **Greeting** — "Dear Mr Azeez,"
2. **Opening sentence** — "I hope this letter finds you well."
3. **Purpose of the letter** — "I am writing to express my gratitude for your unwavering support and collaboration in our recent project."
4. **Closing** — a polite close such as "Best regards" or "Sincerely"
5. **Signature** — sign with your name

The sample also carries a **subject heading** ("Letter of Appreciation") and closes "**Yours sincerely**". The convention behind that: *Yours sincerely* goes with a **named** recipient ("Dear Mr Azeez"); *Yours faithfully* goes with an **impersonal** salutation ("Dear Sir/Madam"). A formal letter additionally carries the writer's address and date, and the recipient's address, above the salutation.

## Steps to writing a report — and where each sample section belongs

Define the purpose → research and gather information → outline the structure → write the introduction (**topic, purpose and scope**) → present methodology (**the methods used to gather data, ensuring transparency and reliability**) → analyse and present findings (main findings **with supporting evidence**, using graphs, charts or tables if necessary) → draw conclusions (**summarise the key findings and interpret them**) → make recommendations, if applicable (**actionable** suggestions based on the conclusions) → write a summary of main points and outcomes → include references and appendices → review and revise.

Traced through the lesson's poverty report: *Introduction* — aim to analyse global poverty's causes, consequences and solutions. *Methodology* — systematic review of statistical agencies, research institutions and NGOs, whose own methods were critically evaluated; that is "transparency and reliability" in practice. *Findings* — a declining global trend but persistent regional variation (Sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Asia) traced to lack of access to education, healthcare and economic opportunity. *Conclusions* — eradication requires multifaceted intervention on both immediate needs and long-term systemic issues. *Recommendations* — investment in education and healthcare infrastructure, targeted economic development, social safety nets, cross-sector collaboration. *Summary* — the state of poverty, its root causes and solutions, and the need for a holistic, internationally cooperative approach.

## Commonly confused

- **Conclusions vs recommendations** — a conclusion **interprets what the findings mean**; a recommendation **prescribes what someone should now do**. Test: can it be given to a person as an instruction with a budget attached? Then it is a recommendation.
- **Findings vs conclusions** — findings are the **evidence itself** (rates, regional variation, tables and charts); conclusions are the **judgement drawn from it**. New data never appears in the conclusions.
- **A report's Introduction vs its Summary** — the introduction comes **first** and states topic, purpose and scope, i.e. what the report is *about to* do; the summary comes **near the end** and restates the main points and outcomes, i.e. what it *did*.
- **"Closing" vs "Closing salutation"** in the letter steps — *closing* is the final **paragraph** (summarise, thank, conclude); *closing salutation* is the **sign-off phrase plus signature** ("Yours sincerely, / Katie Afam").
- **Summary vs report** — both condense, but a summary is **derived entirely from one source text** and adds nothing; a report is **generated by the writer's own research**, and adds methodology, conclusions and recommendations that exist nowhere in a source.
- **Condensing vs quoting** — condensing means restating **in your own words**; a "summary" stitched from the original's own sentences has failed step 4 no matter how short it is. Likewise, keeping the **original's order** is step 5: reordering it for effect breaks the rule even where the new order is genuinely clearer.

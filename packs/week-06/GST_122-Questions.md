## Section A — Multiple choice (12)

**1.** A student condenses a 2,000-word article but moves the author's conclusion to the front because it "reads better that way". Which step of the summary procedure has she broken?
a) Highlight key points
b) Check for coherence
c) Maintain structure — retain the logical order of the original while condensing
d) Eliminate unnecessary details

**2.** The first step in the letter-writing procedure is:
a) Identify purpose and audience
b) Choose the right format
c) Begin with a greeting
d) Provide a brief introduction stating the reason for writing

**3.** The sample letter ends "Yours sincerely" rather than "Yours faithfully". The convention that decides this is:
a) Business letters always take "Yours sincerely"
b) Letters of appreciation take "Yours sincerely"; letters of complaint take "Yours faithfully"
c) "Yours sincerely" is used whenever the letter runs to more than one paragraph
d) "Yours sincerely" follows a named salutation; "Yours faithfully" follows an impersonal one (Dear Sir/Madam)

**4.** Which sequence correctly orders four consecutive steps of report writing?
a) Write a summary → draw conclusions → make recommendations → include references and appendices
b) Draw conclusions → make recommendations → write a summary → include references and appendices
c) Make recommendations → draw conclusions → include references and appendices → write a summary
d) Include references and appendices → draw conclusions → make recommendations → write a summary

**5.** A report states: "Access to education, healthcare and sustainable economic opportunities emerges as a critical factor in poverty alleviation." This sentence belongs in:
a) Methodology
b) Findings
c) Conclusions
d) Recommendations

**6.** Methodology appears in a report chiefly in order to:
a) Make the data-gathering transparent so the reliability of the findings can be judged
b) Bring the report up to the required length
c) List the sources cited, doing the work of a reference section
d) State what the report says should now be done

**7.** A "summary" turns out to reproduce whole sentences of the original word for word. Which step has it failed?
a) Read the text carefully
b) Identify the thesis or main idea
c) Review and revise
d) Condense information — restate the key points in your own words

**8.** "Eliminate unnecessary details" tells the summariser to omit minor details and examples:
a) Under all circumstances, since an example is never a main idea
b) Unless they are crucial to the main idea

**9.** In the letter-writing steps, "Closing" and "Closing Salutation" differ in that:
a) They are one step listed twice under two names

c) "Closing" is the final paragraph — summarise, thank, conclude; "Closing Salutation" is the sign-off phrase plus signature
d) "Closing" belongs to business letters and "Closing Salutation" to informal ones

**10.** Graphs, charts and tables are introduced at which step of report writing?
a) Present methodology
b) Analyse and present findings
c) Outline the structure
d) Include references and appendices

**11.** A student must give the Dean a document setting out how often the campus water supply fails, how those figures were collected, what the pattern means and what should be done about it. The correct text type and structure are:
a) A summary — thesis, key points, condensed restatement in the writer's own words
b) An informal letter — greeting, body, closing salutation
c) A formal letter — greeting, opening sentence, purpose, closing, signature

**12.** Which single step closes all three procedures taught this week — summary, letter and report?
a) A final review and edit for clarity, coherence and accuracy, confirming the piece achieves its purpose
b) A statement of actionable recommendations
c) A closing salutation and signature

## Section B — Short answer (3)

**13.** A classmate hands you a "summary" of a 1,200-word article. It runs to 900 words, is built largely out of the article's own sentences, opens with the article's most striking anecdote rather than its argument, and ends with the summariser's own view of the topic. Name four distinct faults, each against a named step or convention of summary writing.

**14.** Distinguish a summary, a letter and a report by (i) the definition given in the lesson, (ii) where the content comes from, and (iii) the structure each takes.

**15.** Name the five standard sections of a report, say what belongs in each, and give the one-line test that separates a conclusion from a recommendation.

## Answers

**1. c** — *maintain structure*: step 5 requires the summary to retain the logical order of the original while condensing it. Option a fails because key points were still identified; b confuses coherence (does it flow?) with order (does it follow the original?); d is about what is cut, not about sequence.

**2. a** — *identify purpose and audience*: everything downstream, including the choice of format, depends on why you are writing and to whom. Option b is genuinely the second step and is the trap — format is *chosen on the basis of* purpose and audience, so it cannot come first; c and d are steps three and four.

**3. d** — *salutation/complimentary-close agreement*: a named recipient ("Dear Mr Azeez") takes *Yours sincerely*; an impersonal "Dear Sir/Madam" takes *Yours faithfully*. Option a ties the close to the letter's category rather than its salutation; b invents a tone rule; c invents a length rule.

**4. b** — *the report sequence*: draw conclusions from the findings, recommend on the basis of those conclusions, then summarise, then add references and appendices, then review. Option c inverts conclusions and recommendations — you cannot recommend before you have concluded; a and d put back-matter or the closing summary ahead of the analytical steps.

**5. c** — *conclusions*: the sentence interprets what the findings mean without prescribing an action, which is exactly a conclusion. Option d is the sharpest distractor — a recommendation would read "invest in education and healthcare infrastructure"; b would be a raw figure or trend; a would describe how the data were collected.

**6. a** — *methodology*: it describes the methods used to gather data so that transparency and reliability can be judged. Option c confuses methodology with the reference section; d with recommendations; b treats a structural section as padding.

**7. d** — *condense information*: step 4 requires the key points to be restated in your own words. Option c is the trap — review and revise is a final check on conciseness and accuracy, not the step that forbids copying; a and b concern comprehension, which the copyist may well have achieved.

**8. b** — *eliminate unnecessary details*: the slide's qualifier is "unless they are crucial to the main idea", and it is the qualifier that is examined. Option a drops that qualifier and would delete an example the argument depends on; c and d attach the rule to length or to genre, neither of which the step mentions.

**9. c** — *closing vs closing salutation*: two separate steps — the closing paragraph summarises, thanks and concludes; the closing salutation is the sign-off phrase plus signature. Option a assumes redundancy in the step list; b reverses the two; d invents a formal/informal split.

**10. b** — *analyse and present findings*: the step specifies presenting the main findings with supporting evidence, using graphs, charts or tables if necessary. Option a would put the visuals with the description of method; d confuses supporting figures within the argument with the extra material stored in appendices.

**11. d** — *report*: only a report combines detailed information, an account of method, analysis of findings, conclusions and recommendations on a specific issue. Option c is the trap — the request is addressed to a person, but a letter has no methodology or findings section; a fails because the content is the student's own research, not a condensation of a source text.

**12. a** — *review and revise*: summary writing ends "review and revise", letter writing ends "review and edit", report writing ends "review and revise" — in each case a check for clarity, coherence and accuracy against the intended purpose. Options b, c and d each belong to exactly one of the three procedures.

**13.** *Summary conventions*: (i) **length** — at roughly 900 words from 1,200 it is not a condensation at all; a summary should run to about one third or less of the original, and step 4 demands brevity and clarity. (ii) **Not in her own words** — building the text from the original's own sentences fails "condense information: summarise the key points in your own words", and a summary admits no direct quotation. (iii) **Wrong opening / structure not maintained** — leading with an anecdote rather than the argument fails step 2 (identify the thesis or main idea) and step 5 (retain the logical order of the original); the anecdote is also the kind of illustrative detail step 6 tells her to cut unless it is crucial. (iv) **Personal opinion added** — a summary must represent the original accurately and add no new material or comment, so the closing view breaks both step 8 ("accurately represents the original") and the no-opinion convention.

**14.** *Summary vs letter vs report*: A **summary** is a condensed, concise version of a larger text, highlighting main ideas, key points and essential information without unnecessary details; its content comes **entirely from one source text** and adds nothing; structurally it is continuous prose in the original's own order, one third or less of its length. A **letter** is a written message addressed to a specific person or group, formal or informal, used to convey information, express thoughts or feelings, make requests, or establish and maintain connections; its content comes from the **writer's purpose towards a named reader**; structurally it runs greeting → opening sentence → statement of purpose → body paragraphs → closing → closing salutation and signature. A **report** is a formal document presenting detailed information, analysis, findings and conclusions on a specific topic; its content comes from the writer's **own research and data**; structurally it runs introduction → methodology → findings → conclusions → recommendations, with references and appendices.

**15.** *Report structure*: **Introduction** — the topic, the purpose and the scope of the report. **Methodology** — the methods used to gather the data, set out so that the reader can judge transparency and reliability. **Findings** — the main results with supporting evidence, presented with graphs, charts or tables where they help. **Conclusions** — the key findings summarised and interpreted; the judgement the evidence supports, with no new data introduced. **Recommendations** (if applicable) — actionable suggestions following from those conclusions. The test: a conclusion says **what the evidence means**; a recommendation says **what someone should now do** — if it can be handed to a person as an instruction with a budget attached, it is a recommendation.

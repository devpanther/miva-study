## Answers

**1. A** — *the indigenous trade era*: **1500 to 1800 AD**. **B**, 1800–1900, is the century of Atlantic-trade abolition and the beginning of colonial penetration, and **C**, 1914–1960, is the colonial period after amalgamation — both come *after* the system described, and both are periods of disruption rather than flourishing. **D** runs the era three centuries early.

**2. C** — the **Niger River**, which the lesson says **played a pivotal role in trade growth**. **Lake Chad**, the **Benue** and the **Cross River** are real trade waters, but the lesson singles out the Niger; picking one of them mistakes a tributary or a lake for the artery the lesson actually names.

**3. B** — colonial intrusion **transformed Nigeria's precolonial economy into the Western capitalist economic model**, altering social and political structures with it. **A** encodes the common belief that colonialism merely added a layer to an intact indigenous economy. **C** halves the disruption — the transformation was of the whole economic model, not just of long-distance trade. **D** is the longest option and the most detailed, and it is invented: the lesson says the apprenticeship system passed down skills and knowledge, and says nothing whatever about technical schools.

**4. D** — **140,431,790** at the **2006 census**. **B**, 180 million, is the **2015 estimate**, not a census figure — that is the swap this question tests. On the 2015 figure Nigeria is **first in Africa** and **sixth in the world**, after **China, India, the USA, Indonesia and Brazil**. **A** and **C** are figures the lesson never gives.

**5. A** — in **1960 agriculture absorbed over 75% of the workforce**, industry and other sectors taking the rest. **B** is the *output* statistic misapplied to labour: agriculture accounted for **over fifty per cent of GDP** before independence. **D** is a GNP figure from another week. Output share and employment share are two different numbers about the same sector, and the shortest option here is the right one.

**6. C** — **1.89% (1960) → 4.89% (1965)**, and Nigeria was tipped for the top 20 **ahead of Malaysia**. **A** substitutes the **1999** democratic-transition pair (**0.58% → 6.59%**). **B** reverses the direction, which would make independence a period of decline. **D** gets the growth right but names the wrong comparator.

**7. B** — the boom came first and **nothing was saved for rainy days**; the **crunch** then exposed the abandonment of agriculture; **austerity measures were introduced during the Buhari regime** in the mid-1980s as Nigeria's own response; **SAP came afterwards, under IMF pressure**. **A** and **C** put SAP before the crisis it was a response to. **D** inverts austerity and the boom. Note that the correct sequence here is the *shortest* of the four.

**8. D** — SAP **destroyed the manufacturing base**: the economy was **flooded with imported goods** and local products became uncompetitive on **price and quality**; the currency also **began the downward movement it has continued since**. **A** describes protectionism, the opposite of SAP. **B** invents a public-sector-only scope. **C** confuses **austerity** (which Nigeria did adopt first, under Buhari) with **SAP** (which it adopted later, under IMF pressure).

**9. A** — the **1990s** boom was **looted by the then Head of State, Sani Abacha**, who kept the funds **in foreign banks**. The failure of the **1970s** boom was different in kind — the government simply **did not save for rainy days**. **C** misdates the **USD 20 billion debt relief**, which came under **Obasanjo** after 1999. **B** and **D** are inventions.

**10. C** — **USD 34 billion** was the **foreign reserve in 2015** under **Jonathan**, down from the **above USD 64 billion** reached under **Obasanjo**, and reached even though oil stood **above USD 100** for most of that administration. **USD 20 billion** is the **debt relief** figure, not a reserve; **A**, **B** and **D** each swap a reserve for a debt figure, or one president for another.

**11. B** — **shale oil production in the US**, which is **"awash with light crude"** in the words of the Swiss-based consultant **Olivier Jakob**. **C** mistakes a **consequence** for the cause: **India replaced the US as the major buyer** *after* the US pulled back. **A** names a real item from the causes list, but not the reason for the US decision. **D** is not in the lesson at all.

**12. D** — **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state** is on the list, alongside **economic diversification**, **curbing corruption**, **providing electricity to boost manufacturing**, **building industrial parks and clusters**, **boosting local refining capacity**, **improving the political-economic structure**, **reducing the cost of governance**, **effective tax collection** and **investing in tourism**. **C** proposes repeating the programme the lesson blames for destroying manufacturing; **A** and **B** are plausible policy ideas that the lesson does not list.

**13.** *Nigeria's economic trajectory since 1960.* **At independence**: agriculture was the hub — **over fifty per cent of GDP** before independence, **over 75% of the workforce in 1960** — with **Development Corporations** significant in the **regional political structure**; GDP growth rose from **1.89% (1960) to 4.89% (1965)**, and Nigeria was tipped to be among the **20 largest economies in the world by 1980, ahead of Malaysia**. **Post-civil war**: the **January coup, the counter-coup and the civil war** truncated economic progress, and the military government **destroyed the economic structure on which the regional political system was built**. **The 1970s**: an **oil boom**, but the government **did not save for rainy days**, and agriculture had been left because of the **easy oil money**; then a **crunch in the oil market**. **Mid-1980s**: the nation struggled to cope, **austerity measures were introduced during the Buhari regime**, and the **IMF pressured African countries, Nigeria included, to adopt the Structural Adjustment Programme**. **The SAP era**: SAP **further weakened** the economy, the **currency began a downward movement** it has continued since, and the **manufacturing base was destroyed** as the economy was **flooded with imported goods** that made local products uncompetitive on **price and quality**. **The 1990s**: a **second oil boom**, **looted by Sani Abacha** into **foreign banks**. **1999**: return to democracy; GDP growth **0.58% → 6.59%** (Premium Times, 2023); under **Obasanjo**, **USD 20 billion debt relief** and foreign reserves **above USD 64 billion**. **After Yar'Adua's death**, under **Goodluck Jonathan**, those savings were **spent with reckless abandon** and reserves fell to **USD 34 billion in 2015**, even though oil stood **above USD 100** for most of that administration. **2014 onward**: **US shale oil** meant less need for Nigeria's crude, the **US — the largest buyer — announced reduced purchases**, **India replaced it** as the major buyer, production dropped, and the budget has been **in deficit**, with the country **borrowing to balance it** and **further austerity measures** introduced.

**14.** *Causes and solutions.* **Causes**: **over-dependence on crude oil as a major source of revenue for the government**; **the level of corruption**; in the **banking sector**, **insider abuses**, **non-adherence to the corporate governance code** and **too many non-performing loans**, and in the **capital market**, **round-tripping and other corrupt practices**, which **led to the crashes of share prices and the economic meltdown**; **poor economic policies**; **the spate of violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; and **lack of economic planning/policy directions, untimely implementation of the national budget, and unguided utterances**. **Solutions**: **economic diversification**, **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state**, **curb corruption**, **provide electricity to boost manufacturing**, **build industrial parks and clusters**, **boost local refining capacity**, **improve the political-economic structure of the country**, **reduce the cost of governance**, **effective tax collection**, and **invest in tourism**. The conclusion singles out **diversification away from crude oil** as decisive, since **renewable energy is gradually becoming popular and there is less need for crude**, and adds that one of Nigeria's **most vital resources is its people**, in whom government should invest more. Note the discrepancy: the deck's **Summary slide says five causes of economic problems were identified** while the causes slide **lists six bullets** — quote the deck's own figure of **five** if a question asks for a number.

**15.** *The indigenous trade system, c. 1500–1800 AD, as the lesson gives it.* It **thrived between 1500 and 1800 AD**. The lesson emphasises **the role of geography and natural resources in shaping economic activities**: the indigenous economies were **shaped by geographical factors**, with **varying regions specialising in different activities like agriculture, trade and craftsmanship**. That **economic specialisation created the need for trade**, so **communities engaged in trade to exchange the goods they produced for those they needed**, **forming a web of economic connections** and **leading to inter-group exchanges and cultural diffusion**. The **Niger River played a pivotal role in trade growth**, and **international markets attracted traders from various regions**. The **apprenticeship system** is discussed as having **played a vital role in passing down skills and knowledge**. Finally, **colonial intrusion disrupted this traditional system, transforming Nigeria's precolonial economy into the Western capitalist economic model** and **altering social and political structures**.

*Boundary note for Q15: the lesson stops exactly there. It does not say which natural resources made which regions unequal, and it does not describe how an apprenticeship was served or for how long. Do not supply either detail in an exam — write what the lesson states and no more.*

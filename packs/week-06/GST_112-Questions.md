## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Trace Nigeria's economic development from 1960 to the present as the lesson sets it out, giving the figures attached to each stage.

## Answers

**1. B** — *the indigenous trade era*: **1500 to 1800 AD**. **1800–1900** is the century of Atlantic-trade abolition and the beginning of colonial penetration, and **1914–1960** is the colonial period after amalgamation — both come *after* the system described, and both are periods of disruption rather than flourishing.
**2. C** — the **Niger River**. It carried bulk goods cheaply, linked the interior to the coast, and made riverside settlements into markets. **Lake Chad** and the **Benue** and **Cross** rivers were real trade waters, but the lesson names the Niger; picking one of them mistakes a tributary or a lake for the artery the lesson singles out.
**3. B** — colonial intrusion **transformed the precolonial economy into the Western capitalist economic model**, altering social and political structures with it. **A** encodes the common belief that colonialism merely added a layer to an intact indigenous economy; **C** confuses the apprenticeship system's *decline* with a policy of replacement; **D** halves the disruption — the transformation was of the whole economic model, not just of long-distance trade.
**4. B** — **140,431,790** at the **2006 census**. **180 million** is the **2015 estimate**, not a census figure — that is the swap the question is testing. On the 2015 figure Nigeria is **first in Africa** and **sixth in the world**, after **China, India, the USA, Indonesia and Brazil**.
**5. B** — in **1960 agriculture absorbed over 75% of the workforce**, industry and other sectors the rest. **A** is the *output* statistic misapplied to labour: agriculture accounted for **over fifty per cent of GDP** before independence. **C** is a Week 5 GNP figure. Output share and employment share are two different numbers about the same sector.
**6. B** — **1.89% (1960) → 4.89% (1965)**, and Nigeria was tipped for the top 20 **ahead of Malaysia**. **A** substitutes the **1999** democratic-transition pair (**0.58% → 6.59%**); **D** reverses the direction, which would make independence a period of decline; **C** gets the growth right but names the wrong comparator.
**7. B** — the boom came first and **nothing was saved for rainy days**; the **crunch** exposed the abandonment of agriculture; **austerity measures were introduced under the Buhari regime** in the mid-1980s as Nigeria's own response; **SAP came afterwards, under IMF pressure**. **A** and **C** put SAP before the crisis it was a response to; **D** inverts austerity and the boom.
**8. B** — SAP **destroyed the manufacturing base**: the economy was **flooded with imported goods** and local products could not compete on **price or quality**; the currency also **began the downward movement it has continued since**. **A** describes protectionism, the opposite of SAP; **D** confuses **austerity** (which Nigeria did adopt first, under Buhari) with **SAP** (which it also adopted, later).
**9. B** — the **1990s** boom was **looted by Sani Abacha** into **foreign banks**. The failure of the **1970s** boom was different in kind — the government simply **did not save**. **C** misdates the **$20 billion debt relief**, which came under **Obasanjo** after 1999.
**10. C** — **$34 billion** was the **foreign reserve in 2015** under **Jonathan**, down from the **above $64 billion** reached under **Obasanjo**, and reached even though oil was **above $100** for most of that administration. **$20 billion** is the **debt relief** figure, not a reserve; **A**, **B** and **D** each swap a reserve for a debt figure or a president for another.
**11. B** — **shale oil production in the US**, which is **"awash with light crude"** in the words of the Swiss-based consultant **Olivier Jakob**. **D** mistakes a **consequence** for the cause: **India replaced the US as the major buyer** *after* the US pulled back. **A** names a real problem from the causes list, but not the reason for the US decision.
**12. B** — **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state** is on the list, alongside **economic diversification**, **curbing corruption**, **providing electricity to boost manufacturing**, **building industrial parks and clusters**, **boosting local refining capacity**, **improving the political-economic structure**, **reducing the cost of governance**, **effective tax collection** and **investing in tourism**. **C** proposes repeating the programme the lesson blames for destroying manufacturing; **A** and **D** are plausible policy ideas that the lesson does not list.
**13.** *Nigeria's economic trajectory since 1960.* **At independence**: agriculture was the hub — **over fifty per cent of GDP** before independence, **over 75% of the workforce in 1960** — with **Development Corporations** significant in the **regional political structure**; GDP growth rose from **1.89% (1960) to 4.89% (1965)**, and Nigeria was tipped to be among the **20 largest economies in the world by 1980, ahead of Malaysia**. **Post-civil war**: the **January coup, the counter-coup and the civil war** truncated progress, and the military **destroyed the economic structure on which the regional political system was built**. **The 1970s**: an **oil boom**, but the government **did not save for rainy days**, and agriculture was abandoned for **easy oil money**; then a **crunch in the oil market**. **Mid-1980s**: the nation struggled, **austerity measures were introduced under the Buhari regime**, and the **IMF pressured African countries, Nigeria included, to adopt the Structural Adjustment Programme**. **The SAP era**: SAP **further weakened** the economy, the **currency began a downward movement**, and the **manufacturing base was destroyed** by **imported goods** that made local products uncompetitive on **price and quality**. **The 1990s**: a **second oil boom**, **looted by Sani Abacha** into **foreign banks**. **1999**: return to democracy; GDP growth **0.58% → 6.59%**; under **Obasanjo**, **$20 billion debt relief** and foreign reserves **above $64 billion**. **After Yar'Adua's death**, under **Goodluck Jonathan**, those savings were **spent with reckless abandon** and reserves fell to **$34 billion in 2015** despite oil **above $100**. **2014 onward**: **US shale oil** cut demand, the **US announced reduced purchases**, **India replaced it** as the major buyer, production dropped, and the budget has been **in deficit**, with the country **borrowing to balance it** and **further austerity measures** introduced.
**14.** *Causes and solutions.* **Causes**: **over-dependence on crude oil as the government's major source of revenue**; **corruption**; in the **banking sector**, **insider abuses**, **non-adherence to the corporate governance code** and **too many non-performing loans**, and in the **capital market**, **round-tripping and other corrupt practices**, which **crashed share prices and produced the economic meltdown**; **poor economic policies**; **the spate of violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; and **lack of economic planning and policy direction, untimely implementation of the national budget, and unguided utterances**. **Solutions**: **economic diversification**, **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state**, **curbing corruption**, **providing electricity to boost manufacturing**, **building industrial parks and clusters**, **boosting local refining capacity**, **improving the political-economic structure of the country**, **reducing the cost of governance**, **effective tax collection**, and **investing in tourism**. The conclusion singles out **diversification away from crude oil** as decisive, since **renewable energy is becoming popular and the need for crude is falling**, and adds that Nigeria's **most vital resource is its people**. Note the discrepancy: the deck's **Summary slide says five causes were identified** while the causes slide **lists six bullets** — quote the deck's own figure of **five** if a question asks for a number.
**15.** *The indigenous trade system, c. 1500–1800 AD.* It **thrived between 1500 and 1800 AD** and was shaped by **geographical factors and natural resources**: soil, rainfall, vegetation and mineral endowment made regions unequal, so different areas **specialised in agriculture, trade or craftsmanship**. Because **no community was self-sufficient**, specialisation **created the need to exchange**: communities traded what they produced for what they needed, forming a **web of economic connections** between them. The **Niger River** was **pivotal to the growth of trade**, carrying bulk goods and linking interior to coast, and **international markets attracted traders from other regions**. Beyond goods, the system carried **cultural diffusion** — **inter-group exchange** spread language, religion, craft technique and political ideas along the trade routes. Skills and knowledge were transmitted through the **apprenticeship system**, in which a young person served under a master craftsman or trader for a term and was then established in the trade himself, reproducing the craft economy across generations. **Colonial intrusion disrupted the whole arrangement**, **transforming the precolonial economy into the Western capitalist economic model** — production reoriented to **export** and **imported manufactures** displaced local crafts — and thereby **altering the social and political structures** that rested on indigenous trade.

# GST_112 — Week 6 Summary

*Nigerian Peoples and Culture · Week 6 (12–18 Oct 2026) · Topic: Nigeria's indigenous trade system, c. 1500–1800 AD, and Nigeria's modern economic realities*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **The indigenous trade system thrived between 1500 and 1800 AD.** It was shaped by **geography and natural resources**: different regions specialised in **agriculture, trade or craftsmanship**, and communities traded what they produced for what they lacked. The **Niger River** was pivotal to the growth of that trade, and **international markets** drew traders from other regions.
2. **Colonial intrusion disrupted the indigenous system**, transforming Nigeria's precolonial economy into the **Western capitalist economic model** and altering **social and political structures** with it.
3. **Population**: the **2006 census** gives **140,431,790**; by **2015** the estimate was **180 million** — **first in Africa** and **sixth most populated in the world**, after **China, India, the USA, Indonesia and Brazil**.
4. **Before independence, agriculture accounted for over fifty per cent of Nigeria's GDP; in 1960 it absorbed over 75% of the workforce**, industry and other sectors taking the rest. **Development Corporations** played a significant role in the **regional political structure**.
5. **GDP growth jumped from 1.89% in 1960 to 4.89% in 1965.** Nigeria was tipped to be **one of the 20 largest economies in the world by 1980, ahead of Malaysia**.
6. **The sequence that broke the economy: 1970s oil boom → the government saved nothing for a rainy day → a crunch in the oil market → agriculture already abandoned for easy oil money → the nation struggled in the mid-1980s → austerity measures under the Buhari regime → IMF pressure → the Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP).**
7. **1999, return to democracy: GDP growth rose from 0.58% to 6.59%** (Premium Times, 2023). Under **Obasanjo**, Nigeria got **debt relief of $20 billion** and foreign reserves **jumped above $64 billion**; after **Yar'Adua's death**, under **Goodluck Jonathan** those savings were spent "with reckless abandon" and reserves **fell to $34 billion in 2015**, even though oil stood **above $100** for most of that administration.
8. **The causes of Nigeria's economic woes**, as the lesson lists them: **over-dependence on crude oil**; **corruption**; **abuses in the banking sector and capital market**; **poor economic policies**; **violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; and **lack of economic planning/policy direction, untimely implementation of the national budget and unguided utterances**.

## Part 1 — The indigenous trade system, c. 1500–1800 AD

- **Geography determines specialisation.** Soil, rainfall, vegetation and mineral endowment made regions unequal in what they could produce; no community was self-sufficient, so **specialisation created the need to exchange**.
- **Trade built a web of economic connections** between communities, and with it **cultural diffusion** — language, religion, dress, craft technique and political ideas travelled the trade routes with the goods.
- **The Niger River** (with its tributaries and the creeks of the delta) was the main artery: it carried bulk goods cheaply, linked the interior to the coast, and made riverside settlements into markets.
- **International markets** attracted traders from beyond Nigeria — the **trans-Saharan trade** to the north and, later, **Atlantic coastal trade** to the south.
- **The apprenticeship system** was how skills and knowledge were transmitted: a young person was attached to a master craftsman or trader, worked under him for a fixed term, and was then established in the trade himself. It reproduced the craft economy across generations without formal schooling.
- **Colonialism** replaced this with a **Western capitalist economy**: production reoriented from local exchange to **export cash crops**, imported manufactures displaced local crafts, and the social and political structures resting on indigenous trade were broken.

## Part 2 — Nigeria's modern economic realities

**Endowment.** Solid minerals — **crude oil, gold, tin, iron ore, niobium, lead, zinc, limestone, salt** — plus arable land producing **palm oil, cocoa, groundnut, beans, melon, corn, rice**. **Agriculture was the hub of the economy before oil was found in commercial quantity**; with oil the country shifted from **agriculture-dependent** to **crude-oil-dependent**.

| Period | What happened | The numbers to quote |
|---|---|---|
| **At independence, 1960–65** | Agriculture dominant, economy growing, Development Corporations central to the regions | Agriculture **>50% of GDP** pre-independence, **>75% of the workforce** in 1960; GDP **1.89% (1960) → 4.89% (1965)**; tipped for the **top 20 economies by 1980, ahead of Malaysia** |
| **Post-civil war, 1970s** | January coup, counter-coup and civil war truncated economic progress; the military **destroyed the economic structure on which the regional political system was built**; **oil boom**, but **no savings for rainy days** | — |
| **Mid-1980s** | Oil market **crunch**; agriculture already abandoned; **austerity measures under the Buhari regime**; **IMF pressure** on African countries to adopt SAP | — |
| **The SAP era** | SAP **further weakened** the economy; the **currency began a downward movement** and has continued since; SAP **destroyed the manufacturing base** as the economy was **flooded with imported goods**, making local products uncompetitive on **price and quality** | — |
| **The 1990s** | **Another oil boom**, but Head of State **Sani Abacha looted the treasury** and kept the funds **in foreign banks** | — |
| **1999–2007, Obasanjo** | Return to democracy; recovery | GDP growth **0.58% → 6.59%**; **$20 billion debt relief**; reserves **above $64 billion** |
| **2010–2015, Jonathan** | Savings of the previous administration spent recklessly | Reserves **down to $34 billion in 2015**, with oil **above $100** for most of the period |
| **2014 onward** | **US shale oil** production cut demand; in **2014** the **US — the largest buyer of Nigeria's crude — announced reduced purchases**; **India replaced the US** as the major buyer; production dropped | Budget **in deficit**; the country **borrows to balance the budget**; further **austerity measures** |

**The Olivier Jakob quote** (a **Swiss-based consultant**): *"It's a very plausible scenario that one day the U.S. won't need to import crude oil from Nigeria; the U.S. is awash with light crude. Nigeria crude may need to be placed at a discount to go to new markets in Asia."*

**Causes, in the lesson's own order:** (1) **over-dependence on crude oil as the major source of government revenue**; (2) **corruption**; (3) in the **banking sector**, **insider abuses**, **non-adherence to the corporate governance code** and **too many non-performing loans**, and in the **capital market**, **round-tripping** and other corrupt practices, which led to the **crash of share prices and the economic meltdown**; (4) **poor economic policies**; (5) **the spate of violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; (6) **lack of economic planning/policy direction, untimely implementation of the national budget, and unguided utterances**.

**Solutions, all ten:** **economic diversification**; **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state**; **curb corruption**; **provide electricity to boost manufacturing**; **build industrial parks and clusters**; **boost local refining capacity**; **improve the political-economic structure of the country**; **reduce the cost of governance**; **effective tax collection**; **invest in tourism**.

**Conclusion as the lesson states it:** Nigeria's economic problems **will worsen if nothing is done to diversify away from crude oil**, especially as **renewable energy becomes popular and the need for crude falls**. Nigeria's **most vital resource is its people**, and government should **invest more in Nigerians**.

## Commonly confused

- **"Over 50%" vs "over 75%".** **Over fifty per cent** is agriculture's share of **GDP before independence**; **over 75%** is the share of the **workforce** agriculture absorbed **in 1960**. One is output, the other is employment.
- **1.89% → 4.89% vs 0.58% → 6.59%.** The first pair is **1960 to 1965**, at independence. The second pair is the **return to democracy in 1999**. Do not swap the eras.
- **$20 billion, $64 billion, $34 billion.** **$20bn = debt relief under Obasanjo**; **$64bn = the foreign reserve peak under Obasanjo**; **$34bn = the reserve in 2015 under Jonathan**. Only the first is a debt figure.
- **Austerity measures vs SAP.** **Austerity measures came first, under the Buhari regime** in the mid-1980s, as Nigeria's own response to the oil crunch. **SAP came after**, adopted under **IMF pressure**. SAP is not the same thing as austerity and did not precede it.
- **The 1970s boom vs the 1990s boom.** The **1970s** boom was wasted by **failure to save**; the **1990s** boom was **looted by Abacha** into foreign banks. Different decade, different failure.
- **Who stopped buying, and who took over.** The **US** was the **largest buyer** and cut purchases in **2014** because of **its own shale oil**; **India replaced it** as the major buyer. The US did not stop because of Nigerian violence or vandalisation.
- **Cause vs consequence in the banking bullet.** **Insider abuse, non-performing loans and round-tripping** are the **causes**; the **crash of share prices and the economic meltdown** are the **consequences**. The lesson lists the meltdown as a result, not as a cause in its own right.
- **Diversification vs "economic tutelage from a metropolitan state".** Both are on the **solutions** list; diversification is the one the **conclusion** singles out as decisive.
- **Trade as exchange vs trade as cultural contact.** The indigenous system is examined both ways: **economically** it moved goods between specialised regions; **culturally** it produced **inter-group exchange and cultural diffusion**. A question naming "cultural exchange" is still a question about trade.

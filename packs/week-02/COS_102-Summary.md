# COS_102 — Week 2 Summary

*Introduction to Problem Solving · Week 2 (14–20 Sep 2026) · Topics: Problem Solving Strategies; Understanding Problems and Problem Types*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **A problem-solving strategy is a plan used to find a solution or overcome a challenge.** Each strategy **includes multiple steps that provide helpful guidelines** on how to resolve a business problem or industry challenge. Note the wording: a strategy is a *plan*, not a solution.
2. **Different problems require different strategies to obtain the best solution** — so you must consider several strategies, choose an appropriate one, and then **study the rules and procedures of that chosen strategy**. **An efficient strategy produces a robust and reliable solution.**
3. **The four strategies the week's Summary sheet names: brainstorming, heuristics, trial-and-error, means-ends analysis.** The lecture slides teach **two more — abstraction and analogy**. If asked "the four strategies discussed", give the first four; if asked to list the strategies taught, give all six. Learn both lists.
4. **Trial and error's suitability rule (the most-asked single fact):** useful **where the number of possible solutions is few**; **time-consuming and inefficient where many possible solutions exist.** The mid-lesson question turns on exactly this.
5. **Routine problem = any problem that can be solved with an arithmetic operation.** It supplies a question plus the required facts or numbers, **can be solved with algorithms**, is **solely about identifying the most appropriate algorithm to apply**, and can be solved very quickly.
6. **Non-routine problem = any problem that does not have a standard method for solving it.** Procedures and answers are not immediately clear; it needs **distinct, unconventional thinking based on originality and creativity**; usually solvable by various approaches; takes time. A **'novel' method** must be developed: **study the problem critically → gather relevant information → identify and implement an effective strategy.**
7. **Solvable = solvable by the Universal Turing Machine, i.e. by a systematic method (an algorithm). Unsolvable = not solvable by the UTM / by any algorithm.** But **"unsolvable" does NOT mean no solution can possibly be found** — it means **there is no systematic method for deciding**. So what we really have are **unsolvable *decision* problems**.
8. **Decidable = a systematic way can be found to solve the problem accurately. Undecidable = no algorithm can give an accurate true/false for every input value; undecidable problems are a subset of unsolvable problems.** Named examples: **the halting problem** (will a running program eventually halt or run for ever?) and **"does my code have a bug?"**.

## The six strategies, stated precisely

| Strategy | Definition (course wording) | Suits which problems | One-line example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | A **group-focused technique** where people come together to solve a problem; involves **critical thinking and coming up with various solutions spontaneously**. Rules: **avoid criticism of ideas**, **gather as many ideas as possible**, and **other people's ideas can be fine-tuned**. | Open, ill-defined problems needing many candidate solutions and diverse insight. | A cross-functional team meets to generate causes of XYZ Corp's falling product quality. |
| **Heuristics** | Obtains a solution **while saving time and energy**; **decisions are made without stopping to think about the next course of action**. Fast and efficient, but **solutions are not always rational**. | Situations where a **sub-optimal solution is acceptable**. | Rule of thumb; choosing between brands of food at the grocery store; migrating to a country you do not know. |
| **Trial and error** | **Recognising and removing errors or failures through various experimental trials**; various approaches are implemented **until a solution is arrived at**. | Where the **number of possible solutions is few**; **inefficient where many exist**. | Trying various jobs before choosing a career; living in various places before choosing a home. |
| **Means-ends analysis** | **Thinking about the solution beforehand and determining the best strategy to adopt based on the issues that will be encountered** while solving it. It **breaks the problem into smaller problems and solves each smaller problem with the best possible approach until the main problem is solved.** | Large goal-directed problems with a stated target and a gap to close. | Completing a degree syllabus in six months; a company targeting a $2 bn turnover in a year. |
| **Abstraction** | **Filtering out the unneeded attributes of the problem so we can concentrate on the important parts.** All aspects or patterns that will not help us reach a solution are **removed**; it forms **an essential idea of what the problem is and how to solve it** — that idea is called a **model**. | Problems obscured by irrelevant detail. | The **London Underground map**: true distances and geography removed, connections kept. |
| **Analogy** | **Using previously existing understanding to understand a new similar system, as long as the underlying principles are the same.** Steps: **define the problem and generate analogies → find similarities and select one → use the description and similarities to generate ideas.** | Genuinely new problems that share a **deep structure** with a solved one. | A doctor treating a deep inoperable tumour with high-intensity rays; a manager handling a difficult employee. |

**Root cause analysis** appears in the case study as the *methodology* step: identify the underlying process fault before acting, then analyse data (customer feedback, quality records, production reports), form a cross-functional team, strengthen quality control, train staff, and build a customer feedback loop.

## Understanding the problem, and how that fixes the strategy

- **Ill-defined problems** have **no clear goals, expected solutions or solution paths**. **Well-defined problems** have **clear goals, expected solutions or solution paths**. People meet both straightforward and complex problems daily.
- Understanding the problem comes **first**: *then* you decide how to solve it. The classification does the work — **routine → find and apply the right algorithm; non-routine → develop a novel method** via critical study, information gathering, and choosing an effective strategy.
- **Solvable problems based on data structures**: vectors, stacks, queues, arrays, heaps, trees, graphs, linked lists. **Solvable problems based on algorithms**: sorting, divide-and-conquer, recursive, searching, and text-processing problems.
- **The Universal Turing Machine (UTM)**, Alan Turing's 'universal computing machine', has a **pre-loaded single fixed table of instructions**. **Any task we can write an instruction table for can be implemented by the UTM**; the instruction table (program) is simply **put on the UTM's tape** as a string of letters, read, and then carried out on that tape.
- **Turing's two contributions to the modern computer**: (i) **manipulating a computer's functionality by storing encoded instructions in its memory**; (ii) **showing that a single machine of fixed structure can carry out every computation the UTM can**.
- **Substitution puzzles**: any puzzle — a chess problem, say — **can be re-expressed as a substitution puzzle**. Example rules: replace **OB → BOOB** and **BOB → O**. Question: can **BOOOB** be obtained from **BOB**? It can: `BOB → BBOOB → BBOBOOB → BOOOB`. Notice the string **grows before it shrinks** — which is precisely why **deciding whether an arbitrary substitution puzzle "comes out" is unsolvable**: no rule bounds how far you must search before giving up.

## Commonly confused

| Pair | The distinction |
|---|---|
| Trial and error vs heuristics | **Trial and error** actually *implements* approach after approach until one works — it needs **few** possible solutions. **Heuristics** does not try them at all: it **decides without stopping to think about the next course of action**, trading rationality for time and energy, and is only for when a **sub-optimal** answer is acceptable. |
| Means-ends analysis vs abstraction | Both "break the problem down", but **means-ends** keeps every sub-problem and **solves each with the best possible approach**, working from a stated target. **Abstraction** **throws material away** — the unneeded attributes are removed to leave a **model**. Reduction by solving vs reduction by discarding. |
| Abstraction vs analogy | **Abstraction** simplifies **this** problem by deleting irrelevant detail. **Analogy** imports understanding from a **different, already-understood** problem — and is only valid **if the underlying principles are the same**, not merely the surface features. |
| Brainstorming vs the other five | Brainstorming is the only **group-focused** strategy, and the only one that **forbids evaluation while it is running** (avoid criticism; gather as many ideas as possible). Judging ideas as they arrive is not strict brainstorming. |
| Routine vs non-routine | **Routine**: solvable by an **arithmetic operation**, the whole task is **picking the right algorithm**. **Non-routine**: **no standard method exists**, so a **novel method** must be developed with originality and creativity. Being numeric does not make a problem routine (estimating the hairs on your head is numeric and non-routine). |
| Unsolvable vs "has no solution" | **Unsolvable** means **no systematic method / algorithm** exists to decide it — not that no answer exists. A particular substitution puzzle may well come out; what is unsolvable is **deciding, in general, whether it does**. Hence: **unsolvable decision problems**. |
| Unsolvable vs undecidable | **Undecidable** = **no algorithm returns an accurate true/false for every input value**; undecidable problems are a **subset** of unsolvable problems. Halting problem and "does my code have a bug?" are the course's examples. |
| Non-routine vs undecidable | A **non-routine** problem has no *standard* method but can still be solved by inventing one. An **undecidable** problem has **no method at all**, however clever you are. Creativity rescues the first, never the second. |

**1.** Charges lie on the x-axis: −4.0 μC at x = 0, −2.0 μC at x = 3.0 m and +6.0 μC at x = 5.0 m. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is the net force on the −2.0 μC charge?
A. 0.019 N in the +x direction
B. 0.035 N in the −x direction
C. 0.035 N in the +x direction
D. 0.027 N in the +x direction

**2.** On the x-axis: Q₁ = −8.0 μC at x = −3.0 m, Q₂ = −6.0 μC at x = 1.0 m and Q₃ = +16 μC at x = 5.0 m. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is the net force on Q₁?
A. 0.045 N in the −x direction
B. 0.0090 N in the −x direction
C. 0.0090 N in the +x direction
D. 0.027 N in the −x direction

**3.** A +2.0 nC charge is at x = 0 and a +6.0 nC charge at x = 0.40 m. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is the electric field at x = 0.60 m?
A. 1400 N/C in the +x direction
B. 1300 N/C in the +x direction
C. 200 N/C in the +x direction
D. 1400 N/C in the −x direction

**4.** A +4.0 μC charge is at x = 0 and a −1.0 μC charge at x = 0.30 m. At which point on the x-axis is the resultant electric field zero?
A. x = 0.20 m
B. x = 0.10 m
C. x = −0.30 m
D. x = 0.60 m

**5.** Two point charges 2.0 cm apart repel with a force of 2.7 × 10⁻³ N. To what separation must they be moved so that the force is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ N?
A. 6.0 cm
B. 18 cm
C. 4.0 cm
D. 0.67 cm

**6.** Two small spheres carry equal charges q and repel each other with a force of 0.90 N when 3.0 cm apart. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is q?
A. 9.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ C
B. 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ C
C. 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ C
D. 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ C

**7.** Charges of −4.0 nC are fixed at x = 0 and at x = 0.60 m. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is the electric field at x = 0.20 m?
A. 1125 N/C in the −x direction
B. 0
C. 675 N/C in the −x direction
D. 675 N/C in the +x direction

**8.** Three point charges are held at fixed positions. Which statement correctly gives the net electric force on one of them?
A. The sum of the magnitudes of the two Coulomb forces, always
B. The vector sum of the Coulomb forces from each of the other two charges, worked out one pair at a time
C. Coulomb's law applied to the total charge of the other two, placed at their midpoint
D. The Coulomb force from the nearer charge only

**9.** A +5.0 nC charge is at x = 0 and a −4.0 nC charge at x = 0.90 m. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² and e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. What is the field at x = 0.30 m, and the force on an electron placed there?
A. 600 N/C along +x; 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ N along −x
B. 600 N/C along +x; 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ N along +x
C. 400 N/C along +x; 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁷ N along −x
D. 544 N/C along +x; 8.7 × 10⁻¹⁷ N along −x

**10.** Two protons are 4.0 nm apart. An electron lies on the line between them, 1.0 nm from one proton. Take k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² and e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. What is the net force on the electron?
A. 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ N toward the nearer proton
B. 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ N toward the nearer proton
C. 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ N toward the nearer proton
D. 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ N toward the farther proton

**11.** A positive test charge q₀ a distance r from a point charge Q feels a force of magnitude kQq₀/r². Using E = F/q₀, what is the field of Q, and its value for Q = +3.0 nC at r = 0.30 m with k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²?
A. E = kQq₀/r²; 300 N/C directed away from Q
B. E = kQ/r²; 90 N/C directed away from Q
C. E = kQ/r²; 300 N/C directed away from Q
D. E = kQ/r²; 300 N/C directed toward Q

**12.** A student puts q₁ = −6.0 μC and q₂ = +3.0 μC into F = kq₁q₂/r² with r = 2.0 m, gets −0.0405 N, and reads the minus sign as meaning the force is 0.0405 N to the left. What does that minus sign actually mean?
A. It marks the force as repulsive, the product of the charges being negative
B. It means the force points to the left, as the student read it
C. It means a force of −0.0405 N, force being a signed quantity in one dimension
D. It marks the force as attractive; the magnitude is 0.0405 N and the direction comes from where the charges sit

---

## Answers

**1. C** — *Net force on a charge when both forces point the same way.* From the −4.0 μC charge, 3.0 m to the left: F = (9.0 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(3.0)² = 0.072/9.0 = 0.0080 N, repulsive, so it pushes the −2.0 μC charge to the right (+x). From the +6.0 μC charge, 2.0 m to the right: F = (9.0 × 10⁹)(6.0 × 10⁻⁶)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(2.0)² = 0.108/4.0 = 0.027 N, attractive, pulling it to the right (+x). Same direction, so add: 0.035 N in the +x direction.

0.019 N subtracts the two forces as if they opposed; the −x option has the direction backwards (a negative charge on the left repels, a positive one on the right attracts, both rightward); 0.027 N ignores the −4.0 μC charge.

**2. B** — *Net force on an end charge when the forces oppose.* Separations: Q₁ to Q₂ is 4.0 m, Q₁ to Q₃ is 8.0 m. F₂₁ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(8.0 × 10⁻⁶)(6.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(4.0)² = 0.432/16 = 0.027 N; both negative, so Q₂ pushes Q₁ away, to the left (−x). F₃₁ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(8.0 × 10⁻⁶)(16 × 10⁻⁶)/(8.0)² = 1.152/64 = 0.018 N; unlike signs, so Q₃ pulls Q₁ to the right (+x). They oppose: net = 0.027 − 0.018 = 0.0090 N in the −x direction. The nearer, smaller Q₂ wins because distance enters squared.

0.045 N adds forces that point opposite ways; '+x' picks the direction of the larger charge instead of the larger force; 0.027 N ignores Q₃.

**3. A** — *Superposition of fields beyond two like charges.* Both charges are positive and both lie to the left of the field point, so both fields point away from them, in the +x direction. E₁ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.60)² = 18/0.36 = 50 N/C. For the second charge the distance is 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20 m: E₂ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(6.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.20)² = 54/0.040 = 1350 N/C. Add: 1400 N/C in the +x direction.

1300 N/C subtracts, as if the fields opposed; 200 N/C uses 0.60 m for both charges instead of the distance from each source to the point; the −x option has the direction of the field of a positive charge backwards.

**4. D** — *Zero-field point for unlike charges.* Between unlike charges the two fields point the same way and cannot cancel. Beyond the smaller charge (x > 0.30 m) they oppose, so set the magnitudes equal there: 4.0/x² = 1.0/(x − 0.30)². Square-root: 2/x = 1/(x − 0.30), so 2x − 0.60 = x and x = 0.60 m. Check: 4.0/0.36 = 11.1 and 1.0/0.09 = 11.1.

x = 0.20 m applies the like-charge method between the charges, where the fields actually add; x = 0.10 m and x = −0.30 m are on the side of the larger charge, where its field always dominates.

**5. A** — *Separation from a force ratio by inverse square.* F ∝ 1/r², so F₁/F₂ = (r₂/r₁)². The force must fall by 2.7 × 10⁻³/3.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 9, so (r₂/r₁)² = 9 and r₂/r₁ = 3. r₂ = 3 × 2.0 cm = 6.0 cm.

18 cm uses the ratio 9 without taking the square root; 4.0 cm doubles the distance, which only quarters the force; 0.67 cm moves the charges closer, which would increase the force.

**6. D** — *Finding a charge from Coulomb's law.* F = kq²/r², so q² = Fr²/k = (0.90)(0.030)²/(9.0 × 10⁹) = (0.90)(9.0 × 10⁻⁴)/(9.0 × 10⁹) = 8.1 × 10⁻⁴/9.0 × 10⁹ = 9.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ C². Square root: q = 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ C = 0.30 μC.

9.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ C stops at q² and forgets the root; 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ C leaves r in centimetres; 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ C forgets to square r.

**7. C** — *Superposition of fields between two like charges.* The field of a negative charge points toward it. From the charge at x = 0, 0.20 m away: E₁ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.20)² = 36/0.040 = 900 N/C toward x = 0, that is −x. From the charge at x = 0.60 m, 0.40 m away: E₂ = 36/0.16 = 225 N/C toward it, that is +x. They oppose: net = 900 − 225 = 675 N/C in the −x direction, toward the nearer charge.

1125 N/C adds fields that point opposite ways; '+x' picks the direction of the weaker field; 0 assumes the equal charges cancel, which happens only at the midpoint.

**8. B** — *Principle of superposition of forces.* Coulomb's law describes only a pair of point charges. For more than two, the principle of superposition (an experimental result) says the total force on a charge is the vector sum of the forces the other charges would exert individually. Along a line this means add when the arrows agree and subtract when they oppose.

Summing magnitudes is only right when the forces are parallel; lumping the other charges at their midpoint ignores the 1/r² weighting of each; the nearer charge is not the only one acting, it is simply weighted more heavily.

**9. A** — *Field between unlike charges then force on an electron.* The +5.0 nC charge is 0.30 m away: E₁ = 45/0.090 = 500 N/C pointing away from it, so along +x. The −4.0 nC charge is 0.90 − 0.30 = 0.60 m away: E₂ = 36/0.36 = 100 N/C pointing toward it, also along +x. They add: E = 600 N/C along +x. On the electron, |F| = eE = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ N, directed along −x because the electron is negative.

A force along +x forgets that sign; 400 N/C subtracts two fields that point the same way; 544 N/C uses 0.90 m as the distance to the second charge.

**10. B** — *Net force on an electron between two protons.* The electron is pulled toward both protons, which sit on opposite sides of it, so the two pulls subtract. Nearer proton at 1.0 nm: F₁ = ke²/r² = 2.304 × 10⁻²⁸/1.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.30 × 10⁻¹⁰ N. Farther proton at 3.0 nm: F₂ = 2.304 × 10⁻²⁸/9.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.56 × 10⁻¹¹ N, a ninth of F₁. Net = 2.05 × 10⁻¹⁰ N toward the nearer proton.

2.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ N adds two pulls that point opposite ways; 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ N ignores the farther proton; the last option has the direction backwards, since the nearer pull is the bigger one.

**11. C** — *Deriving the point-charge field from Coulomb's law.* Dividing the force by q₀ cancels the test charge: E = (kQq₀/r²)/q₀ = kQ/r², which is why the field depends only on the source and the distance. Numerically E = (9.0 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.30)² = 27/0.090 = 300 N/C, directed radially away from Q because Q is positive.

Leaving q₀ in the expression means the division was not done; 90 N/C forgets to square 0.30 m; 'toward Q' is the direction for a negative source.

**12. D** — *Sign of a charge versus direction of the force.* Coulomb's law returns a magnitude, F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = 0.0405 N, and a magnitude is never negative. A minus sign from multiplying signed charges records only that the signs are unlike, so the force is attractive; the formula has no idea where the charges sit, so it cannot say left or right. The direction is read off the positions: each charge is pulled along the line toward the other.

Unlike charges attract, not repel; 'to the left' reads a position out of a formula that contains none; treating F as signed confuses the sign of the charge product with a direction in space.

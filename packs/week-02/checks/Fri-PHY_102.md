## Answers

**1. A** — F = 9.0 × 10⁹ × (5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 0.135/0.09 = 1.5 N, attractive. Option 2 divides by 0.30 instead of 0.30², treating the law as inverse distance. Option 3 substitutes the signed charge −3.0 μC and reports a negative magnitude; the law takes |q₁q₂|, and the attraction is stated in words, not by a minus sign. Option 4 forgets to divide by r² at all, leaving the numerator 0.135.

**2. B** — F is proportional to 1/r², so tripling r divides the force by 3² = 9. This is the same arithmetic that turns Example 1's 16 × 10⁻¹¹ N into 4 × 10⁻¹¹ N when the distance doubles. Option 1 uses 1/r. Options 3 and 4 have the force growing with distance, which would make distant charges dominate every problem.

**3. C** — The electron sits between the two protons and is attracted to both, so the two forces point in opposite directions along the line and the vector sum is 16 − 4 = 12 × 10⁻¹¹ N, directed toward the nearer proton. Option 1 adds two opposing arrows. Option 2 uses only the nearer proton, ignoring the far one entirely. Option 4 uses only the far one. The deck computes both magnitudes and stops, so this last step has to come from you.

**4. D** — The separation is 7 − (−9) = 16 m, so F₃₁ = 9.0 × 10⁹ × (24 × 10⁻⁶)(18 × 10⁻⁶)/16² = 3.888/256 = 0.0152 N. Option 1 is F₂₁, the other force on Q₁. Option 2 divides by 16 rather than 16², the inverse-distance slip. Option 3 comes from computing the separation as 7 − 9 = 2 m, dropping the minus sign on x₁ — the single commonest error in this problem, and it inflates the answer sixty-fold.

**5. A** — Q₁ is negative and to the left, so it repels Q₂ rightward: 9.0 × 10⁹(18 × 10⁻⁶)(12 × 10⁻⁶)/10² = 0.0194 N along +x. Q₃ is positive and to the right, so it attracts Q₂ rightward: 9.0 × 10⁹(24 × 10⁻⁶)(12 × 10⁻⁶)/6² = 2.592/36 = 0.0720 N along +x. Both arrows agree, so the total is 0.0914 N along +x. Option 2 subtracts, copying part b's minus sign without redrawing the diagram. Option 3 reverses a correct magnitude. Option 4 drops the repulsion from Q₁.

**6. B** — E = F/q₀ = 2.0 × 10⁻³/5.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.0 × 10² = 400 N/C, the same move as her solved problem 1. Option 1 divides 2.0 by 5.0 without converting the prefixes, losing a factor of a thousand. Option 3 inverts the fraction, computing q₀/F. Option 4 has the right number with the unit of force; a field is newtons per coulomb, never newtons.

**7. C** — The separation is 0.800 − 0.200 = 0.600 m, so E₂ = 9.0 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹/(0.600)² = 45/0.360 = 125 N/C, pointing toward the negative charge, i.e. +x. Option 1 uses the charge's coordinate 0.800 m as if it were the distance. Option 4 uses the field point's coordinate 0.200 m instead. Option 2 is E₁, the contribution of the +2.00 nC charge. Adding E₁ and E₂ gives her 575 N/C.

**8. D** — |F| = |q|E = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.5 × 10³ = 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, and since the electron is negative the force is antiparallel to E, so along −x. Option 1 has the right size but keeps the field's direction, forgetting the sign of the charge. Option 2 divides E by the charge instead of multiplying. Option 3 quotes the field strength as if it were a force.

**9.** A good answer sets the separations first, r₁₂ = 1 − (−9) = 10 m and r₁₃ = 7 − (−9) = 16 m, then draws the arrows: Q₂ is negative and to the right so it repels Q₁ leftward; Q₃ is positive and to the right so it attracts Q₁ rightward. Then F₂₁ = 9.0 × 10⁹(18 × 10⁻⁶)(12 × 10⁻⁶)/10² = 1.944/100 = 0.0194 N and F₃₁ = 9.0 × 10⁹(24 × 10⁻⁶)(18 × 10⁻⁶)/16² = 3.888/256 = 0.0152 N. Taking +x as positive, ΣF = F₃₁ − F₂₁ = 0.0152 − 0.0194 = −0.0042 N, i.e. 4.2 × 10⁻³ N in the −x direction. Credit the reading of the minus sign as a direction rather than a negative magnitude, and the observation that the nearer 12 μC beats the larger 24 μC because distance enters squared.

**10.** A good answer draws Q₂'s free-body diagram first: Q₁ is negative and lies to the left of Q₂, so it repels Q₂ along +x; Q₃ is positive and lies to the right, so it attracts Q₂ along +x. Then F₁₂ = 9.0 × 10⁹(18 × 10⁻⁶)(12 × 10⁻⁶)/(10)² = 0.0194 N and F₃₂ = 9.0 × 10⁹(24 × 10⁻⁶)(12 × 10⁻⁶)/(6)² = 2.592/36 = 0.0720 N, both along +x, so ΣF = 0.0194 + 0.0720 = 0.0914 N in the +x direction. It should note that F₁₂ has the same magnitude as F₂₁ from part b — Newton's third law — but appears on the opposite free-body diagram pointing the opposite way.

**11.** A good answer gets the directions before the numbers: at x = 0.200 m the field of the positive charge points away from it, i.e. +x, and the field of the negative charge points toward it, also +x, so the two add. Then E₁ = 9.0 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹/(0.200)² = 18/0.0400 = 450 N/C and E₂ = 9.0 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹/(0.600)² = 45/0.360 = 125 N/C, giving E = 575 N/C along +x. For the electron, F = qE: |F| = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 575 = 9.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ N, directed along −x because the charge is negative. The deck stops at 575 N/C even though its own question asks for the force, so the last two lines must come from you. Note also that the second distance is 0.800 − 0.200 = 0.600 m, not 0.800 m.

**12.** A good answer uses |E| = k|Q|/r² with the magnitude of the charge: 9.0 × 10⁹ × 6.8 × 10⁻⁶ = 6.12 × 10⁴, divided by 7.4² = 54.76, giving 1.1 × 10³ N/C — and it must supply the direction the slide omits, radially inward, toward the charge, because the source is negative and field lines end on negative charges. At 3.7 m the distance is halved, so r² is quartered and the field is four times as large, about 4.5 × 10³ N/C, still radially inward. Credit explicitly the point that the minus sign of the charge is expressed as the inward direction and never as a negative field strength.

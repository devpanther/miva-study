## Answers

**1. a — Sets of commands the computer can follow, implemented in a particular order.** *Concept: the definition of an algorithm.* The course gives the definition twice — "sets of commands that the computer can follow to carry out calculations or other problem-solving tasks" and "instructions implemented in a particular order to carry out a task". (b) is a **computer program**, and it also contradicts the characteristic of **language independence**; (c) is visualisation from Week 3; (d) is data mining.

**2. b — Inputs, a process, and outputs.** *Concept: the input-process-output triple.* This is also the first characteristic: an algorithm should have **input values that will be processed to produce some output**. (a) lists development steps, (c) lists the representation forms, (d) lists the time-complexity cases.

**3. b.** *Concept: computers, programs and algorithms.* The three definitions are given as a set, and the two extra claims about algorithms — that they are **designed by people** and that they **must be converted into a computer program** — are the examinable part. (a) reverses the direction of authorship. (c) is the error the whole slide exists to prevent. (d) swaps plan and program.

**4. a.** *Concept: the five development steps.* Description, analysis, high-level development, refinement, review. (b) puts analysis before there is a description to analyse and refinement before there is anything to refine. (c) imports Week 3's computational methods. (d) delays analysis until after the algorithm has been written.

**5. d.** *Concept: the three Step 1 issues.* The three named issues are **unstated assumptions, ambiguity and incompleteness**, and they arise because two different parties are involved — the **client** finding a solution and the **programmer** providing it. "Can the complexity be reduced?" is one of the two **Step 5 review** questions.

**6. a — The starting point in problem analysis.** *Concept: Step 2's two sets of questions.* Starting point: what data we have, where it is, what formulas are related, what rules are required to manipulate the data, how the data values are related. Ending point (the answer to (b)): what results we expect, what parameters have changed in the items, what changes have been made, what items are now non-existent.

**7. b.** *Concept: why the high-level algorithm comes first.* Step 3 focuses on the **main parts of the solution and not on every minor detail**, because attending to every detail before there is even a solution is time-wasting and costly; the details are added at Step 4. (c) is false — language independence is a characteristic of algorithms at every level of detail.

**8. b.** *Concept: what refinement depends on.* Who implements it, how much knowledge they have, and the computer specifications — the three questions the course attaches to stepwise refinement. (a) and (d) are complexity material from Lesson II, and (c) is a matter of representation, not of detail.

**9. c — Repetition.** *Concept: the three forms of representation.* The course's stated reason is precisely that **writing the same step on different lines is not sustainable**, so a construct is needed that executes the same step repeatedly. (a) is selection, whose forms are `if ... then` and `if ... then ... else`; (b) is sequence, ordered so that **no step is skipped unless there is a compelling reason**; (d) is not one of the three.

**10. b.** *Concept: priori versus posterior analysis.* Priori is **before** implementation in a particular programming language and **does not directly affect the implementation**; (a) is the definition of **posterior** analysis. The discriminator is whether the code yet exists in a language, not whether the analysis is theoretical or measured.

**11. c — O(log n).** *Concept: the loop rules.* A loop variable **divided or multiplied by a constant amount** gives **O(log n)**; **incremented or decremented by a constant amount** gives O(n), which is (a)'s error. (b) carries the constant into the notation, which Big O never does — it describes the **growth rate**. (d) is the rule for a variable **reduced or increased exponentially** by a constant amount.

**12. b.** *Concept: characteristics versus design considerations.* The characteristics are **input, unambiguity, finiteness, effectiveness and language independence** — the conditions for being an algorithm at all. (a) is the list of **design considerations**, the qualities of a well-designed algorithm, and is the single most-swapped pair this week. (c) lists representation forms, (d) complexity vocabulary.

**13.** *Concept: the algorithm development process.* **Step 1 — Problem description.** Obtain a description of the problem; harder than it looks because **two different parties** are involved, the **client (finding a solution)** and the **programmer (providing the solution)**. Its three named issues: **descriptions relying on unstated assumptions; ambiguous descriptions; incomplete descriptions.** **Step 2 — Problem analysis.** Analyse the described problem so that the **starting and ending points of the problem-solving process are clear**. *Starting-point questions:* what data do we have, where is the data, what formulas are related to the problem, what rules are required to manipulate the data, how are the data values related. *Ending-point questions:* what results are we expecting, what parameters have changed in the items, what changes have been made, what items are now non-existent. **Step 3 — High-level algorithm development.** Develop an algorithm focused on the **main parts of the solution and not on every minor detail**, because it is **time-wasting and costly to focus on every minor detail before at least coming up with a solution**; the course's example is the five-step meeting-scheduling algorithm. **Step 4 — Algorithm refinement.** Provide more detail on each high-level step (**stepwise refinement**), the amount depending on **who will implement the algorithm, how much knowledge that person has, and what the computer specifications are**. **Step 5 — Algorithm review.** Confirm that the algorithm **solves the problem**, asking **can the algorithm only solve a particular problem or be applied to more general problems**, and **can the complexity of the algorithm be reduced**.

**14.** *Concept: tracing a counter-controlled loop, and locating the three representation forms in it.*

```
1  sum   <- 0
2  count <- 0
3  Enter n
4  sum   <- sum + n
5  count <- count + 1
6  If count < 5 then go to step 3
7  Print sum
8  Stop
```

**Trace on 6, 1, 8, 4, 5, 2.** Initialisation: `sum = 0, count = 0`. Pass 1, `n = 6`: `sum = 6, count = 1`, `1 < 5` true → back to step 3. Pass 2, `n = 1`: `sum = 7, count = 2`, true → loop. Pass 3, `n = 8`: `sum = 15, count = 3`, true → loop. Pass 4, `n = 4`: `sum = 19, count = 4`, true → loop. Pass 5, `n = 5`: `sum = 24, count = 5`, and `5 < 5` is **false** → step 7. **Printed output: 24.** **Five values are read** — 6, 1, 8, 4 and 5; the final 2 is never entered, because step 3 is only reached again when step 6 sends control back to it. The order of steps 5 and 6 is what fixes this: the counter is incremented **before** the decision, so the pass that reads the fifth number is the last. **Sequence:** steps 1–5 and steps 7–8, performed in order with no step skipped. **Selection:** step 6, the decision `count < 5`, in the course's `if condition is true then perform activity` form. **Repetition:** steps 3–6 taken together, since the branch at step 6 returns control to step 3 and causes the same steps to be executed repeatedly.

**15.** *Concept: algorithm complexity, its cases, and growth rate as the basis of comparison.* The **complexity or performance of an algorithm is analysed in terms of time complexity and space complexity**. **Time complexity** deals with the **time required by an algorithm to complete its execution**; **space complexity** deals with the **amount of computer space required by an algorithm to complete its execution**. Both are represented by notations such as **Big O**. Time complexity is stated in three cases, each for an **input of size n**: **best case**, a measure of the **minimum** time the algorithm will require for an input of size n; **worst case**, a measure of the **maximum** time it will require for an input of size n; **average case**, the time it will require to execute a **typical** input of size n. Because a computing problem generally has various solutions, in the shape of **different algorithms with different logic**, we normally want to compare them to see which is more efficient, and the course states that **the most important factor to consider is the growth rate of both algorithms** — **Big O is useful because it describes the growth rate**, and provides the basis for computing and measuring the efficiency of a specific algorithm. The course's example is **1000N versus N²**: at N = 10 the first costs 10,000 and the second 100, so the quadratic looks far better; they meet at **N = 1000** with 1,000,000 each; and beyond that N² grows without limit relative to 1000N — so timing an algorithm on small inputs, or buying faster hardware, changes only the constant, never the growth rate. **The growth-rate names, slowest to fastest: C constant, Log N logarithmic, Log² N log-squared, N linear, N Log N, N² quadratic, N³ cubic, 2^N exponential.** **The rules:** a function or set of statements is **O(1)** if it contains **no loop, no recursion and no call to any other non-constant-time function**; a loop is **O(n)** if its loop variable is **incremented or decremented by a constant amount**; a loop is **O(log n)** if its loop variable is **divided or multiplied by a constant amount** (and **O(log log n)** if the variable is reduced or increased **exponentially** by a constant amount); **nested loops** are **O(n^c)**, the complexity being **equal to the number of times the innermost statement is executed**; and algorithms running in **O(2ⁿ)** are often recursive algorithms that solve a problem of size n by recursively solving **two smaller problems of size n − 1**.

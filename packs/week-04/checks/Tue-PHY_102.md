## Answers

**1. c** — *A zeroed source must be replaced by the element that imposes the same constraint it does: zero volts across it is a wire, zero amps through it is a gap.* Setting a voltage source to zero means forcing zero potential difference between its terminals, and the lesson makes the consequence explicit: with a dead source, both of its sides sit at the same potential, so it "behaves electrically as if it were shorted by a connecting wire". Setting a current source to zero means forcing zero current through its branch, which is exactly an **open circuit** — the lesson calls the two cases "in a sense complementary". (a) is the inversion, and it is the single most damaging error in superposition: opening a voltage-source branch removes a conducting path that is really still there, and shorting a current-source branch adds one that is not. (b) treats "zeroing" as "deleting", forgetting that a dead voltage source still leaves a wire behind that other currents can flow through. (d) applies the voltage-source rule to both sources; a current source with zero output does *not* have zero volts across it — it has zero current through it, and only an open circuit guarantees that.

**2. d** — *The two ideal sources are duals: zero resistance in series for the voltage source, infinite resistance in parallel for the current source.* An ideal voltage source holds its voltage whatever current is drawn, which requires no internal drop at all, so r = 0 in series. An ideal current source holds its current whatever voltage appears, which requires that none of its output be diverted through an internal path, so r = ∞ in parallel. A real current source is an ideal source **in parallel** with a finite r, so as the load voltage rises more current leaks through r and less reaches the load. (a) collapses the distinction into a labelling choice; if both had r = 0, a current source connected to a short would have to supply infinite power. (b) swaps the two models wholesale, and also misstates the real voltage source: as the load resistance rises, the current *falls*, and the terminal voltage rises towards ε. (c) puts the internal resistance of a current source in **series**, which would make a large r simply a bigger obstacle in the load path; in the correct parallel model, a *large* r is what makes the source *better*, not worse.

**3. b** — *p = qd, a vector from −q to +q, unit C·m, dimension [M⁰L¹T¹I¹].* The magnitude is the product of **either** charge with the separation, and the physics convention runs from the negative charge to the positive one; chemistry uses the opposite sense, which is why the convention must be named. The dimension follows directly: charge = current × time = [I¹T¹], times length [L¹], giving [M⁰L¹T¹I¹]. (a) is the trap laid by the torque derivation τ = 2qaE sin θ: there the separation is written 2a, so d = 2a and p = q(2a) — the factor 2 is already inside d and must not be applied twice. (c) makes p a scalar; the whole content of the dipole concept is that it is a vector with a direction fixed by the charges themselves, before any external field exists. (d) reverses the physics direction (the chemistry convention quoted as though it were physics) and divides by the distance instead of multiplying, which shows up in both the unit and the T⁻¹.

**4. a** — *In a uniform field the two forces cancel as vectors but not as a couple; τ = p × E, magnitude pE sin θ, and a couple's moment is origin-independent precisely because the net force is zero.* Each charge feels a force of magnitude qE, one along E and one against it, so the resultant force is zero. Because the charges sit at different points, the two antiparallel forces form a couple whose moment is (force) × (perpendicular arm) = qE × 2a sin θ = pE sin θ. The torque acts to reduce θ, so the dipole aligns with E and the torque falls to zero when p ∥ E. (b) is the commonest half-truth on this topic: it is right that the forces cancel, but it forgets that forces cancelling in magnitude at *different points of application* still produce a couple. (c) invents a net force in a uniform field — a net force appears only if E is **non-uniform** — and swaps sin for cos, which would wrongly make the torque maximal in the aligned position. (d) has the right formula but the wrong general principle: for a couple, the moment is the same about every origin, and it is exactly the vanishing of the net force that guarantees this — so no origin has to be specified.

**5.** *Concept: potential is a scalar sum, so equal and opposite contributions cancel; the field is a vector sum, and the surviving components add rather than cancel.* On the equatorial plane every point is **equidistant** from +q and −q. The potential contributed by each charge is (1/4πε₀)q/r, a **scalar** carrying the sign of the charge, so the two contributions are equal in size and opposite in sign and cancel exactly: V = 0, at every distance on that plane. The dipole formula agrees — V = (1/4πε₀)(p cos θ)/r² with θ = 90° gives cos 90° = 0, hence V = 0. The **field**, by contrast, is a vector: the two contributions E₊q and E₋q are equal in magnitude but point in different directions, and when they are added by the parallelogram law (resolving into components along and perpendicular to the axis) the components perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel while the components **along the axis add**. The resultant is therefore non-zero and points **opposite to p** (from +q back towards −q). For comparison, at θ = 0° the same formula gives its maximum, V = (1/4πε₀)p/r². The general lesson: V = 0 at a point means no *net* work per coulomb to bring a charge there, not the absence of a field — E is the *gradient* of V, and a scalar can pass through zero while its rate of change across the surface does not.

**6.** *Concept: assigning one potential per node builds KVL into the labelling, so only charge conservation is left to impose; and the reference node fixes an arbitrary zero of potential, which cancels out of every physical answer.* KVL says that the potential differences round any closed loop sum to zero — equivalently, that the potential of a point does not depend on the path taken to reach it. The moment you give each node a **single number** V₁, V₂, … and write every branch voltage as a *difference* of two of these numbers, that path-independence is already built into the notation: any loop sum automatically telescopes to zero, (V₁ − V₂) + (V₂ − V₃) + (V₃ − V₁) = 0, whatever the values. So KVL can never give you new information, and the only physical law left to impose is **KCL**, charge conservation at each node — which is why the recipe is: pick a reference, assign node voltages, apply KCL at each non-reference node, solve the simultaneous equations. The reference node is arbitrary because only **differences** of potential are physical: adding the same constant to every node voltage changes no branch current and no measurable voltage, so any node may be declared "zero". The advice to take the node joined to the **negative terminal of the source** is a convenience, not a law — it makes the source's own terminal voltage appear directly as a known node voltage (positive), which removes one unknown and keeps the signs in the KCL equations tidy.

## Most likely to be examined

1. **The four components of a good paragraph**, named exactly: **Unity, Coherence, Topic sentence, One idea at a time**. The source stresses that across the literature the one constant component is the **topic sentence**.
2. **Unity vs Coherence** — Unity = a single central theme, all sentences relating to it, no digression. Coherence = ideas ordered logically and linked by cohesive links (conjunctions, pronouns) so they flow smoothly. Test: a paragraph can be *unified* (all about one topic) yet *incoherent* (sentences jumbled, no links).
3. **Topic sentence vs supporting sentences.** The topic sentence states the main idea/argument and is usually at the **beginning** (sometimes in the middle or at the end). Every other sentence is a **supporting sentence / supporting detail**. Rule to quote: *five paragraphs = five topic sentences.*
4. **"One idea at a time"** — a point introduced must be finished inside that paragraph; it is not carried over. Advantages in one paragraph, disadvantages in a separate one.
5. **Transition words by category** — Addition, Contrast, Comparison, Cause and Effect, Emphasis, Sequence/Order, Illustration/Example, Conclusion/Summary, Time, Clarification. Be able to sort a given word into its column (e.g. *However* = Contrast, *Consequently* = Cause and Effect, *Namely* = Illustration/Example and Clarification).
6. **The four functions of punctuation** given in the deck: **Changing the Meaning, Emphasising a Point, Conveying Nuance, Clarifying Ambiguity** — with the set-piece examples "Let's eat, Grandma." vs "Let's eat Grandma." and "I like cooking, my family, and pets." vs "I like cooking my family and pets."
7. **Semicolon vs colon vs comma.** Semicolon joins two closely related **independent clauses** without a coordinating conjunction, and separates list items that already contain commas. Colon **introduces** a list, explanation or quotation and follows a complete statement. Comma joins independent clauses only *with* a coordinating conjunction. Test: could a full stop stand there? If yes and the halves are related → semicolon; if the second half delivers what the first announced → colon.
8. **Hyphen vs en dash vs em dash**, and **parentheses vs brackets**. Hyphen joins compound words and compound adjectives before a noun; en dash marks a range; em dash sets off or emphasises information in place of parentheses or commas. Parentheses enclose *your own* extra information; brackets enclose *your* additions inside *someone else's* quoted words.

## Paragraphs (deck 1)

**Paragraph** — an individual unit of thought within a written composition; in an essay, a discrete segment of discourse that maintains independence while contributing to the collective meaning of the whole essay through its connection to other paragraphs. Its fundamental purpose is to uphold the **structural integrity** of the essay. (Deck analogy: the paragraph is the *filling of the burger* — it carries the juicy details.)

**Components of a good paragraph:** Unity · Coherence · Topic sentence · One idea at a time.

- **Unity** — a singular central theme, with all sentences relating to that theme so the paragraph fulfils its purpose; avoiding digression is essential to a coordinated, focused essay.
  *Example:* "Lagos traffic wastes commuters' time. Many workers leave home by 4 a.m. Some spend four hours daily in transit." (Adding "My uncle drives a Toyota" breaks unity.)
- **Coherence** — all parts, lexical and syntactic, contribute meaningfully; ideas are orderly and logically connected by **cohesive links like conjunctions and pronouns**, preventing monotony and ambiguity.
  *Incoherent (source example):* "In the morning, I woke up early to go for a run. The sun was shining brightly in the sky. My favourite breakfast is pancakes with maple syrup. After my run, I went to the park to read a book. I love spending time outdoors." Each sentence makes sense alone but they do not connect.
  *Coherent (revised):* run → "casting a warm glow on the neighbourhood" → "After my energising run" → breakfast → "With a satisfied appetite and a clear mind" → the park. Logical sequence, unified narrative.
- **Topic sentence** — the sentence stating the main idea or central point, giving a clear, concise preview of what the paragraph will discuss; it acts as a **roadmap**, setting the tone and direction. Usually first, sometimes mid-paragraph or at the end. Five paragraphs in an essay require five topic sentences.
  *Example:* "Group study improves retention in three ways." (Everything after it must deliver those three ways.)
- **Supporting sentences / supporting details** — since the topic sentence is only one sentence, the remaining sentences support it or add detail.
  *Source specimen ("Body Positivity"):* topic sentence = "The concept of body positivity, encouraging self-acceptance and diverse body appreciation, has become a subject of controversy in recent times." Everything from "Advocates argue…" onward = supporting sentences.
- **One idea at a time** — a paragraph focuses on one idea exclusively and any point introduced is fully discussed there; transitioning to a new paragraph must not continue a point. All advantages in one paragraph, all disadvantages in another.

**Transition words** named on the slide: Additionally, Furthermore, Moreover, However, Nevertheless, On the contrary.

**Full transition table (memorise the column names):**

| Category | Examples given |

## Punctuation (deck 2)

**Punctuation** — a crucial element of writing that helps convey meaning, clarify sentences, and guide the reader through the text. Its correct use in professional and academic writing underscores credibility and enhances overall quality.

**Four uses of punctuation (the source's own headings):**
1. **Changing the Meaning** — "Let's eat, Grandma." (inviting Grandma to a meal) vs "Let's eat Grandma." (a gruesome alternative).
2. **Emphasising a Point** — "We won the game!" (excitement) vs "We won the game." (bare fact).
3. **Conveying Nuance** — "I appreciate your help." vs "I appreciate your help!"
4. **Clarifying Ambiguity** — "I like cooking, my family, and pets." (three separate things) vs "I like cooking my family and pets."
Classroom provocation: *"A woman without her man is nothing."* — repunctuate it and the meaning reverses.

**The marks, with rule + a right/wrong pair:**

- **Period (.)** — ends declarative sentences and statements; also used in abbreviations (Dr., Mr., etc.).
  Right: "The lecture ends at noon." Wrong mark: "The lecture ends at noon?" turns a statement into doubt about the timetable.
- **Comma (,)** — separates items in a list; sets off introductory phrases and clauses; separates adjectives when they modify the same noun; separates independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or); sets off non-essential or parenthetical information.
  Right: "After the exam, Ada, who had studied all night, slept." Wrong mark: "After the exam Ada who had studied all night slept" loses the non-essential clause, so the reader misreads it as identifying *which* Ada.
- **Semicolon (;)** — connects closely related independent clauses **without** a coordinating conjunction; separates items in a list when the items themselves contain commas; balances sentence structure and improves flow.
  Right: "The results were clear; the hypothesis still failed." / "We visited Kano, Nigeria; Accra, Ghana; and Dakar, Senegal."
  Wrong mark: "We visited Kano, Nigeria, Accra, Ghana, and Dakar, Senegal" reads as six places instead of three.
  Source examples: "The experiment yielded significant results; however, further analysis is required before drawing conclusions."; "The conference will include speakers from London, UK; Berlin, Germany; and Paris, France."; "Her argument was compelling; it was based on extensive research, sound reasoning, and practical examples."
- **Colon (:)** — introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation; used to emphasise or introduce a key point.
  Right: "The kit needs three items: gloves, goggles, and a mask." Wrong mark: "The kit needs three items; gloves, goggles, and a mask" wrongly presents the second half as an independent clause, which it is not.
  Source examples: "The research project requires various elements: a detailed literature review, extensive data analysis, and comprehensive interviews."; "The key to success is simple: hard work, dedication, and a relentless pursuit of knowledge."; Shakespeare's famous line reads: "To be or not to be, that is the question."
- **Apostrophe (')** — indicates possession ("John's car"); used in contractions ("it's" for "it is", "don't" for "do not"); used in plural possession ("The students' essays were well-written").
  Right: "It's a beautiful day." / "The cat's tail was fluffy." Wrong mark: "Its a beautiful day" drops the contraction; conversely "The dog wagged it's tail" means "it is tail" — nonsense.
- **Quotation Marks (" ") (' ')** — enclose direct speech or dialogue; set off titles of shorter works such as articles, short stories or chapters; can convey sarcasm or irony.
  Right: "She said, 'I'll be there by 2 PM,' and then hung up the phone." / His so-called "expertise" in the field was questionable. Wrong: dropping the marks in the second turns an ironic jab into a sincere compliment.
- **Question Mark (?)** — ends direct questions; used in rhetorical questions to prompt thought or emphasise a point; can indicate doubt or uncertainty ("The results of the study were unexpected?").
  Right: "What is the purpose of this experiment?" Wrong mark: "What is the purpose of this experiment." reads as a statement, not a question.
- **Exclamation Point (!)** — indicates strong emotion, enthusiasm, excitement, urgency, surprise, or outrage; **use sparingly in formal writing**.
  Right: "Hurry up! The concert is about to begin!" Wrong mark: "Hurry up. The concert is about to begin." loses the urgency — and in reverse, "The sample was contaminated!" in a lab report reads as unprofessional.
- **Hyphen (-)** — joins compound words ("mother-in-law"); connects words in a compound adjective before a noun ("well-known author"); used when breaking a word at the end of a line; indicates number ranges ("April 5-7").
  Right: "a well-known author" Wrong: "a well known author" — the reader briefly takes *well* as modifying *known author*.
- **Dash (— or -)** — sets off or emphasises information within a sentence. The **em dash (—)** is longer and often replaces parentheses or commas, and can create a dramatic pause; the **en dash (-)** is shorter and indicates a range ("pages 10-15").
  Right: "The team—except for a few members who were on leave—worked diligently to meet the project deadline." Wrong mark: hyphens there ("The team-except for a few members-worked…") read as compound-word joins, not an interruption.
- **Ellipsis (…)** — indicates omitted words or a trailing-off thought; used in quotations when part of the text is omitted; conveys a pause or uncertainty.
  Right: "The original text said, 'To be or not to… that is the question.'" Wrong mark: a full stop instead ("To be or not to. that is the question") signals a completed sentence and hides the omission.
- **Parentheses ( )** — enclose supplementary or explanatory information; set off references, clarifications, or non-essential details; clarify acronyms or abbreviations; used for in-text citations.
  Right: "The World Health Organization (WHO) issued new guidelines." / "According to Smith (2019), the impact of climate change is severe." Wrong mark: brackets there — "According to Smith [2019]" — falsely signals an editorial insertion into quoted text.
- **Brackets [ ]** — used within quotations to indicate added or changed words for clarity; enclose editorial comments or explanations within quoted text; in mathematics, used for grouping and indicating intervals.
  Right: "The original text read, 'The city [London] is a vibrant metropolis.'" Wrong mark: "'The city (London) is a vibrant metropolis'" claims the original author actually wrote *London* — a misquotation.
- **Slash / Forward Slash (/)** — indicates alternatives or choices ("red/green"); represents dates or ratios ("10/15/2023"); separates directories or paths in URLs.
  Right: "You can choose red/green for the background colour." Wrong mark: a hyphen — "red-green" — names one blended colour rather than two options.
- **Ampersand (&)** — represents "and", often in abbreviations ("R&D — Research & Development"), company names ("Johnson & Johnson"), and certain citation styles for multiple authors ("Smith, J., & Brown, A. (2021)").
  Right: "Johnson & Johnson is a pharmaceutical company." Wrong: "Johnson and Johnson expanded R and D" — the ampersand belongs to the registered name and to the abbreviation.

## Commonly confused

- **Unity vs Coherence** — Unity asks *is every sentence about the same theme?*; Coherence asks *are the sentences in a logical order and linked?* A paragraph can pass one and fail the other (see the "morning run" example: unified topic, broken flow).
- **Topic sentence vs supporting sentence** — the topic sentence can stand alone as a summary of the whole paragraph; a supporting sentence leaves the reader asking "supporting what?"
- **Semicolon vs comma** — a semicolon needs an independent clause on both sides; a comma alone between two independent clauses is wrong unless a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or) follows it.
- **Semicolon vs colon** — semicolon = two balanced, related complete statements; colon = the first part announces, the second part delivers (list, explanation, quotation).
- **Colon vs comma before a list** — use the colon only when what precedes it is a complete statement ("requires various elements: …").
- **Hyphen vs en dash vs em dash** — hyphen joins words into one unit; en dash spans a range (10-15); em dash interrupts the sentence to set off or emphasise.
- **Parentheses vs brackets** — parentheses = the writer's own aside; brackets = an insertion into *quoted* material (or maths grouping).
- **Its vs It's** — apostrophe only for the contraction of *it is*; possessive *its* takes none.
- **Question mark for a real question vs for doubt** — "The results were unexpected?" is not a question to the reader; it flags the writer's uncertainty.
- **Same word, two columns** — *Consequently* appears under both Cause and Effect and Conclusion/Summary; *Namely*, *Specifically* and *In particular* appear across Illustration/Example, Emphasis and Clarification. Decide by the job the word does in the sentence, not by a memorised column.

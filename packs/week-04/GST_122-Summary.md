## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. A paragraph needs four things at once: **unity** (one central theme), **coherence** (logically linked sentences), a **topic sentence**, and **one idea at a time** — a new idea always means a new paragraph.
2. The **topic sentence** states the paragraph's main idea; it usually opens the paragraph but can also sit mid-paragraph or at the end. Rule of thumb: one topic sentence per paragraph — 5 paragraphs need 5 topic sentences.
3. Every other sentence in the paragraph is a **supporting sentence** — it exists to add detail, evidence, or argument to the topic sentence, never the reverse.
4. Transition words are grouped by the job they do (addition, contrast, comparison, cause/effect, sequence, illustration, conclusion, time, clarification) — picking a word from the wrong group breaks coherence even when the grammar is fine.
5. This week's Summary deck actually covers a third topic: **writing strategies** — persuasion, narration, description, exposition, comparison, contrast, cause and effect, problem-solution, definition, chronological order, and process analysis — each suited to a different communication purpose and audience, plus the role of tone, style, and revision.
6. Punctuation is rule-governed, not stylistic: a **comma** joins two independent clauses only together with a coordinating conjunction; a **semicolon** joins them without one; a **colon** introduces a list, explanation, or quotation.
7. The **apostrophe** does two unrelated jobs — possession (student's / students') and contraction (it's, don't) — and mixing them up is the single most common punctuation error.
8. Hyphens join compound words/adjectives before a noun ("well-known author"); dashes (em/en) set off emphasis or show a range — the source uses the same short mark for both hyphenated ranges and en-dash ranges, so don't over-read the glyph, read the job it's doing.

## Paragraphs and topic sentences

- **Unity** — one central theme; every sentence in the paragraph relates to it; avoid digression.
- **Coherence** — ideas connect logically via cohesive links (conjunctions, pronouns, transitions), preventing monotony and ambiguity. *Incoherent:* "I woke up early to run. The sun was shining. My favourite breakfast is pancakes." *Coherent:* "I woke up early to run. After my energising run, I made my favourite breakfast: pancakes."
- **One idea at a time** — a paragraph fully develops a single idea; a new point (e.g., moving from advantages to disadvantages) starts a new paragraph.
- **Topic sentence** — states the paragraph's main idea and previews its content; acts as a roadmap for the reader; most often the first sentence, but can appear in the middle or at the end.
- **Supporting sentences** — elaborate, evidence, or exemplify the topic sentence.
- **Worked example** ("Body Positivity" paragraph): sentence 1 ("The concept of body positivity... has become a subject of controversy") is the topic sentence; the four sentences after it (advocates' arguments, critics' claims, exclusion concerns, ongoing debates) are supporting sentences.
- **Transition words by function** — Addition: *furthermore, moreover, in addition*. Contrast: *however, nevertheless, on the contrary*. Comparison: *similarly, likewise, just as*. Cause/effect: *therefore, consequently, as a result*. Emphasis: *indeed, certainly, particularly*. Sequence: *first, then, finally*. Illustration: *for example, for instance, namely*. Conclusion: *in conclusion, to sum up, overall*. Clarification: *in other words, that is to say*.

## Writing strategies (the Summary deck's actual topic)

- Writing strategies are compared to programming languages: **persuasion, narration, description, exposition, comparison, contrast, cause and effect, problem-solution, definition, chronological order, process analysis** — each has its own "syntax and rules" and suits a different communication goal.
- Choosing the right strategy depends on **purpose, audience, and desired outcome** — not habit.
- **Tone and style** must match the audience; mismatched tone/style is what most often loses engagement, not weak content.
- **Editing and revision** are a distinct, essential stage — for clarity, coherence, and overall quality — not just a final proofread.
- Sentence *function* (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory — from Week 3) is named again here as a contributor to effective written and spoken communication, tying structure to strategy.
- **Case study takeaway** (marketing team): running writing-strategy workshops, applying strategies deliberately to campaign materials, then gathering feedback and iterating is what improved team communication — strategy choice is treated as a skill to practise, not a one-off decision.

## Writing mechanics: punctuation

- **Period (.)** — ends a declarative sentence/statement; also punctuates abbreviations. *"Dr. Bello submitted the report on time."*
- **Comma (,)** — separates list items; sets off an introductory phrase/clause; separates coordinate adjectives modifying the same noun; joins two independent clauses **only with** a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or...); sets off non-essential/parenthetical information. *"After the meeting ended, the manager approved the budget, and the team celebrated."*
- **Semicolon (;)** — connects two closely related independent clauses **without** a coordinating conjunction; separates list items that themselves contain commas. *"The experiment yielded significant results; further analysis is required."* / *"Speakers came from London, UK; Berlin, Germany; and Paris, France."*
- **Colon (:)** — introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation; introduces or emphasises a key point. *"The key to success is simple: hard work, dedication, and persistence."*
- **Apostrophe (')** — marks possession (singular: add 's — "the cat's tail"; plural ending in s: add only ' — "the students' essays"); marks a contraction ("it's" = it is, "don't" = do not). *"It's clear the students' essays were well written."*
- **Quotation marks (" ")** — enclose direct speech/dialogue; set off titles of short works (articles, chapters); can flag sarcasm/irony. *"She said, 'The deadline is Friday.'"*
- **Question mark (?)** — ends a direct question; can flag doubt in a statement written as a question. *"Why should we settle for less?"*
- **Exclamation point (!)** — marks strong emotion or emphasis; use sparingly in formal writing. *"Congratulations on your promotion!"*
- **Hyphen (-)** — joins compound words ("mother-in-law") and compound adjectives before a noun ("well-known author"); breaks a word at a line end.
- **Dash (— em / – en)** — em dash sets off or emphasises information (can replace parentheses/commas); en dash indicates a range. *"The results—though unexpected—confirmed the hypothesis (pages 10–15)."*
- **Ellipsis (...)** — shows omitted words in a quotation, or a trailing-off thought. *"The report noted, 'Sales rose sharply... before declining in Q4.'"*
- **Parentheses ( )** — enclose supplementary/explanatory information, references, or citations. *"The World Health Organization (WHO) issued new guidelines."*
- **Brackets [ ]** — add or change a word inside a quotation for clarity, or enclose an editorial comment within quoted text. *"The report stated, 'The city [Lagos] recorded record rainfall.'"*
- **Slash (/)** — shows alternatives; represents dates/ratios; separates parts of a URL path. *"Choose the red/blue cover design."*
- **Ampersand (&)** — stands for "and" in abbreviations and company names. *"The R&D report was filed by Johnson & Johnson."*

## Commonly confused

- **Comma splice vs correct joining** — a comma alone cannot join two independent clauses. *Wrong:* "The results were significant, the team celebrated." *Right:* "...significant, so the team celebrated" (comma + conjunction) or "...significant; the team celebrated" (semicolon).
- **Semicolon vs colon** — semicolon joins two independent, equally-weighted clauses with no conjunction; colon introduces something that explains, lists, or quotes what came before, and what follows it need not be a full clause. *Semicolon:* "The room was silent; everyone awaited the verdict." *Colon:* "The room held one thing: silence."
- **its vs it's** — "its" is the possessive pronoun (no apostrophe, like "his"/"hers"); "it's" is always the contraction of "it is" or "it has." *"It's clear the committee changed its decision."*
- **Apostrophe in plurals** — a plain plural never takes an apostrophe ("essays," not "essay's"); where the apostrophe sits marks *possession* and depends on whether the possessor is singular ("the student's essay" = one student) or plural ending in s ("the students' essays" = many students).

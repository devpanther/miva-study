# GST_122 — Week 4 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 4 (28 Sep–4 Oct 2026) · Topic: Paragraphs, Topic Sentences and Writing Mechanics (Punctuation)*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. A **paragraph is a single unit of thought** — one idea per paragraph, fully discussed before you move on. If an essay has five paragraphs it has **five topic sentences**.
2. The **topic sentence** is the sentence carrying the paragraph's main idea. It is the one component every source agrees on. It is **usually at the beginning**, but may also fall **in the middle ("in the models") or at the end**.
3. The named components of a good paragraph: **unity, coherence, topic sentence, one idea at a time**. Add **adequate development** (enough supporting detail) as the standard fourth quality.
4. **Unity** = one central theme, every sentence relating to it, **no digression**. **Coherence** = ideas ordered logically and linked by **cohesive links** (conjunctions, pronouns, transitions) so the paragraph flows.
5. All sentences after the topic sentence are **supporting sentences / supporting details** — they explain, exemplify or give evidence for it.
6. **Transitional words** signal the relationship between sentences: additionally, furthermore, moreover, however, nevertheless, on the contrary. Know the categories: addition, contrast, comparison, cause and effect, emphasis, sequence, illustration, conclusion, time, clarification.
7. **Punctuation changes meaning, not just appearance.** The lesson's set pieces: *"Let's eat, Grandma."* vs *"Let's eat Grandma."*; *"I like cooking, my family, and pets."* vs *"I like cooking my family and pets."*; and *"A woman without her man is nothing."* (repunctuate it and the meaning reverses).
8. The four functions of punctuation as the lesson names them: **changing the meaning, emphasising a point, conveying nuance, clarifying ambiguity.**

## Paragraph structure — the model, fully worked

**Body Positivity**

> **[Topic sentence]** The concept of body positivity, encouraging self-acceptance and diverse body appreciation, has become a subject of controversy in recent times.
> **[Supporting detail 1]** Advocates argue that body positivity challenges societal norms, combating body shaming and unrealistic beauty standards.
> **[Supporting detail 2]** Critics claim that the movement may unintentionally normalise unhealthy lifestyles, fostering the idea that all body sizes are equally healthy.
> **[Supporting detail 3]** Debates surround concerns about potential exclusions within body positivity, particularly regarding individuals with disabilities or those facing health challenges.
> **[Concluding sentence]** Ongoing discussions centre on the complex impact of the body positivity movement on mental health, societal perceptions, and overall well-being.

- **Topic sentence** — announces the controversy; acts as a roadmap so the reader can anticipate what follows.
- **Supporting sentences** — advocates, critics, exclusions: each develops the same controversy, none digresses. That is **unity**.
- **Concluding sentence** — closes the unit without opening a new idea.
- **Position options for the topic sentence:** beginning (most common, sets the tone), middle, or end (as a summing-up). Exam wording from the slides: "often found at the beginning of a paragraph. Sometimes in the models or at the end of each paragraph."

**Incoherent vs coherent (the lesson's own pair):** "In the morning, I woke up early to go for a run. The sun was shining brightly in the sky. My favourite breakfast is pancakes with maple syrup. After my run, I went to the park to read a book." — every sentence is grammatical, but nothing links them. The revised version adds sequence and connectives ("casting a warm glow", "After my energising run, I returned home and prepared…", "With a satisfied appetite and a clear mind, I headed to the nearby park…"), producing a unified narrative. **Coherence is a matter of links and order, not of grammar.**

## Punctuation — rule, then right vs wrong

- **Period (.)** — ends declarative sentences and statements; also used in abbreviations (Dr., Mr., etc.). ✓ *The report is finished.* ✗ *The report is finished*
- **Comma (,)** — separates items in a list; sets off introductory phrases and clauses; separates adjectives modifying the same noun; joins two independent clauses **when a coordinating conjunction is present** (and, but, or); sets off non-essential/parenthetical information. ✓ *After the meeting, we revised the budget, the schedule, and the staffing plan.* ✗ *After the meeting we revised the budget the schedule and the staffing plan.*
- **Semicolon (;)** — connects closely related independent clauses **without** a coordinating conjunction; separates list items that themselves contain commas. ✓ *The experiment yielded significant results; however, further analysis is required.* ✓ *Speakers will come from London, UK; Berlin, Germany; and Paris, France.* ✗ *The experiment yielded significant results, however further analysis is required.*
- **Colon (:)** — introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation; emphasises a key point. The clause before the colon must be complete. ✓ *The key to success is simple: hard work, dedication, and a relentless pursuit of knowledge.* ✗ *The project requires: a literature review, data analysis, and interviews.*
- **Apostrophe (')** — possession (*John's car*, *the cat's tail*), plural possession (*the students' essays*), and contractions (*it's* = it is, *don't* = do not). ✓ *The students' essays were well-written.* ✗ *The students essay's were well-written.*
- **Quotation marks (" ") (' ')** — enclose direct speech; set off titles of short works (articles, short stories, chapters); mark sarcastic or ironic use. ✓ *She said, "I'll be there by 2 PM," and then hung up.* ✗ *She said I'll be there by 2 PM and then hung up.*
- **Question mark (?)** — ends a direct question; also rhetorical questions and expressions of doubt. ✓ *What is the purpose of this experiment?* ✗ *She asked what the purpose of the experiment was?* (that is an indirect question — use a period).
- **Exclamation point (!)** — strong emotion, urgency, surprise, outrage. **Use sparingly in formal writing.** ✓ *Congratulations on your outstanding achievement!* ✗ *The results were statistically significant!*
- **Hyphen (-)** — joins compound words (*mother-in-law*); joins a compound adjective **before** a noun (*well-known author*, *state-of-the-art technology*); breaks a word at the end of a line; marks number ranges (*April 5-7*). ✓ *a well-known author* ✗ *a well known author* (and note: *The author is well known* takes no hyphen, because the compound follows the noun).
- **Dash (— / –)** — the **em dash (—)** sets off or emphasises information inside a sentence, replacing parentheses or commas, or creates a dramatic pause; the **en dash (–)** indicates a range (*pages 10–15*). ✓ *The team—except for a few members on leave—worked diligently to meet the deadline.* ✗ *The team - except for a few members on leave, worked diligently.*
- **Ellipsis (…)** — marks omitted words in a quotation, or a trailing-off thought/pause. ✓ *The original text said, "To be or not to… that is the question."* ✗ *I'm not sure....... maybe.*
- **Parentheses ( )** — enclose supplementary or explanatory information, glosses of acronyms, and in-text citations. ✓ *The World Health Organization (WHO) issued new guidelines.* ✓ *According to Smith (2019), the impact of climate change is severe.* ✗ *The results, (which were surprising) were published.*
- **Brackets [ ]** — added or changed words **inside a quotation**, and editorial comments; also mathematical grouping. ✓ *The original text read, "The city [London] is a vibrant metropolis."* ✗ *He said he looked forward to (the) challenge.*
- **Slash (/)** — alternatives or choices, dates and ratios, and directories in URLs. ✓ *You can choose red/green for the background colour.* ✗ *He asked whether we would attend / or not.*
- **Ampersand (&)** — stands for "and" in abbreviations (*R&D*), company names (*Johnson & Johnson*), and some citation styles (*Smith, J., & Brown, A. (2021)*). ✓ *Research & Development* ✗ *We finished the report & sent it to the client.* (not a substitute for "and" in running prose).

## Commonly confused

- **Comma splice vs correct compound sentence** — a comma alone cannot join two independent clauses. ✗ *The results were significant, further analysis is required.* Four fixes: comma **+ coordinating conjunction** (*…significant, but further analysis is required*); **semicolon** (*…significant; further analysis is required*); **full stop**; or subordination (*Although the results were significant, further analysis is required*). Note that *however, therefore, consequently* are **not** coordinating conjunctions — they need a semicolon before them.
- **Colon vs semicolon** — a colon **introduces** what follows (a list, an explanation, a quotation) and needs a complete clause before it; a semicolon **balances** two things of equal weight (two independent clauses, or comma-heavy list items). *The key is simple: hard work.* vs *Her argument was compelling; it rested on extensive research.*
- **its vs it's** — *it's* is only ever the contraction of "it is" (or "it has"); *its* is the possessive and takes **no apostrophe**. ✓ *It's a beautiful day.* ✓ *The company revised its policy.* ✗ *The company revised it's policy.* The apostrophe rule for nouns (*John's car*) does not extend to possessive pronouns.
- **Hyphen vs dash** — the hyphen is a **word-level** joiner (compound words, compound adjectives before nouns, ranges of numbers); the em dash is a **sentence-level** separator that sets off or emphasises a whole element. The en dash sits between them and marks ranges. Length order: hyphen - , en dash – , em dash —.
- **Restrictive vs non-restrictive commas** — non-essential (parenthetical) information is fenced by commas; essential information is not. *The students, who had studied all night, passed* means all of them studied and all passed; *The students who had studied all night passed* means only that subgroup passed. The commas change the meaning of the sentence, which is exactly the lesson's point about punctuation and ambiguity.
- **Apostrophe for plural vs possession** — apostrophes never make a plural. ✗ *The book's are on the table.* ✓ *The books are on the table.* ✓ *The book's cover is torn.*
- **Unity vs coherence** — unity is about **what** is in the paragraph (one theme, nothing off-topic); coherence is about **how it is arranged and linked** (logical order plus connectives). A paragraph can be unified but incoherent, as the "morning run" example shows.

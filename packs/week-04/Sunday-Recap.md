## Fallback topic 1 — PHY_102: What 'zeroing' a source means in superposition: a dead voltage source becomes a short circuit, a zeroed current source becomes an open circuit

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** There are at least four layers of confusion stacked on this one step, and they reinforce each other. The first is verbal: 'zeroed', 'dead' and 'removed' sound like synonyms, so the instinct is to delete the source and leave a gap - which is right for a current source and catastrophically wrong for a voltage source. The second is that the student is thinking about the SOURCE rather than about the CONSTRAINT it imposes: a voltage source is defined by the potential difference it holds across its terminals, so zeroing it means forcing that difference to zero, and the element that guarantees zero volts is a plain wire; a current source is defined by the current it pushes through its branch, so zeroing it means forcing that current to zero, and the element that guarantees zero amps is a break. The third layer is that the two ideal-source models are duals which are routinely half-remembered - r = 0 IN SERIES for the ideal voltage source, r = infinite IN PARALLEL for the ideal current source - and once the series/parallel halves are swapped, the zeroing rules come out swapped too, since replacing a source by its ideal internal resistance is exactly what the rules amount to. The fourth is that the error is silent: the wrongly zeroed circuit is still a perfectly solvable circuit, so the student gets a clean, confident, wrong number with no arithmetic warning at all, and the same wrong instinct then propagates into Thevenin, where R_Th is computed with the sources zeroed. On top of all this sits the separate and equally common belief that if currents superpose then powers must too, which is false because P = I² R is quadratic - the cross term is missing.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why does a superposition calculation require you to zero the other sources at all, instead of just leaving them in the circuit?
2. Why is a dead voltage source replaced by a wire rather than by a gap, when 'dead' sounds like it should mean 'not there'?
3. Why is the rule for a zeroed CURRENT source the opposite one - an open circuit - and in what sense are the two rules complementary?
4. Why do the zeroing rules follow automatically from the ideal internal resistances (r = 0 in series for a voltage source, r = infinite in parallel for a current source)?
5. Why do the branch currents superpose but the branch POWERS not, and what exactly is the term that the naive sum of powers leaves out?

**What a good answer contains:**

- Line 1: it must state both rules and tie each to the constraint the source imposes - a voltage source fixes the potential difference across its terminals, so zeroing it means forcing zero volts, which a wire (short circuit) guarantees; a current source fixes the current in its branch, so zeroing it means forcing zero amps, which a break (open circuit) guarantees - and it must name the duality with the ideal internal resistances, r = 0 in series versus r = infinite in parallel.
- Line 2: it must state the superposition principle itself in the lesson's own two halves (the current in any branch is the sum of the currents produced in that branch by each independent source acting alone with all the others zeroed; the voltage at any node is the sum of the voltages produced there the same way) and name the restriction to LINEAR elements, which is also what Thevenin and Norton require.
- Line 3: it must show what goes wrong concretely - a worked pair of numbers where the reversed rule gives a plausible but wrong answer - and it must say that powers do not superpose because P = I² R is quadratic, so the currents are added first and squared afterwards.

**Worked example.** A 6 V source in series with 2 Ω and an ideal 3.0 A current source both feed a common node; a 6 Ω load runs from that node to the common rail. Find the load current by superposition. Step 1, the 6 V source acting alone: the CURRENT source is zeroed, so it is replaced by an OPEN circuit; the 6 V source then drives the 2 Ω and the 6 Ω in series, giving I = 6/(2 + 6) = 0.75 A through the load, and a node voltage of 0.75 × 6 = 4.5 V. Step 2, the 3.0 A source acting alone: the VOLTAGE source is zeroed, so it is replaced by a SHORT circuit, which puts the 2 Ω directly from the node to the rail, in parallel with the 6 Ω load; the 3.0 A divides between them in inverse proportion to their resistances, so the load takes 3.0 × 2/(2 + 6) = 0.75 A, again with a node voltage of 4.5 V. Step 3, add the contributions: I_load = 0.75 + 0.75 = 1.50 A, and the node sits at 4.5 + 4.5 = 9.0 V. Nodal check: (V - 6)/2 + V/6 = 3.0, so 3V - 18 + V = 18, 4V = 36, V = 9.0 V, and I_load = 9.0/6 = 1.50 A - the two methods agree. Now do it with the rules reversed, to see the damage: opening the voltage source leaves the 3.0 A with nowhere to go but the load, giving 18 V at the node, while shorting the current source ties the node to the rail and gives 0 V from the voltage source - a total of 18 V and 3.0 A, twice the correct current and twice the correct node voltage, with nothing in the arithmetic to signal the error. Finally, the power in the load is I² R = (1.50)² (6) = 13.5 W, whereas adding the separate powers gives (0.75)² (6) + (0.75)² (6) = 3.375 + 3.375 = 6.75 W - exactly half, because the cross term 2 × 0.75 × 0.75 × 6 = 6.75 W has been thrown away. Superpose currents, then square.

## Fallback topic 2 — MTH_102: Reading a graph: the limit at a versus the function value f(a), and the classification of the resulting discontinuity

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** Almost every graphical-limit error the student will make this semester traces back to one collapse: treating 'the limit at a' and 'the value at a' as the same question. On a plot the two are read from different objects - the limit from the two branches sliding toward the vertical line x = a, the value from the single filled dot sitting on that line - and the definition of a limit deliberately excludes x = a itself, so the dot is the one point the limit never inspects. Week 3 established this algebraically, where the student could shelter behind the mechanical instruction 'cancel and substitute'; graphically there is no formula to hide behind and the confusion surfaces immediately. It shows up in three recognisable ways. First, the student reads the filled dot and reports it as the limit, which is right only when f happens to be continuous at a and wrong in exactly the cases the topic exists to discuss. Second, the student sees that the dot disagrees with the branches and concludes that the limit does not exist, when in fact the limit exists perfectly well and it is continuity that has failed - that is the whole definition of a removable discontinuity. Third, the student sees that f(a) is undefined, with an open circle and no dot at all, and again concludes that there is no limit, which is the same error wearing different clothes. The confusion then propagates into classification, because the four discontinuity types are distinguished by what the one-sided limits do, not by how damaged the picture looks: a hole and a jump and an asymptote can all sit at a point where f is undefined, yet only the hole is repairable. Matters get worse once ∞ appears, since a student who is already treating the limit as 'the height at a' will happily declare that two matching +∞ branches satisfy the existence test, forgetting that the test requires two finite numbers and that '= +∞' is a description of failure. The cure is procedural: on every graph question answer four separate questions in order - left limit, right limit, two-sided limit, then f(a) - and only afterwards ask the single continuity question, which is whether the third answer and the fourth answer are the same number.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why does the value f(a) play no part in determining lim as x → a of f(x)?
2. Why can a function be undefined at a and still have a perfectly good limit there, as (x² - 1)/(x - 1) does at x = 1?
3. Why is a mismatch between the limit and f(a) called a removable discontinuity rather than a failure of the limit?
4. Why is a jump discontinuity not repairable by redefining f at the single point a, when a hole is?
5. Why do two matching one-sided limits of +∞ fail the existence test, when two matching one-sided limits of 5 pass it?

**What a good answer contains:**

- The limit is read from the two branches approaching the line x = a and never from the dot on that line, because the definition inspects only x with 0 < |x - a| < δ, so f(a) may be missing, or present and different, without affecting it. Continuity is the extra demand that f(a) exists, that the limit exists as a finite number, and that the two are equal; the classification of any break is then decided by the one-sided limits alone - equal and finite means removable, unequal and finite means jump, at least one infinite means an infinite discontinuity with a vertical asymptote, and a one-sided limit that does not exist at all means essential or oscillatory. Only the removable case can be repaired by redefining f at one point, because it is the only case in which the branches already agree on a finite height, and this is also why '= +∞' never satisfies the existence test: it is shorthand for unbounded growth, not a number that the branches settle on.

**Worked example.** Worked example. Let f(x) = (x² - 9)/(x - 3) for x not equal to 3, with f(3) = 2. Step 1, simplify away from the point: for every x not equal to 3, (x² - 9)/(x - 3) = (x - 3)(x + 3)/(x - 3) = x + 3, so off x = 3 the graph is the straight line y = x + 3. Step 2, left limit: sliding in from the left along that line, the height approaches 3 + 3 = 6, so lim as x → 3- of f(x) = 6. Step 3, right limit: sliding in from the right along the same line, lim as x → 3+ of f(x) = 6. Step 4, two-sided limit: both are finite and equal, so lim as x → 3 of f(x) = 6, and the graph carries an open circle at (3, 6). Step 5, the value: the second rule supplies f(3) = 2, drawn as a lone filled dot at (3, 2), three units below the line. Step 6, continuity: f(3) is defined and the limit exists, but 6 is not equal to 2, so the third condition fails and f is discontinuous at 3. Step 7, classify and repair: since the limit exists as a finite number, the discontinuity is removable; redefining f(3) := 6 moves the dot onto the line, fills the hole and makes f continuous at 3. Contrast: if the rule had instead been f(x) = 2x for x ≥ 3, then the right limit would be 2(3) = 6 and f(3) = 6 as well, so all three conditions would hold and f would be continuous at 3 despite the corner, which affects differentiability only. Contrast again: if the rule had been f(x) = x + 10 for x > 3, the right limit would be 13 while the left limit stayed 6, giving a jump of size 7 that no choice of f(3) can repair.

## Reserve topic (if both fallbacks are already used) — COS_102: The characteristics of an algorithm versus the design considerations for one, and why 'is this an algorithm at all?' is a different question from 'is this a good algorithm?'

Both lists arrive on adjacent slides, both are bullet lists of abstract nouns, and several items sound interchangeable, so students memorise twelve words and lose which list each belongs to. The damage is worse than a naming slip. The characteristics - input, unambiguity, finiteness, effectiveness and language independence - are entry conditions: fail one and what has been written is simply not an algorithm, because there is no defined output, or no bound on the work, or no shared reading of a step, or no way to convert the plan into some other programming language. The design considerations - modularity, correctness, maintainability, functionality, robustness, simplicity and extensibility - are quality judgements about something that already is an algorithm; fail one and you have a genuine but poor algorithm. Two specific confusions recur. First, students put correctness among the characteristics, which quietly implies a wrong algorithm is not an algorithm - but a procedure can be finite, unambiguous, language independent and produce output, and still compute the wrong answer, which is why correctness is listed as a design consideration and why Step 5 review exists. Second, students invert language independence, reading it as 'must be independent of the computer' or, worse, as a requirement that the algorithm be written in a programming language - the exact opposite of what the course says, and it clashes with the course's other claim that algorithms are designed by people and must be converted into a computer program. Robustness is a third snare, because the ordinary English sense (copes with bad input) is not the course's, which defines robust narrowly as the solution to the problem being clearly defined. Because Week 4 is examined on precise wording, mixing the lists costs marks even when the underlying understanding is sound.

**Probe with:** Name the five characteristics of an algorithm and the seven design considerations, and say which list each of 'finiteness' and 'correctness' belongs to. → Why is correctness a design consideration rather than a characteristic - can something be an algorithm and still give the wrong answer? → A procedure is written in Java-specific syntax. Which characteristic does it fail, and why does the course insist on the opposite of what most students first assume? → A procedure repeats 'adjust the value until the output looks reasonable'. Which characteristic does it fail, and would a second implementer necessarily produce the same result on the same input? → A recommendation algorithm is finite, unambiguous, language independent and produces output, but nobody on the team can extend or modify it and it silently returns an empty list for new users. Which requirements does it satisfy, which does it fail, and what does that tell you about the difference between the two lists?

**A good answer contains:** The five characteristics stated in the course's words - input values processed to produce some output; straightforward and clear instructions (unambiguity); a limited and countable number of instructions (finiteness); effectiveness; and not dependent on any programming language (language independence) - alongside the seven design considerations: modularity (breakable into smaller steps), correctness, maintainability (easily refactored), functionality (steps logical and practical), robustness (the solution to the problem clearly defined), simplicity (very easy to understand) and extensibility (another designer can update and upgrade it). The stated principle that the characteristics are entry conditions - failing one means the thing is not an algorithm - while the design considerations are quality judgements about something that already is one, which is why correctness sits in the second list and finiteness in the first. The link back to the course's other definitions: an algorithm is a plan for solving a problem, designed by people, which must be converted into a computer program, so language independence is a requirement to stay above any one language, not to be written in one; and an algorithm consists of inputs, a process and outputs, which is the input characteristic restated.

**Worked example.** Take this in-house procedure: 'def rank(users): for u in users: score = sum(w * s for w, s in u.pairs) ; print(u.id, score)' with a comment saying 'run it again with different weights until the ordering looks about right'. Judge it against both lists. Characteristics: it has input (the users) but produces no output value - it prints, and printing progress is not the processed result the definition asks for, so the input-process-output triple is broken. It is written in Python-specific syntax, so it fails language independence: the plan cannot be handed to a team working in Java without being re-derived, which contradicts the course's claim that an algorithm is a people-designed plan converted into a program. The instruction 'until the ordering looks about right' fails unambiguity - two implementers will stop at different weights on the same data - and because that stopping test is not decidable, the enclosing 'run it again' loop also fails finiteness, since the number of instructions executed is not limited and countable. Verdict: this is not yet an algorithm, and no amount of tidying the code changes that; the description has to be repaired first, which is Step 1 and Step 4 work, not coding. Now repair it: '1 read the list of users and the weight vector w; 2 best <- empty list; 3 for each user u in the list: 4 score <- sum over pairs of w times s; 5 append (u.id, score) to best; 6 sort best by score, descending; 7 return best.' It now has input and returns an output, every step is executable identically by any reader, the loop runs exactly once per user so the instruction count is bounded, and nothing in it names a language. It is an algorithm. Only now do the design considerations bite, and they are separate questions: is it correct - does the weighted sum actually rank the way the business wants? Is it modular - steps 3 to 5 could be a named sub-step reused elsewhere. Is it maintainable and extensible - can another designer add a new signal without rewriting step 4? Is it robust in the course's narrow sense - is the solution clearly defined for a new user with no pairs, or is that case simply undefined? Is it simple enough to be very easy to understand? And what is its complexity - step 3's loop increments by a constant amount over n users, giving O(n) for the scoring, while step 6's sort dominates, so a priori analysis compares candidate designs by growth rate before any of this is implemented in a particular language. The order matters: characteristics first, because they decide whether there is anything to evaluate, and design considerations second, because they decide whether what you have is worth shipping.

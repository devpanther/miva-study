# GST_122 — Week 9 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 9 (2 – 8 Nov 2026) · Topic: Responding to Speaking Prompts (Guidelines)*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Speaking prompt** = "**questions or statements designed to elicit a spoken response from you**." The deck's own gloss: they **serve as a stimulus to initiate and guide verbal communication**, and are **commonly used in interviews, discussions, language learning, and public speaking**. Their nature **varies widely, from open-ended questions to more complex scenarios**, requiring you to **articulate thoughts, share opinions, or provide information verbally**. Learn the definition word-for-word — "elicit a spoken response" is the phrase that gets tested.
2. **The 13 guidelines, in the deck's own order:** **Understand the Prompt/Question → Organise Your Thoughts → Introduction → Address the Key Points → Provide Examples and Evidence → Speak Clearly and Concisely → Elaborate and Expand → Stay Relevant → Balance → Conclude Effectively → Adapt to the Context → Practice Active Listening → Manage Time Effectively.** Expect to be given a scenario and asked which guideline it illustrates, or asked to name them in order.
3. **The order is itself examinable.** The first three are all *before you speak* — understand, organise, then open with a concise introduction. **Understanding comes before organising; organising comes before the introduction.** Anything that puts drafting or speaking ahead of comprehension is wrong.
4. **The 9 stated importances of speaking prompts:** **Communication Skill Development · Critical Thinking · Expressing Ideas Clearly · Language Proficiency · Interviews and Assessments · Promoting Active Participation · Building Confidence · Cultural and Social Awareness · Enhancing Listening Skills.** Note that **Enhancing Listening Skills** is on the list — speaking prompts train *listening* too, because interactive settings require you to attend to others' contributions.
5. **Elaborate and Expand vs Speak Clearly and Concisely** is the deck's own deliberate tension: be concise and avoid jargon, **yet "strive to provide sufficient detail to fully address the prompt"**. The resolution the deck gives is **"succinct yet comprehensive"** — conciseness is about *jargon, pace and enunciation*, not about saying less than the prompt requires.
6. **Balance** = "maintain a balanced perspective, **considering different angles or counter arguments if applicable**." Stated payoff: it **demonstrates a nuanced understanding of the topic**. This is the guideline the three model answers most visibly perform (benefits *and* risks, opportunities *and* challenges).
7. **Adapt to the Context** — adjust the response to the setting; the deck's own example is that **responses in an academic setting may require a more formal tone than those in a casual conversation**. Paired with the objective of adapting to **job interviews, academic assessments, and group discussions**.
8. **The model-answer shape the deck extracts from all three samples:** every response followed a **structured format — a clear introduction, comprehensive analysis, and thoughtful conclusion**, addressing complexity through **in-depth exploration, examples, and the inclusion of potential solutions or recommendations**, while showing **critical thinking, nuanced understanding, and the ability to articulate complex ideas effectively**. And the closing line: **practice is key** — regularly engage in **speaking exercises, mock interviews, or discussions**.

## The 13 guidelines — what each one actually requires

| # | Guideline | What the deck says to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Understand the Prompt/Question** | Carefully **read or listen** to the prompt to ensure clear understanding; **identify key components and any specific instructions** provided |
| 2 | **Organise Your Thoughts** | Take a moment before responding; create a **mental outline or jot down key points** for a coherent, structured response |
| 3 | **Introduction** | Open with a **concise introduction** giving context or outlining the main points you intend to discuss; it **sets the tone** |
| 4 | **Address the Key Points** | Respond **directly to the main components**; cover the **core elements** with relevant information or examples |
| 5 | **Provide Examples and Evidence** | Support points with **specific examples or evidence**; concrete examples **illustrate ideas and make the response more convincing** |
| 6 | **Speak Clearly and Concisely** | Articulate clearly; **avoid unnecessary jargon or overly complex language**; be **mindful of pace and enunciation** |
| 7 | **Elaborate and Expand** | While concise, give **sufficient detail to fully address the prompt**; elaborate on key points for a **thorough and insightful** response |
| 8 | **Stay Relevant** | Keep focused on the topic; **avoid veering off into unrelated tangents**, which **dilutes the impact** of the answer |
| 9 | **Balance** | Maintain a balanced perspective, **considering different angles or counter arguments if applicable**; shows **nuanced understanding** |
| 10 | **Conclude Effectively** | A **brief conclusion summarising your main points** that **reinforces the key message** you want to convey |
| 11 | **Adapt to the Context** | Adjust to the setting — an **academic setting may require a more formal tone** than casual conversation |
| 12 | **Practice Active Listening** | If the prompt sits inside a conversation, **actively listen to follow-up questions or comments** so you can adapt and stay relevant to the ongoing exchange |
| 13 | **Manage Time Effectively** | Under time constraints, be mindful of duration; practise **succinct yet comprehensive answers within the allotted time** |

Grouping that makes them memorable: **1–2 prepare**, **3 opens**, **4–9 build the body**, **10 closes**, **11–13 govern delivery in the room**.

## The 9 importances — the stated reason for each

- **Communication Skill Development** — opportunities to **practise and enhance verbal communication**; responding helps you **articulate thoughts coherently and express ideas effectively**.
- **Critical Thinking** — thoughtful prompts require you to **analyse information, consider different perspectives, and provide well-reasoned responses**.
- **Expressing Ideas Clearly** — prompts push you to express ideas **clearly and concisely**, which is crucial in **professional settings, academic discussions, and everyday conversations**.
- **Language Proficiency** — for **language learners**, prompts give practice in **vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation in a contextually relevant manner**.
- **Interviews and Assessments** — in **job interviews, academic assessments, or standardised tests**, prompts evaluate the ability to **communicate effectively, think on your feet, and present yourself coherently**.
- **Promoting Active Participation** — prompts encourage participation in **discussions and group settings**, fostering **engagement and collaboration**.
- **Building Confidence** — regular engagement develops **confidence in verbal communication**; practice makes you **more comfortable expressing yourself in diverse settings**.
- **Cultural and Social Awareness** — some prompts touch on **cultural or social issues**, developing **cultural competence and sensitivity**.
- **Enhancing Listening Skills** — in interactive settings you must **actively listen to others' contributions**, which improves listening and the ability to hold **meaningful conversations**.

**Learning objectives stated:** think critically and express thoughts clearly in response to prompts, fostering **effective communication skills**; practise and apply **vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation** to enhance **language proficiency**; build **confidence in verbal expression** and adapt responses to diverse contexts — **job interviews, academic assessments, and group discussions**.

## The week's own summary sheet: stress and sentence stress

The Week 9 summary sheet carries a **different topic from the slide deck** — it is headed **"Topic: Speaking"** and covers **stress**. Learn it too; either can be examined.

- **Stress in linguistics** = "the **emphasis on a particular syllable or segment within a word, phrase, or sentence during speech**."
- **Word stress** — demonstrated with the word **"demonstrate"**, showing how emphasis on **different syllables alters pronunciation and meaning**.
- **Sentence stress** — the impact of stress on the **overall meaning, rhythm, and pronunciation** of sentences. Stressing **different words in the same sentence alters its meaning**.
- **The seven-way sentence:** **"I didn't say you should bring coffee"** — the deck stresses each of **"I," "didn't," "say," "you," "should," "bring," "coffee"** in turn. Each shift moves the denial onto a different element: *I* (someone else did) · *didn't* (flat denial) · *say* (I implied/wrote it, but did not say it) · *you* (someone else should) · *should* (not an obligation) · *bring* (send it, or make it, but not bring) · *coffee* (something else, not coffee).
- **Practical example:** **"I can't believe you're going to the MOVIES again!"** — stress on **movies** conveys **surprise or disapproval**.
- **Case study — "The Power of Stress: Deconstructing a Sentence."** *Objective:* demonstrate the **transformative impact of sentence stress on the interpretation and intent** of a sentence. *Methodology:* analyse "I didn't say you should bring coffee" **with varying stress patterns**, deconstructing it by emphasising different words to **observe shifts in meaning**. *Conclusion:* stress is **dynamic** and can **influence the listener's perception and understanding of the speaker's message**.
- **Skills and competencies listed:** **Listening Acuity, Effective Communication, Cultural Sensitivity, Adaptability, Interpretation Skills, Conscious Speaking.**

## Commonly confused

- **Speaking prompt vs speaking topic** — a prompt is a **question or statement designed to elicit a spoken response** and to **guide** it; a topic is only the subject matter. The prompt carries the **specific instructions** ("provide specific examples", "propose frameworks") that guideline 1 tells you to identify.
- **Understand the Prompt vs Address the Key Points** — understanding is the **first, pre-speech** step (read/listen, identify key components and instructions); addressing key points happens **in the body**, responding directly to the main components. The first is comprehension, the second is coverage.
- **Organise Your Thoughts vs Introduction** — organising is **internal and silent** (a mental outline or jotted key points); the introduction is the **first thing you say**, giving context or outlining what you will discuss. Do not report your outline as your introduction.
- **Speak Clearly and Concisely vs Elaborate and Expand** — conciseness targets **jargon, overly complex language, pace and enunciation**; elaboration demands **sufficient detail to fully address the prompt**. They are not in conflict: cut the padding, not the substance. The deck's phrase for the union is **"succinct yet comprehensive"**.
- **Stay Relevant vs Balance** — relevance forbids **unrelated tangents**; balance requires **different angles or counter arguments**. A counter-argument on the set topic is balance, not a tangent; an interesting story about something else is a tangent even if you argue both sides of it.
- **Provide Examples and Evidence vs Elaborate and Expand** — examples are **specific and concrete**, supplied to **make the response convincing**; elaboration is **depth on your key points** to make it **thorough and insightful**. One supplies proof, the other supplies development.
- **Conclude Effectively vs Introduction** — the introduction **sets the tone** and previews; the conclusion is **brief**, **summarises your main points** and **reinforces the key message**. A conclusion that adds a new argument has failed the guideline.
- **Adapt to the Context vs Practice Active Listening** — adapting is calibrating **register and tone to the setting** (formal for academic, looser in casual conversation); active listening is tracking **follow-up questions and comments in an ongoing exchange** so you can adjust content. Setting versus interlocutor.
- **Manage Time Effectively vs Speak Clearly and Concisely** — time management is about the **duration of the response under a constraint** and practising within the **allotted time**; concision is about **language choice and delivery** whether or not a clock is running.
- **Word stress vs sentence stress** — word stress falls on a **syllable within a word** (*demonstrate*), changing **pronunciation and meaning**; sentence stress falls on a **word within the sentence** (*I didn't say you should bring coffee*), changing **the meaning, rhythm and pronunciation of the whole utterance**.

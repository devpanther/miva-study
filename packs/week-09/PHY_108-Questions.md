## Multiple choice

**1.** The specific heat capacity of a material is:
A. the heat energy required to raise the temperature of the whole sample by one degree Celsius, in J/°C
B. the heat needed to raise unit mass of the material by one degree Celsius, in J/kg·°C
C. the heat energy required to change the state of unit mass of the material without a temperature change, in J/kg
D. the temperature rise produced in unit mass of the material by one joule of heat, in °C/J

**2.** The heat needed to raise a mass m of a substance from T_i to T_f is:
A. Q = mc(T_f + T_i)
B. Q = c(T_f − T_i)/m
C. Q = mc(T_f − T_i)
D. Q = m(T_f − T_i)/c

**3.** In the mixture method, the principle of conservation of energy is applied in the form:
A. m_s c_s (T_s − T_f) = (m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r)
B. m_s c_s (T_s − T_r) = (m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r)
C. m_s c_s (T_f − T_s) = (m_w c_w − m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r)
D. m_s c_s (T_s − T_f) = m_w c_w (T_s − T_r)

**4.** For the copper run, m_w = 68.63 g, m_c = 36.36 g, c_w = 4186 J/kg·°C and c_c = 387 J/kg·°C. The total heat capacity of the water plus the calorimeter is:
A. 287 J/°C
B. 14.1 J/°C
C. 4573 J/kg·°C
D. 301 J/°C

**5.** With those values and m_s = 8.9 g, T_r = 16.1 °C, T_s = 91.3 °C and T_f = 17.0 °C, the specific heat of the solid is:
A. 271 J/kg·°C

## Short answer

**13.** Define the specific heat capacity of a material and give its SI unit. State the principle used in the mixture method, write the full working equation with every symbol defined, and say precisely what the **insulating jacket**, the **crucible tongs** and the **tare button on the balance** are each there for.

**14.** A copper block of mass m_s = 8.9 g is heated to T_s = 91.3 °C and dropped into a copper calorimeter of mass m_c = 36.36 g containing m_w = 68.63 g of water, both at T_r = 16.1 °C. The mixture settles at T_f = 17.0 °C. Taking c_w = 4186 J/kg·°C and c_c = 387 J/kg·°C, calculate the heat capacity of the water plus calorimeter, the heat gained, and hence the specific heat of the solid. Then state what value would have been obtained had the calorimeter been ignored, and comment on the accuracy of the result.

**15.** Set out the procedure of this experiment in the correct order, from weighing the block to the download of the Excel sheet. Then give three precautions and three sources of error, saying in each case which quantity in the working equation the precaution protects, or in which direction the error pushes c_s.

## Answers

**1. B** — *definition and SI unit*: specific heat capacity is the heat required **per unit mass** for a rise of **one degree**, so its unit carries both a kilogram and a degree: **J/kg·°C** (= J/kg·K). Option A defines the **heat capacity** of the object, mc, whose unit is J/°C — it depends on how much material you have, so it is not a property of the material. Option C defines **specific latent heat**, which is about a change of state at constant temperature and has unit J/kg. Option D inverts the definition; the reciprocal of a specific heat is not a quantity anyone tabulates.

**2. C** — *the heat equation*: Q = mc∆T with ∆T = T_f − T_i, so that Q comes out **positive when the body gains heat and its temperature rises** and negative when it cools. Option A adds the temperatures instead of subtracting them, which would make Q depend on where the zero of the scale sits. Options B and D divide by the wrong quantity: doubling the mass must double the heat needed, and a material with a **larger** c must need **more** heat, not less.

**3. A** — *conservation of energy for the mixture*: heat lost by the solid as it falls from T_s to T_f equals heat gained by the water **and** the calorimeter as they rise together from T_r to T_f. Option B uses (T_s − T_r) on the left — but the solid stops falling at T_f, not at room temperature. Option C reverses the solid's difference, making the left side negative, and **subtracts** the calorimeter's heat capacity instead of adding it; the calorimeter absorbs heat, it does not give any back. Option D drops the calorimeter term entirely *and* gives the water the solid's temperature drop, so both sides are wrong.

**4. D** — *heat capacity of the mixture, in J/°C*: m_w c_w = 0.06863 × 4186 = 287.3 J/°C and m_c c_c = 0.03636 × 387 = 14.1 J/°C, and the two add to **301 J/°C**. Option A is the **water alone** — exactly the omission tested in question 6. Option B is the **calorimeter alone**, the small term, easily mistaken for negligible. Option C adds the two **specific heats** (4186 + 387) instead of the two heat capacities, and the unit gives it away: a heat capacity is J/°C, not J/kg·°C.

**5. C** — *the mixture-method result*: heat gained = 301.36 × (17.0 − 16.1) = 301.36 × 0.9 = **271.2 J**; the solid's side is m_s(T_s − T_f) = 0.0089 × 74.3 = **0.6613 kg·°C**; so c_s = 271.2/0.6613 = **410 J/kg·°C**, close to copper's accepted 387. Option A quotes the **heat in joules** as though it were the specific heat — the single most common slip, and detectable from the unit alone. Option B is what you get by **omitting the calorimeter term** (287.3 × 0.9/0.6613 = 391). Option D is the specific heat of **water**, which was given in the question, not asked for.

**6. A** — *why the calorimeter term matters*: the vessel warms from T_r to T_f along with the water and absorbs real energy, m_c c_c(T_f − T_r) = 12.7 J here. Leaving it out understates the numerator of c_s = Q_gained/[m_s(T_s − T_f)], so the computed c_s falls — from 410 to **391 J/kg·°C**, a 5 % error from one dropped term. Option B has the direction backwards. Option C is simply false: the calorimeter is in thermal contact with the water and reaches the same T_f. Option D confuses a coincidence of this particular set-up (block and vessel both copper) with a reason, and still gets the direction wrong.

**7. B** — *what the jacket is for*: the working equation says the solid's heat goes **entirely** into the water and calorimeter. Any leakage to the room is a missing term, and the jacket and cover are what suppress it, keeping T_f as high as the energy balance demands. Option A names a real minor benefit — the cover does reduce evaporation — but m_w is measured before the mixing and evaporation is not the reason for insulating. Option C is a mechanical convenience, not the physics. Option D is not a thermal argument at all; the block is only at about 95 °C.

**8. D** — *why and when to tare*: the calorimeter is weighed **first** and recorded as m_c; the balance is then zeroed so that when water is run in from the reservoir the reading is **m_w alone**. Option A would give a single combined reading with no way to separate m_c from m_w. Option B is nonsense in this sequence — the block was weighed at the very start, before it went into the beaker. Option C invents a calibration step the procedure does not contain. Had the tare been skipped, m_w would have been recorded as 105 g instead of 68.6 g and c_s would have come out around 617 J/kg·°C.

**9. C** — *heat lost in transfer*: the equation credits the water with all the heat the block had at T_s. Heat shed to the air on the way is subtracted from the water's share but still counted in (T_s − T_f), so the denominator is too big and **c_s comes out too low**. Option A reverses the flow — the block at ~95 °C is far hotter than the air and can only lose heat. Option B inverts the point: a little water carried over from the beaker is itself a source of error, and dryness is not what the tongs achieve. Option D is a hazard invented from nothing; the water in the calorimeter is at room temperature and is not being heated.

**10. D** — *where the uncertainty lives*: 8.9 g of metal with a specific heat about a tenth of water's is warming 68.6 g of water, so a 74 °C fall for the solid buys only a 0.9 °C rise for the mixture. Read to ±0.05 °C, that rise is uncertain by roughly 5–10 %, and since c_s is directly proportional to (T_f − T_r) the same fraction appears in the answer — the **dominant** error, and the reason for using as large a block and as little water as practicable. Option A is a fabricated rule; equilibrium is judged by the reading becoming steady, not by its size. Option B is false — Q = mc∆T has no minimum ∆T. Option C does not follow: a small rise is exactly what this mass ratio predicts even with a correctly heated block.

**11. A** — *linearising the mixture equation*: the working equation is Q_gained = c_s · m_s(T_s − T_f), which is y = mx with **m = c_s** and **no intercept**, so the plot is a straight line through the origin of gradient **c_s in J/kg·°C**. Option B names the wrong constant — (m_w c_w + m_c c_c) is the gradient of a plot of Q against ∆T of the mixture, a different graph. Option C is the Boyle's-law isotherm from last week and has no place here; nothing in this relation is inversely proportional. Option D confuses gradient with intercept — the intercept should be **zero**, and a non-zero one is the signature of heat lost in transfer or through the jacket.

**12. B** — *units*: specific heat capacity is heat per unit mass per degree, **J/kg·°C**; because a Celsius **interval** and a kelvin interval are the same size, J/kg·°C and J/kg·K are numerically identical; and dividing by 1000 g per kg, water's 4186 J/kg·°C is 4.186 J/g·°C. Option A gives the unit of **heat capacity** (J/°C), which belongs to an object rather than a material. Option C uses the non-SI gram-based unit *and* attaches the kilogram-based number to it, an error of 1000. Option D confuses a temperature **interval** with an **absolute** temperature: the 273.15 offset applies when converting a temperature, never when converting a difference.

**13.** *Definition, principle, working equation and the purpose of three items.* **Definition**: the specific heat capacity of a material is the **quantity of heat energy required to raise the temperature of unit mass (1 kg) of the material by one degree Celsius (1 °C)**; it measures the material's ability to store or release heat and is a **property of the material**, independent of the size of the sample. **SI unit: J/kg·°C**, equivalently J/kg·K. **Principle**: the **principle of conservation of energy applied to a mixture** — in a thermally isolated system, **heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the cold bodies**, all of them ending at one common final temperature. **Working equation**: **m_s c_s(T_s − T_f) = (m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r)**, whence **c_s = (m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r)/[m_s(T_s − T_f)]**, where **m_s** is the mass of the solid (kg), **c_s** its specific heat capacity (J/kg·°C, the unknown), **T_s** its temperature just before transfer (°C); **m_w** the mass of water (kg) and **c_w = 4186 J/kg·°C**; **m_c** the mass of the calorimeter (kg) and **c_c** its specific heat capacity (387 J/kg·°C for copper); **T_r** the common initial temperature of water and calorimeter (room temperature) and **T_f** the final equilibrium temperature of the mixture, with **T_s > T_f > T_r**. **Purposes**: the **insulating jacket and its cover** isolate the calorimeter from the room so that no heat enters or leaves the system, which is precisely what makes "heat lost = heat gained" an equation with no missing term; the **crucible tongs** allow the hot block (at 90–99 °C) to be moved quickly and safely, so that it arrives in the water still at the recorded T_s and so that the hand is not burned; the **tare button** zeroes the balance after the empty calorimeter has been weighed as m_c, so that the subsequent reading is the **mass of the water alone**, m_w, and not water plus vessel.

**14. Heat capacity = 301 J/°C; heat gained = 271 J; c_s = 410 J/kg·°C; 391 J/kg·°C if the calorimeter is ignored** — *the mixture method worked through*. Convert the masses: m_s = 8.9 × 10⁻³ kg, m_c = 36.36 × 10⁻³ kg, m_w = 68.63 × 10⁻³ kg. **Heat capacity of the mixture**: m_w c_w = 0.06863 × 4186 = **287.29 J/°C**; m_c c_c = 0.03636 × 387 = **14.07 J/°C**; total **m_w c_w + m_c c_c = 301.36 J/°C** (a water equivalent of 301.36/4186 = 72.0 g of water). **Heat gained**: T_f − T_r = 17.0 − 16.1 = **0.9 °C**, so Q = 301.36 × 0.9 = **271.2 J**. **The solid's side**: T_s − T_f = 91.3 − 17.0 = **74.3 °C**, so m_s(T_s − T_f) = 0.0089 × 74.3 = **0.6613 kg·°C**. **Hence c_s = 271.2/0.6613 = 410 J/kg·°C.** **Ignoring the calorimeter** would give 287.29 × 0.9/0.6613 = **391 J/kg·°C**, about 5 % lower — the calorimeter term is small but not negligible. **Comment**: the accepted specific heat of copper is **387 J/kg·°C**, so the experimental 410 J/kg·°C is high by about **6 %**. The result is limited chiefly by the tiny temperature rise of 0.9 °C, which a thermometer reading to 0.1 °C cannot resolve to better than a few per cent; a slightly high reading of T_f — from heat gained through the jacket, or from hot water carried over on the block — raises c_s, while heat lost in transfer would have lowered it. Using a larger block, less water and a thermometer of finer scale would tighten the result.

**15.** *Procedure, precautions and errors.* **Procedure**: (1) drag the metal cube onto the **digital balance** to read its mass **m_s** and click **Record**; (2) drag the block into the **glass beaker**; (3) raise the **water reservoir** above the beaker to fill it with water; (4) place the beaker on the **hot plate**; (5) drag the **thermometer** to the beaker; (6) rotate the knob to set the heating temperature between **90 and 99 °C** and Record; (7) while the solid heats, weigh the **calorimeter** on the balance as **m_c** and Record; (8) **tare the balance**, then run water into the calorimeter from the reservoir to read the mass of water **m_w** and Record; (9) place the calorimeter inside the **insulating jacket**, fit the **top cover**, read the water temperature **T_r** and Record; (10) return to the hot plate, confirm the solid has reached **T_s**, and Record; (11) using the **crucible tongs**, lift the hot cube and transfer it into the calorimeter; (12) replace the cover and read the final equilibrium temperature **T_f** (which must exceed T_r) and Record; (13) the run ends and an **Excel sheet** downloads with m_s, m_c, m_w, T_r, T_s, T_f, c_w and c_c, from which c_s is computed. **Precautions** (any three): **transfer the block quickly and gently with the tongs, fully submerging it** — this protects **T_s**, the temperature at which the block actually enters the water, and ensures all of its heat is delivered; **keep the jacket closed with its cover on throughout** — this protects **T_f** by preventing heat exchange with the room; **tare the balance after weighing the calorimeter and before adding water** — this protects **m_w**, which must be the water alone; **read T_f at the highest steady value**, not at a fixed time, so that the mixture is genuinely at equilibrium but has not begun to cool; **use a large block and only enough water to cover it**, to make (T_f − T_r) as large and therefore as precisely measurable as possible; **avoid parallax and check the thermometer for zero error**, keeping the bulb in the liquid rather than against the block or the vessel wall. **Sources of error** (any three, with direction): **heat lost by the block during a slow transfer** — it enters below the recorded T_s, so (T_s − T_f) is overstated and **c_s comes out too low**; **heat leaking from the calorimeter to the room** — T_f is depressed, the heat gained is understated and **c_s is too low**; **omitting the calorimeter term m_c c_c** — the heat gained is understated and **c_s is too low** (391 rather than 410 J/kg·°C); **the very small temperature rise of 0.9 °C read on a 0.1 °C scale** — a large random uncertainty that passes proportionally into c_s in either direction; **hot water carried over on the block from the beaker** — extra heat unaccounted for, so **c_s is too high**; **failure to tare, so that m_w includes the calorimeter** — the heat gained is grossly overstated and **c_s is far too high**; **heat absorbed by the thermometer** and any stirrer, which appears in no term of the equation — c_s slightly low; and **mixing grams with kilograms**, which shifts the answer by a factor of 10³.

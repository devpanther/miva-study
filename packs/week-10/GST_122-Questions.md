## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Define inductive reasoning and deductive reasoning as the lesson defines them. Then set out four points of contrast between them, using the lesson's own summary line about "all or nothing" logic in your answer.

**14.** Name the three types of inductive reasoning the lesson treats in detail, give the lesson's own example of each, and list the four further types it sets for private reading.

**15.** Distinguish validity, truth and soundness, using one worked argument of your own to show that a valid argument need not have a true conclusion. Then explain why the words *valid* and *invalid* are never applied to an inductive argument, and say what words are used instead.

## Answers

**1. b** — *inductive reasoning*: the lesson's definition word for word — likely or probable, but not certain. (a) is its opposite: deduction gives a conclusion that is *necessarily* true if the premises are true. (c) modus ponens is a particular *deductive* form, not a whole kind of reasoning. (d) a categorical syllogism is likewise one deductive form; both (c) and (d) sit inside (a).

**2. c** — *categorical syllogism*: the lesson's own label for this worked example; the premises link **classes** (mammals, whales) and establish a necessary relationship, so the conclusion is certain given true premises. (a) modus tollens needs an if–then premise plus the denial of its consequent, and nothing here is conditional or negated. (b) and (d) are types of *inductive* reasoning, but this conclusion is guaranteed, not probable.

**3. b** — *modus tollens*: the lesson's stated pattern, "If A, then B; Not B; therefore, Not A" — it **denies the consequent** and is valid. (a) modus ponens affirms the antecedent ("It is raining") instead. (c) affirming the consequent would run "the ground is wet, therefore it's raining" — invalid. (d) denying the antecedent would run "it's not raining, therefore the ground isn't wet" — also invalid. All four options involve the same conditional, so the question tests which clause the second premise touches and whether it is affirmed or denied.

**4. b** — *pattern recognition (premise)*: the lesson's three-stage table runs specific observation ("My swan is white") → pattern recognition, which it explicitly labels the **premise** ("Every observed swan is white") → general conclusion ("Therefore, all swans are white"). (a) and (c) are the other two stages; (d) is a type of inductive reasoning, not a stage in this table.

**5. c** — *probability of conclusions*: the lesson says twice that the strength of the conclusion depends on the **quantity and quality of the evidence**. (a) counting premises has nothing to do with it. (b) is the definition of **validity**, which belongs to deduction — the standard trap in this question. (d) syllogistic form is a deductive matter, and dressing an inductive argument as a syllogism would not make it stronger.

**6. b** — *analogical reasoning*: conclusions drawn about one thing from its **similarities** to another. (a) generalisation moves from many observed instances to a broad statement about the group, whereas this moves from one known group to one new member by resemblance. (c) would require statistical data or a probability figure. (d) is deductive and would need a universal premise ("all cats are good hunters"), which would make the conclusion certain rather than "likely".

**7. c** — *validity*: the lesson's answer — an argument is valid if the conclusion **follows logically from the premises**. (a) is soundness, not validity, and is the commonest error here: true premises alone make nothing valid. (b) a true conclusion can be reached by an invalid argument, and a valid argument can reach a false one. (d) weight of evidence is the measure of inductive **strength**, not of validity.

**8. a** — *valid but unsound*: the form is impeccable — if the premises were true the conclusion would have to be — so it is **valid**; but "All cats are reptiles" is false, so it is **unsound** and the conclusion is false. (b) is incoherent: soundness requires validity, so nothing can be sound and invalid. (c) fails on the false premise. (d) fails because the form is faultless. This is the question that separates form from truth.

**9. c** — *affirming the consequent*: the second premise restates the **"then" half** and reasons backwards to the "if" half, which does not follow — a burst pipe or a sprinkler would wet the ground. (a) modus ponens would require "It is raining" as the second premise. (b) modus tollens would require "The ground is not wet". (d) denying the antecedent would require "It is not raining"; nothing here is negated at all.

**10. c** — *inductive reasoning / prediction*: the lesson's opening problem. Past census and growth figures are specific observations, the growth rate is the **pattern**, and the 15-year figure is **probable**, not guaranteed. (a) mistakes the subject matter for the method — arithmetic on the data does not make the projection necessary. (b) no census record entails a future population. (d) reaches the right kind of reasoning by the wrong reason: guaranteed conclusions are the mark of **deduction**, and induction never guarantees.

**11. d** — *limitations of inductive reasoning*: the lesson names sampling bias, reliance on incomplete data, the potential for coincidental patterns, and the contingency of conclusions on evidence that varies in quality and quantity. Clarity of statement is not among them, and it is not a defect peculiar to induction. (a), (b) and (c) are quoted from the lesson.

**12. a** — *statistical reasoning, inductive*: the lesson defines statistical reasoning as drawing conclusions from statistical data or probability, and gives this very example; a 70% probability is by definition not certain, so it is inductive. (b) misfiles it as deductive — a probability figure is the clearest possible marker of induction. (c) a mathematical proof (the lesson's example: the sum of 2 and 4 is even) yields certainty from axioms and established rules, not a percentage. (d) is wrong on both counts: it is not the generalisation type, and it is not deductive.

**13.** *Inductive vs deductive reasoning*: **Inductive reasoning** is a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is considered **likely or probable, but not certain**, based on the premises; the method is to look for **patterns** so as to draw conclusions or make a **generalisation**, moving from **specific instances to a probable conclusion**. **Deductive reasoning** is a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is **necessarily true if the premises are true** — if the premises are true, the conclusion **must** also be true; it uses a set of data to reach a specific conclusion that comes up to be a fact, and is often used in **mathematics and formal logic**. *Four contrasts*: (i) **Certainty** — deduction guarantees the conclusion when the argument is valid and the premises true; induction only makes it probable. (ii) **Direction** — deduction runs from a general rule to a specific conclusion; induction runs from specific observations to a general or predictive conclusion. (iii) **Assessment words** — deductive arguments are **valid or invalid**, and **sound** when valid with true premises; inductive arguments are **strong or weak**, their strength depending on the **quantity and quality of the evidence**. (iv) **Typical use** — deduction in mathematics, proofs and formal logic (syllogisms, modus ponens, modus tollens); induction in forecasting, statistics and everyday generalisation, where evidence is incomplete. The lesson's summary line: **deductive reasoning is akin to "all or nothing" logic, while inductive reasoning acknowledges the nuances and probabilities inherent in many real-world situations** — deduction admits no degrees, induction is a matter of degree.

**14.** *Types of inductive reasoning*: **Generalisation** — a broad statement about a group or category based on specific observations; *example*: after observing multiple instances of people wearing sunglasses and hats on sunny days, you might generalise that "Many people wear sunglasses and hats on sunny days." **Analogical reasoning** — drawing conclusions about one situation or object based on its similarities to another situation or object; *example*: since cats are known to be good hunters, and this new breed of cat has the same characteristics, it's likely to be a good hunter as well. **Statistical reasoning** — drawing conclusions based on statistical data or probability; *example*: based on past election results and current polling data, Candidate A has a 70% probability of winning the upcoming election. *Set for further reading*: **causal inference, prediction, abductive reasoning, enumerative induction**.

**15.** *Validity, truth and soundness*: **Truth** is a property of individual **statements** — a premise or a conclusion is true or false. **Validity** is a property of an argument's **form**: an argument is valid when the conclusion **follows logically from the premises**, that is, when it would be impossible for all the premises to be true and the conclusion false. **Soundness** combines the two: an argument is sound when it is **valid and all its premises are actually true**, and only a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion. *Worked example*: "All cats are reptiles. Fluffy is a cat. Therefore, Fluffy is a reptile." The form is faultless — if those premises were true the conclusion would have to be — so the argument is **valid**; but the first premise is false, so the argument is **unsound** and its conclusion is false. Validity therefore says nothing about truth. *Why not "valid" for induction*: validity is an all-or-nothing property defined by the impossibility of true premises with a false conclusion, and an inductive argument **always** leaves that possibility open — its conclusion goes beyond the evidence, which is why the lesson says induction gives conclusions that are probable but not guaranteed. Calling such an argument invalid would also be misleading, since it was never trying to guarantee anything. The correct words are **strong** and **weak** — an argument is strong when its premises make the conclusion probable, and its strength varies with the **quantity and quality of the evidence**; a strong argument with true premises is called **cogent**, which is induction's counterpart to soundness.

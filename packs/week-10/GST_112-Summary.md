# GST_112 — Week 10 Summary

*Nigerian Peoples and Culture · Week 10 (9 – 15 Nov 2026) · Topic: Nigeria's pop culture and the four waves of popular Nigerian music, and police brutality, the #EndSARS crisis, the 2023 election and youth political awakening*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Definition, both halves of it.** **Popular culture refers to the traditions and material culture of a particular society.** In the **modern West** it refers to **cultural products such as music, art, literature, fashion, dance, film, cyberculture, television, and radio consumed by most of a society's population**. The deck's second definition: **"those types of media that have mass accessibility and appeal."**
2. **The coinage, and what it was coined against.** The term **"popular culture" was coined in the mid-19th century** and refers to the **cultural traditions of the people, in contrast to the "official culture" of the state or governing classes.** This exact sentence is repeated on the deck's own Summary slide — a strong signal it is examinable.
3. **The origin story: Industrial Revolution → middle class → cities → post-war mass media.** Scholars trace the origins of popular culture to the **creation of the middle class generated by the Industrial Revolution**; people **configured into working classes** who **moved into urban environments** began creating their **own culture and distinct lifestyle**; and **after the end of World War II, innovations in mass media** led to significant cultural and social changes in the West. The rise of popular culture **coincided with the rise of capitalism**, so newly invented goods were **marketed to different classes**, and the meaning of popular culture **began to merge with that of mass culture, consumer culture, image culture, media culture, and culture created by manufacturers for mass consumption.**
4. **Popular music, defined.** **All contemporaneous music with broad, immediate, and implicitly transient attractiveness that appeals to a mass audience** — receptive to people because they are **familiar with its idioms** and it **does not require guided listening**. It **primarily attracts urban dwellers, particularly the youth**, seeking **social entertainment and dancing**, and **predominantly thrives in pubs and nightclubs**.
5. **The evolution of Nigerian popular music has been delimited into four epochal stages** — **1950–67, 1967–80, 1980–90, 1992–till present**. Learn the four date ranges, the genre that defines each, and two or three artists per wave (table below).
6. **#EndSARS, defined by the deck:** a **decentralised social movement and series of mass protests against police brutality in Nigeria**. The agitation began with a **call for the disbanding of the Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS), a unit of the police**, attributed to a host of **unlawful activities, especially against youths**. It **began as a hashtag on Twitter in 2017** and **metamorphosed into a crisis in October 2020**. The **immediate cause** of the crisis was **a shooting captured on video in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian**.
7. **The three weeks, and the Lekki toll gate.** The **October 2020** protest **lasted for three weeks** and **ended with the Nigerian army shooting at unarmed protesters**: on **October 20th, 2020, the Nigerian Army opened fire on protesters at the Lekki toll gate**, killing and injuring scores. Protests **initially started as a response to police brutality** but **soon became a broader demand for good governance, transparency, and an end to systemic corruption**.
8. **The numbers.** Youth are **roughly 60% of the population**; **The Cable** identified **91 deaths caused by police actions between March 2019 and February 2020**; the movement created a record of **10 million new registered voters**; **76% of newly registered voters were young people**, and **40% of those registered as students**; national **voter turnout was 29%** — **no election in Nigeria's six decades of independence has had a lower participation rate**; **87.2 million of the 93.4 million registered voters obtained their Permanent Voter Cards**.

## Strand 1 — The four waves of popular Nigerian music

| Wave | Dates | Genres / what defines it | Artists the deck names |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First Wave** | **1950–67** | **Deeply traditional.** Evolution of **Juju, Apala, Nigerian Highlife**. External influences: **Ghanaian Highlife, Caribbean calypso, and Mambo**. Popular for its **bands and stage performances** | **I.K. Dairo and his Blue Spots**, **Haruna Ishola and his Group**, **E.C. Arinze**, **Sammy Akpabot and His All Stars**, **Godwin Omabuwa and his Sound Makers**, **Ganiyu Kale and His Guinea Mambo Orchestra**, **Sefiu Ayan**, **Kasumu Adio**, **Ayinla Omowura**, **Bobby Benson**, **Jim Lawson & the Mayor's Dance Band**, **Dan Maraya** |
| **Second Wave** | **1967–80** | **Highlife regained popularity in the 1970s**; **Afrobeat became mainstream**; **Juju waxing strong** (a **song for the upper class**); **Fuji emerged as a fusion of Apala and Ajisari**, drawing **major influences from Islam** and **mostly for the lower class** | Highlife: **Victor Olaiya, Osadebe, Oliver De Coque, Oriental Brother, Sunny Okosun, Victor Uwaifo, Orlando Owoh, Christie Essien Igbokwe**. Afrobeat: **Fela**, a household name, who **addressed social ills with his brand of music**. Juju: **Ebenezer Obey** and **King Sunny Ade**. Fuji: **Sikiru Ayinde Barrister** and **Ayinla Kollington** |
| **Third Wave** | **1980–90** | New sounds: **Waka, Yo Pop, and Afro Juju**. **International record labels** arrive — **Island Record signed Sunny Ade**, **Virgin Records signed Ebenezer Obey**. A **yearning to appeal to an international audience** led to **greater fusion of international sounds and costumes**; artists infused **Reggae, Calypso, rap, and R&B**. Then **dwindling** as the international labels **left** and the music was **sounding more American than Nigerian** | **Salawa Abeni** (fame with her **Waka** brand), **Wasiu Ayinde Marshall** (Fuji), **Segun Adewale (Yo Pop)**, **Shina Peters (Afro Juju)**; out of **Ajegunle**, **Junior and Pretty** with **Bolanle** and **Monika**; **Oritz Williki, Blacky (Rosie), Majek Fashek, Mike Okri, Ras Kimono, Evi Edna Ogholi** |
| **Fourth Wave** | **1992–till present** | The **quest to sound more American than Nigerian continued**; artists **appealed to the youth**, with a **rarity of local languages in their lyrics**. **The rebuilding process began with the fourth wave.** **Over time**, Nigerian pop culture began to **infuse local languages and styles** into the craft to create a **unique sound and style** | **Remedies, Plantashun Boiz, Styl Plus, Psquare, Mo'Hits Crew** |

**The impact point (a likely essay prompt):** the first wave's music had a **significant impact on the popular culture of that time as it influenced dressing, the pattern of social gathering, and also indicated class.**

**The Civil War point:** the Second Wave was **pivotal** because the **Nigerian Civil War particularly affected Highlife music**, which had gained popularity as **feel-good and upper-class music** — whereas **in Ghana, from where it was borrowed, it was a musical genre that addressed social problems.**

**Class map — hold this one:** **Juju = upper class**; **Fuji = lower class** (fusion of **Apala and Ajisari**, **Islamic** influence); **Highlife = feel-good, upper-class in Nigeria, social-problem music in Ghana**.

## Strand 2 — Police brutality, #EndSARS, 2023

**The long-standing problem.** In Nigeria, **excessive use of force, extrajudicial killings, harassment, and power abuse by law enforcement agencies** are long-standing problems. The **#EndSARS movement**, which **gained significant momentum in October 2020**, brought **international attention** to the issue, calling for an **end to police brutality** and demanding **accountability for the actions of the now-disbanded Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS)**.

**The two scholars — know who says which:**

| Scholar | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Moses Ochonu** | The **Nigerian Police have not been decolonised**. In the **colonial period** the police were about **"suppression of revolts" rather than protection**; **"brute force was the primary operating philosophy of the police."** |
| **Abimbola Oyarinu** (the lecturer) | **SARS began during a moment of turmoil for Nigeria**: **"the falling and failing economy after the oil boom, the depreciating naira, the Nigerian civil war, the Structural Adjustment Programme, and poor leadership"** — factors that created **an environment ripe for insecurity and violent crimes** |

**Why nothing was punished.** The **courts and the judicial system** at the time were **either too slow, inefficient, incompetent, or not respected enough to impose justice on the police force**. **Erring officers were not sanctioned as they boasted openly about taking lives without repercussion.**

**What happened in the protest.** **Thousands of Nigerian youths stormed the streets** against **police brutality and extrajudicial killings**. **Two correctional facilities in Edo State were attacked**, **public and private properties were vandalised**, and **business premises were looted**. **Security forces responded harshly**; the government's **first attempts to quell protesters aggravated the situation**, but it **finally dissolved SARS and promised significant police reform** — **however, there has been little discernible success.**

**Social media.** The October protest was **not the first time** Nigerian youths protested police brutality, **but this time they harnessed the power of social media** to draw **both local and international attention**. **Like the Arab Spring**, they used social media to **distribute e-flyers detailing the time and location of the protest**. The protest **spread nationwide but was not popular in the northern part of the country**. **Twitter was especially deployed** to achieve its purpose.

**Post-EndSARS — two separate reports, two separate bodies:**

| Body | Finding | Period |
|---|---|---|
| **The Cable** | **91 deaths** caused by police actions | **March 2019 – February 2020** (before the crisis) |
| **Global Rights** | **164 cases of extrajudicial killings** — *just one year after the crisis* | **January – September 2021** |
| **Amnesty International** | **52 incidences of unlawful killings** and **62 cases of arbitrary arrest, ill treatment, and torture**, **in the south-east alone** | **4 months from January 2021** |

**The Arab Spring frame.** Nigerian youth are **roughly 60% of the population** and **a significant influence on the future of the country**; if **not effectively utilised**, this **big youth population could potentially lead to uprisings like the 2011 Arab Spring**. That a **similar pattern was seen in 2020** shows **youth upheavals are not unheard of in Nigeria**.

**2023 elections.** After **October 20, 2020**, **a lot of youth decided to take action and take their anger to the polls**, and the **most frequent** cause prompting participation was **the way the EndSARS protest ended, which resulted in the loss of so many lives**. The movement created a record of **10 million new registered voters** for the **February 25th and March 11th** elections; **around 76% of newly registered voters were young people**, and **40% of those numbers registered as students**. **Despite the huge attendance of youngsters, the countrywide voter turnout was 29%** — the **lowest participation rate in Nigeria's six decades of independence**. **87.2 million of the 93.4 million registered voters obtained their Permanent Voter Cards.**

**Youth reawakening.** The **#EndSARS protests and the broader youth movement** have **sparked a significant political awakening**: young Nigerians are **increasingly engaged in political discourse, demanding accountability and actively participating in political activities**. This has led to **increased civic engagement**, including the **formation of youth-led advocacy groups**, **participation in voter registration drives**, and **calls for increased representation of young people in political offices**. **Social media has played a crucial role in amplifying the voices of young Nigerians and facilitating the organisation of collective action.**

## Commonly confused

- **2017 vs October 2020.** **#EndSARS began as a hashtag on Twitter in 2017**; it **metamorphosed into a crisis in October 2020**. A question asking when the movement *began* wants **2017**; one asking when it *gained significant momentum* or became a crisis wants **October 2020**.
- **The immediate cause vs the underlying cause.** The **immediate cause** was **a shooting captured on video in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian**. The underlying causes are the **long-standing excessive force, extrajudicial killings, harassment and abuse of power** — and, on Oyarinu's account, **the post-oil-boom economy, the depreciating naira, the civil war, SAP and poor leadership** that produced SARS in the first place.
- **October 2020 (the protest) vs October 20th, 2020 (Lekki).** The protest ran through October and **lasted three weeks**; the **army opened fire at the Lekki toll gate on October 20th, 2020**.
- **Ochonu vs Oyarinu.** **Ochonu** = the **police have not been decolonised**, colonial policing was **"suppression of revolts"** and **"brute force was the primary operating philosophy"**. **Oyarinu** = **SARS was born of economic and leadership turmoil**. Do not attach the quote to the wrong name.
- **The Cable (91) vs Global Rights (164) vs Amnesty (52 / 62).** Three bodies, three figures, three windows: **91 deaths, March 2019–Feb 2020**; **164 extrajudicial killings, Jan–Sep 2021**; **52 unlawful killings and 62 arbitrary arrests/ill treatment/torture in the south-east, four months from January 2021**.
- **76% vs 40% vs 29% vs 60%.** **76%** = share of **newly registered voters** who were young; **40%** = share of *those* who registered **as students**; **29%** = **countrywide voter turnout**; **60%** = youth share of the **population**. And **10 million** = new registered voters; **87.2m of 93.4m** = **PVCs collected of registered voters**.
- **Juju vs Fuji vs Apala vs Waka vs Afro Juju vs Yo Pop.** **Juju** — first wave, upper class, **Obey** and **Sunny Ade**. **Apala** — first wave, **Haruna Ishola**. **Fuji** — second wave, **fusion of Apala and Ajisari**, **Islamic**, lower class, **Barrister** and **Kollington**. **Waka** — third wave, **Salawa Abeni**. **Yo Pop** — third wave, **Segun Adewale**. **Afro Juju** — third wave, **Shina Peters**. **Afrobeat** — second wave, **Fela**.
- **Island vs Virgin.** **Island Record signed Sunny Ade**; **Virgin Records signed Ebenezer Obey**. Reversing them is the obvious distractor.
- **Highlife in Nigeria vs Highlife in Ghana.** In **Nigeria** it was **feel-good, upper-class** music; **in Ghana, from where it was borrowed**, it **addressed social problems**. The **Civil War** particularly affected it.
- **Third wave decline vs fourth wave rebuilding.** The **third wave** ends in **dwindling** — international labels **left**, the music was **sounding more American than Nigerian**. **"The rebuilding process began with the fourth wave"** — but note the fourth wave *continued* the American quest at first, and only **over time** infused **local languages and styles**.
- **Popular culture vs official culture.** Popular culture is the culture **of the people**, defined **in contrast to the "official culture" of the state or governing classes** — not a synonym for it.
- **Middle class vs working class in the origin story.** Scholars trace origins to the **creation of the middle class generated by the Industrial Revolution**; the people who then **created their own culture and distinct lifestyle** in **urban environments** were those **configured into working classes**. Both appear; do not collapse them.

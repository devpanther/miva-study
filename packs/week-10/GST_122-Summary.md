# GST_122 — Week 10 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 10 (9 – 15 Nov 2026) · Topic: Inductive and Deductive Reasoning*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Inductive reasoning** = "a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is considered **likely or probable, but not certain**, based on the premises." The deck's plain-English gloss: *the method here is to look for **patterns** so that you can draw conclusions or make a **generalisation***. Inductive arguments **generalise from specific observations or past experiences** — they move **from specific instances to a probable or likely conclusion**.
2. **Deductive reasoning** = "a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is **necessarily true if the premises are true**. In other words, if the premises are true, the conclusion **must** also be true." The deck's plain-English gloss: *using a set of data to make a specific conclusion that comes up to be a fact*. Deductive arguments are **often used in mathematics and formal logic**.
3. **The deck's three named types of inductive reasoning, with its own examples.** **Generalisation** — "a broad statement about a group or category based on specific observations" (*after observing multiple instances of people wearing sunglasses and hats on sunny days, you might generalise that "Many people wear sunglasses and hats on sunny days"*). **Analogical reasoning** — "drawing conclusions about one situation or object based on its **similarities** to another" (*since cats are known to be good hunters, and this new breed of cat has the same characteristics, it's likely to be a good hunter as well*). **Statistical reasoning** — "drawing conclusions based on **statistical data or probability**" (*based on past election results and current polling data, Candidate A has a 70% probability of winning*). The deck then sets four more for private reading: **causal inference, prediction, abductive reasoning, enumerative induction**.
4. **The swan argument — the deck's three-stage model of induction.** *Specific observation*: "My swan is white." → *Pattern recognition (premise)*: "Every observed swan is white." → *General conclusion*: "Therefore, all swans are white." Learn the three stage-labels; the exam can give you one stage and ask for the others.
5. **Probability — the deck's central claim about induction.** "Inductive reasoning provides conclusions that are **probable or likely, but not guaranteed to be true**. The **strength of the conclusion depends on the quantity and quality of the evidence**." Its two illustrations: from historical weather data you may conclude it is **likely** to be cold in winter, but **cannot be certain** of the weather on a **specific day**; and the *Prediction of Disease Outbreak* case — Premise 1, "In the past, every time there was an increase in mosquito activity, there was a corresponding increase in cases of a mosquito-borne disease"; Premise 2, "There has been a recent increase in mosquito activity"; conclusion, "It is **likely** that there will be a disease outbreak." The deck's explanation: inductive reasoning is used to **predict a future event** based on **observed patterns** (a correlation between mosquito activity and disease cases).
6. **The three worked deductive examples, by name.** **Categorical syllogism** — *All mammals have a backbone* + *Whales are mammals* → *Therefore, whales have a backbone*; the premises "establish a **necessary relationship**", so the conclusion is certain **given that the premises are true**. **Modus ponens** — *If it's raining, then the ground is wet* + *It is raining* → *Therefore, the ground is wet*; the deck's rule: if you **affirm the antecedent** (the "if" part of a conditional) and the conditional is true, then the **consequent** (the "then" part) **must** be true — "a valid and certain deductive argument". **Mathematical proof** — *The sum of two even numbers is always even* + *2 and 4 are even numbers* → *Therefore, the sum of 2 and 4 is even*; certain because it follows the **logical rules of mathematics**, proving theorems from **axioms and established rules**. The deck's other stock syllogism: *All humans are mortal / Socrates is a human / Therefore, Socrates is mortal*.
7. **Modus tollens** — the deck's one named *type* of deductive reasoning: "another **valid** deductive argument that **denies the consequent**. It follows the pattern **'If A, then B; Not B; therefore, Not A.'**" Example: *"If it's raining, the ground is wet. The ground is **not** wet. Therefore, it's **not** raining."*
8. **Valid vs sound, and the limitations of induction — the deck's two discussion answers.** To decide an argument is deductive, "look for a **clear structure where the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises**. A deductive argument is considered **valid** if the conclusion **follows logically from the premises**, and it is **sound** if the **premises are true**. Valid deductive arguments guarantee certainty in the conclusions." Against induction the deck names four weaknesses: **sampling bias**, **reliance on incomplete data**, **the potential for coincidental patterns**, and the fact that **inductive conclusions are contingent on the quality and quantity of the evidence, which can vary**.

## The deck's opening problem

*"A village wishes to estimate the population for the next 15 years. Which reasoning will be appropriate to use, inductive or deductive?"* The deck poses this twice and never prints the answer. It is **inductive**: you have past census and birth/death figures (specific observations), you extract a **pattern** (growth rate), and you project a **probable** future figure. No premise makes the 2041 population *necessarily* true, so nothing here is deductive. This is exactly the *prediction* subtype the deck lists for further reading.

## Deductive vs inductive, side by side

| | **Deductive** | **Inductive** |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | General rule → specific conclusion | Specific instances → general or probable conclusion |
| Conclusion | **Necessarily true** if premises are true — certain, guaranteed | **Likely or probable** — never guaranteed |
| Quality word | **Valid** / **invalid**; **sound** / unsound | **Strong** / **weak**; cogent / uncogent |
| What it adds | Nothing new — the conclusion is already contained in the premises | New content — it goes **beyond** the evidence |
| Typical home | Mathematics and formal logic; syllogisms, proofs | Science, statistics, forecasting, everyday generalisation |
| Deck's named forms | Categorical syllogism, modus ponens, modus tollens, mathematical proof | Generalisation, analogical reasoning, statistical reasoning (plus causal inference, prediction, abductive reasoning, enumerative induction) |
| Fails when | The form is broken (invalid) or a premise is false (unsound) | Evidence is too little, unrepresentative or coincidental (weak) |

The deck's own summary: "deductive reasoning provides **certainty** in conclusions when the argument is **valid** and the premises are **true**, while inductive reasoning offers **probable** conclusions based on **patterns, evidence and generalisations**. Both forms of reasoning have their strengths and are applied in different contexts based on the nature of the problem or question at hand. **Deductive reasoning is akin to 'all or nothing' logic, while inductive reasoning acknowledges the nuances and probabilities** inherent in many real-world situations."

## Validity, truth, soundness, strength — the four words the exam turns on

- **Validity** is a property of the **form** only. An argument is **valid** when it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false — i.e. the conclusion **follows logically**. Validity says **nothing** about whether the premises are actually true. *All cats are reptiles; Fluffy is a cat; therefore Fluffy is a reptile* is **valid** and its conclusion is **false**.
- **Truth** is a property of individual **statements** (premises and conclusion), not of arguments. Never call an argument "true"; never call a premise "valid".
- **Soundness** = **valid form + all premises actually true**. Only a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion. The deck states this in two halves ("valid if the conclusion follows logically from the premises… sound if the premises are true"); read the second half as *and the argument is also valid*.
- **Strength** is the inductive counterpart of validity: a **strong** argument is one whose premises make the conclusion **probable**. It comes in **degrees** — more and better evidence makes it stronger, per the deck's "quantity and quality of the evidence". A strong argument with true premises is **cogent**.
- **Consequence:** a valid argument can have a false conclusion (if a premise is false); a **sound** one cannot. A **strong** inductive argument can have a false conclusion even with **all premises true** — that is the price of going beyond the evidence, and it is precisely what the swan example illustrates.

## Formal fallacies — the invalid look-alikes of the deck's two valid forms

The deck names **modus ponens** and **modus tollens** as valid. Each has a mirror-image invalid form, and these are the standard 100-level traps:

| Name | Pattern | Verdict | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Modus ponens** (affirming the antecedent) | If A then B; **A**; therefore **B** | **Valid** | If it's raining, the ground is wet. It is raining. So the ground is wet. |
| **Modus tollens** (denying the consequent) | If A then B; **not B**; therefore **not A** | **Valid** | If it's raining, the ground is wet. The ground is not wet. So it isn't raining. |
| **Affirming the consequent** | If A then B; **B**; therefore **A** | **Invalid (formal fallacy)** | If it's raining, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. So it's raining — but a burst pipe would do it. |
| **Denying the antecedent** | If A then B; **not A**; therefore **not B** | **Invalid (formal fallacy)** | If it's raining, the ground is wet. It isn't raining. So the ground isn't wet — the sprinkler says otherwise. |

**Informal fallacies of weak induction** — the failure modes behind the deck's four named limitations: **hasty generalisation** (concluding from too few cases — the swan argument's flaw, and the fallacy behind "reliance on incomplete data"); **biased or unrepresentative sample** (the deck's "sampling bias"); **false cause / *post hoc ergo propter hoc*** (treating correlation as causation — the deck's "coincidental patterns", and the live risk in the mosquito case); **faulty analogy** (the analogical form fails when the two things are alike in irrelevant respects); **anecdotal evidence** (one vivid case outweighing statistics). Note that none of these makes an argument *invalid* — inductive arguments are never valid or invalid, only **strong or weak**.

## Commonly confused

- **Valid vs true** — *valid* applies to an **argument's form**, *true* to a **statement**. A valid argument built on false premises reaches a false conclusion and is still valid; a true conclusion reached by a broken form is still invalid. Test: could the premises all be true while the conclusion is false? If no, valid.
- **Valid vs sound** — validity is form alone; soundness is **validity plus actually true premises**. Every sound argument is valid; most valid arguments are not sound. The deck's phrasing splits the two clauses and can be misread as "sound = true premises" on its own — soundness requires **both**.
- **Inductive strength vs deductive validity** — validity is **all-or-nothing** (the deck: "all or nothing" logic) and admits no degrees; strength is a **matter of degree** and grows with the quantity and quality of the evidence. Never say an inductive argument is "valid" or a deductive one "strong". Sound is to valid what **cogent** is to strong.
- **Probable vs certain** — induction yields *likely*; deduction yields *must*. Words like *likely, probably, most, usually, tends to, 70% chance* mark induction; *therefore… must, necessarily, all… so* mark deduction. The deck's weather line is the test case: cold **in winter** is probable; the weather **on a specific day** is not certain.
- **Modus ponens vs affirming the consequent** — ponens affirms the **antecedent** (the "if" part) and is valid; affirming the **consequent** (the "then" part) to get back to the antecedent is a **formal fallacy**. Ask which half of the conditional the second premise restates.
- **Modus tollens vs denying the antecedent** — tollens denies the **consequent** and is valid; denying the **antecedent** is a **formal fallacy**. Both are negative, so identify *which* clause is negated, not merely that a negation is present.
- **Generalisation vs statistical reasoning** — generalisation moves from observed instances to a broad qualitative statement ("many people wear sunglasses on sunny days"); statistical reasoning reasons from **data or probability** and normally attaches a **figure** ("70% probability"). Both are inductive.
- **Analogical reasoning vs deductive syllogism** — the analogy says the new cat is *likely* a good hunter **because it resembles** known cases; a syllogism would need a universal premise ("all cats are good hunters") and would then be **certain**. Similarity yields probability, category membership yields necessity.
- **Categorical syllogism vs conditional argument** — a categorical syllogism links **classes** (*all mammals… whales are mammals*); modus ponens and modus tollens work on an **if–then conditional**. Both are deductive; the exam distinguishes them by the shape of the first premise.
- **Premise vs conclusion** — a premise is offered as support; the conclusion is what is being supported (in the deck's tables, always the third column, and usually signalled by *therefore*). In the swan example the **pattern-recognition** stage is the premise, not the specific observation on its own.
- **Deduction vs "reasoning from the general"** as a definition — the real test is **necessity**, not direction. Some valid deductive arguments have no universal premise at all (*If A then B; A; so B*), and some inductive arguments start from a general claim.

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Define popular culture as the lesson does, and account for its rise: name the class and the economic transformation scholars trace it to, what happened after the Second World War, and the terms its meaning began to merge with.

**14.** Set out the four epochal stages of popular Nigerian music. For each, give the dates, the genres that define it, and the artists the lesson names.

**15.** Describe the focus of the #EndSARS crisis and its impact on the 2023 elections: what the movement was and when it began, its immediate cause, what happened in October 2020, and the figures the lesson gives for youth participation and turnout.

## Answers

**1. b** — *the coinage and its contrast*: **mid-19th century**, the **cultural traditions of the people**, set **against the "official culture" of the state or governing classes**. Option (a) describes what popular culture's meaning later **merged with** (mass culture, consumer culture, image culture, media culture, culture created by manufacturers for mass consumption), not the 19th-century coinage. Option (c) inverts the contrast — popular culture is the culture of **the people**, not the governing classes. Option (d) makes it pre-modern folklore; the deck ties it to **mass accessibility and appeal** through **media**.

**2. b** — *the middle class generated by the Industrial Revolution*: the deck's exact formulation. Option (d) is a distractor built from the deck's later line that popular music **thrives in pubs and nightclubs** — that is where the music is consumed, not where popular culture originated. Option (a) is plausible history the deck does not give; (c) confuses the origin of popular culture with the political history of Nigeria.

**3. c** — *four epochal stages*: **1950–67, 1967–80, 1980–90, 1992–till present**. The deck says explicitly that the evolution "has been delimited into **four** epochal stages". Option (b) is tempting because there are four decade-ish blocks, but the number stated is four, and the ranges are not tidy decades.

**4. a** — *Fuji, second wave*: a **fusion of Apala and Ajisari**, drawing **major influences from Islam**, **mostly for the lower class**, with **Sikiru Ayinde Barrister** and **Ayinla Kollington**. Option (b) attaches Fuji to the **upper class** — that is **Juju** (Obey, Sunny Ade). Option (c) lists the **Third Wave** new sounds — **Waka, Yo Pop, Afro Juju** — and Salawa Abeni's genre is **Waka**. Option (d) is the **Ajegunle** third-wave scene, a different thing entirely.

**5. b** — *Island to Sunny Ade, Virgin to Obey*: the deck names both signings in the Third Wave. Option (a) simply reverses them, which is the whole point of the question. Options (c) and (d) attach the labels to the **Afro Juju / Yo Pop / Waka / Fuji** artists, none of whom the deck says were signed internationally.

**6. d** — *the Fourth Wave groups*: **Remedies, Plantashun Boiz, Styl Plus, Psquare, Mo'Hits Crew**, who "**blazed the trail**" at that time. Option (a) is the **Second Wave** Highlife revival; option (b) is the **Third Wave** identity-crisis cohort; option (c) is the **First Wave**. Note the Fourth Wave is defined by **groups appealing to the youth** with a **rarity of local languages** in their lyrics, before local languages and styles were infused **over time**.

**7. a** — *the Nigeria/Ghana contrast*: Highlife had gained popularity in Nigeria as **feel-good and upper-class music**, while **in Ghana, from where it was borrowed, it was a musical genre that addressed social problems**. Option (b) reverses the two countries. Option (c) invents a ban. Option (d) is half true and wrongly sequenced: **Fela** and **Afrobeat** do belong to the Second Wave and Fela **addressed social ills**, but the deck says Highlife **regained popularity in the 1970s** rather than being replaced.

**8. c** — *2017 on Twitter*: the agitation **began as a hashtag on Twitter in 2017** and **metamorphosed into a crisis in October 2020**. Option (a) is the date the movement **gained significant momentum**, not the date it began — the commonest slip on this deck. Option (b) borrows the **2011 Arab Spring**, which the deck uses only as a comparison. Option (d) misuses **The Cable's** 91 deaths, which covers **March 2019 – February 2020** and is a finding, not a trigger.

**9. c** — *the immediate cause*: **a shooting captured on video in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian**. Option (a) is the **culmination**, not the cause — **October 20th, 2020** at the **Lekki toll gate**, after the protest had already run for weeks. Option (b) is an event **during** the protests (two correctional facilities in **Edo State** attacked). Option (d) reverses the sequence: SARS was **dissolved** in response to the protests.

**10. b** — *Ochonu on decolonisation*: the **Nigerian Police have not been decolonised**; colonial policing was about **"suppression of revolts" rather than protection**, and **"brute force was the primary operating philosophy of the police."** Option (a) is **Abimbola Oyarinu's** argument about the origins of SARS — the falling and failing economy after the **oil boom**, the **depreciating naira**, the **civil war**, the **Structural Adjustment Programme**, and **poor leadership**. Option (c) is the deck's separate point about the **courts and judicial system**, attributed to no scholar. Option (d) is a fact about the protest's geography, not an argument about the police.

**11. a** — *the four figures, in their right slots*: **76%** of **newly registered voters** were young people; **40%** of those registered **as students**; countrywide **turnout was 29%**, the lowest in **six decades of independence**. Options (b) and (c) shuffle the same numbers between slots, and (c) also misuses **60%**, which is the youth share of the **population**. Option (d) misreads the PVC figure — **87.2 million of 93.4 million registered voters** collected their cards, which is a collection rate, not a turnout.

**12. c** — *Global Rights*: **164 cases of extrajudicial killings between January and September 2021**, "just one year after the #EndSARS crisis". **The Cable** identified **91 deaths caused by police actions between March 2019 and February 2020** — before the crisis. **Amnesty International** records **52 incidences of unlawful killings** and **62 cases of arbitrary arrest, ill treatment, and torture** in the **south-east alone**, in **4 months from January 2021**. Option (d) invents a body; SARS was the unit complained of, not an investigator.

**13.** *Popular culture and its rise.* **Popular culture refers to the traditions and material culture of a particular society.** In the **modern West**, it refers to **cultural products such as music, art, literature, fashion, dance, film, cyberculture, television, and radio consumed by most of a society's population**; the deck also defines it as **"those types of media that have mass accessibility and appeal."** The **term was coined in the mid-19th century** and refers to the **cultural traditions of the people, in contrast to the "official culture" of the state or governing classes.** As to its rise: scholars trace the origins of popular culture to the **creation of the middle class generated by the Industrial Revolution**. **People who were configured into working classes and moved into urban environments began creating their own culture and distinct lifestyle.** **After the end of World War II, innovations in mass media led to significant cultural and social changes in the West.** The rise of popular culture **coincided with the rise of capitalism**, so that **newly invented goods were being marketed to different classes**; from that point the meaning of popular culture **began to merge with that of mass culture, consumer culture, image culture, media culture, and culture created by manufacturers for mass consumption.** Applied to music, **popular music** is **all contemporaneous music with broad, immediate, and implicitly transient attractiveness that appeals to a mass audience** — receptive because listeners are **familiar with its idioms** and it **does not require guided listening** — and it **primarily attracts urban dwellers, particularly the youth**, thriving **in pubs and nightclubs**.

**14.** *The four epochal stages.* **First Wave, 1950–67** — **deeply traditional**, seeing the evolution of **Juju, Apala and Nigerian Highlife**, under external influences from **Ghanaian Highlife, Caribbean calypso and Mambo**, and **popular for its bands and stage performances**. Artists: **I.K. Dairo and his Blue Spots, Haruna Ishola and his Group, E.C. Arinze, Sammy Akpabot and His All Stars, Godwin Omabuwa and his Sound Makers, Ganiyu Kale and His Guinea Mambo Orchestra, Sefiu Ayan, Kasumu Adio, Ayinla Omowura, Bobby Benson, Jim Lawson & the Mayor's Dance Band, Dan Maraya.** Its music **influenced dressing, the pattern of social gathering, and also indicated class**. **Second Wave, 1967–80** — **Highlife regained popularity in the 1970s** with **Victor Olaiya, Osadebe, Oliver De Coque, Oriental Brother, Sunny Okosun, Victor Uwaifo, Orlando Owoh and Christie Essien Igbokwe**; **Afrobeat became mainstream**, with **Fela** a household name **addressing social ills with his brand of music**; **Juju was waxing strong** with **Ebenezer Obey** and **King Sunny Ade**, and was **a song for the upper class**; and **Fuji emerged as a fusion of Apala and Ajisari**, drawing **major influences from Islam**, **mostly for the lower class**, with **Sikiru Ayinde Barrister** and **Ayinla Kollington**. This was **pivotal** because the **Nigerian Civil War particularly affected Highlife**, which in Nigeria was **feel-good and upper-class music** while **in Ghana, from where it was borrowed, it addressed social problems**. **Third Wave, 1980–90** — new sounds **Waka, Yo Pop and Afro Juju**; **Island Record signed Sunny Ade** and **Virgin Records signed Ebenezer Obey**; **Salawa Abeni** achieved fame with **Waka**, **Wasiu Ayinde Marshall** with **Fuji**, **Segun Adewale** with **Yo Pop** and **Shina Peters** with **Afro Juju**. There was a **yearning to appeal to an international audience**, leading to **greater fusion of international sounds and costumes**, with artists infusing **Reggae, Calypso, rap and R&B**; a lot of sounds came out of **Ajegunle**, with **Junior and Pretty** leading with **Bolanle** and **Monika**, alongside **Oritz Williki, Blacky (Rosie), Majek Fashek, Mike Okri, Ras Kimono and Evi Edna Ogholi**. The wave ended in **dwindling**, as **the international record labels left** and **Nigerian music was struggling for identity because it was sounding more American than Nigerian**. **Fourth Wave, 1992–till present** — **the rebuilding process began** here, though at first **the quest to sound more American than Nigerian continued**; the artists **appealed to the youth**, with a **rarity of local languages in their lyrics**. Groups: **Remedies, Plantashun Boiz, Styl Plus, Psquare and Mo'Hits Crew**. **Over time, Nigerian pop culture began to infuse local languages and styles into their craft to create a unique sound and style.**

**15.** *#EndSARS and the 2023 elections.* **#EndSARS is a decentralised social movement and series of mass protests against police brutality in Nigeria.** The agitation began with a **call for the disbanding of the Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS), a unit of the police**, which has been **attributed to a host of unlawful activities, especially against youths**. It **began as a hashtag on Twitter in 2017** and **metamorphosed into a crisis in October 2020**; the **immediate cause** of the crisis was **a shooting captured on video in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian**. The background is a long-standing pattern of **excessive use of force, extrajudicial killings, harassment and power abuse by law enforcement agencies** — **The Cable** identified **91 deaths caused by police actions between March 2019 and February 2020** — compounded by **courts and a judicial system too slow, inefficient, incompetent or not respected enough to impose justice**, so that **erring officers were not sanctioned as they boasted openly about taking lives without repercussion**. In **October 2020** **thousands of Nigerian youths stormed the streets**; the protest **lasted for three weeks**; **two correctional facilities in Edo State were attacked**, **public and private properties were vandalised** and **business premises were looted**; and on **October 20th, 2020, the Nigerian Army opened fire on protesters at the Lekki toll gate**, killing and injuring scores. Though it started as a **response to police brutality**, it **soon became a broader demand for good governance, transparency, and an end to systemic corruption**. **Like the Arab Spring**, the youths **harnessed the power of social media** and **distributed e-flyers detailing the time and location of the protest**, **Twitter especially**; the protest **spread nationwide but was not popular in the northern part of the country**. The government's **first attempts to quell protesters aggravated the situation**, but it **finally dissolved SARS and promised significant police reform**, with **little discernible success** — **Global Rights** recorded **164 cases of extrajudicial killings between January and September 2021**, and **Amnesty International** records **52 incidences of unlawful killings** and **62 cases of arbitrary arrest, ill treatment and torture** in the **south-east alone** in **4 months from January 2021**. On the **2023 elections**: after **October 20, 2020**, **a lot of youth decided to take action and take their anger to the polls**, and the **most frequent** cause prompting participation was **the way the EndSARS protest ended, which resulted in the loss of so many lives**. The movement created a **record of 10 million new registered voters** for the **February 25th and March 11th** elections; **around 76% of newly registered voters were young people** and **40% of those numbers registered as students**; **87.2 million of the 93.4 million registered voters obtained their Permanent Voter Cards**. Yet **despite the huge attendance of youngsters, the countrywide voter turnout was 29%**, and **no election in Nigeria's six decades of independence has had a lower participation rate**. The lasting effect is a **political awakening**: young Nigerians are **increasingly engaged in political discourse, demanding accountability and actively participating in political activities**, producing **youth-led advocacy groups**, **voter registration drives** and **calls for increased representation of young people in political offices**, with **social media amplifying their voices and facilitating the organisation of collective action**.

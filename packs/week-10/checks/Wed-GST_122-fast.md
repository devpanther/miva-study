# Wednesday — GST_122 fast-hour check

*Inductive and deductive reasoning: definitions, named forms, and valid versus sound.*
*Five questions, straight after the fast hour. Recall only. Score out of 5.*

**1.** An inductive argument's conclusion is:
A. Probable but not guaranteed
B. Necessarily true if the premises are true
C. Certain whatever the premises say
D. True only in mathematics

**2.** In the swan argument, 'Every observed swan is white' is which stage?
A. Specific observation
B. Pattern recognition
C. General conclusion
D. Analogical reasoning

**3.** 'If A then B; not B; therefore not A' is the pattern of:
A. Modus ponens
B. Affirming the consequent
C. Modus tollens
D. Denying the antecedent

**4.** Concluding about one case from its similarity to another is:
A. Generalisation
B. Statistical reasoning
C. Enumerative induction
D. Analogical reasoning

**5.** An argument is sound when:
A. Its conclusion is true
B. It is valid and its premises are true
C. Its premises are probable
D. It is strong and cogent

## Answers

**1. A** — Induction gives likely conclusions whose strength depends on the quantity and quality of the evidence. Necessity belongs to deduction, whose home the deck names as mathematics and formal logic.

**2. B** — The stages run specific observation, then pattern recognition, then general conclusion. 'My swan is white' is the observation; 'all swans are white' is the conclusion.

**3. C** — Modus tollens denies the consequent and is valid. Modus ponens affirms the antecedent; the other two negate or affirm the wrong half and are formal fallacies.

**4. D** — Analogical reasoning draws on similarity, as with the new breed of cat. Generalisation moves from many observations to a broad statement; statistical reasoning attaches a figure.

**5. B** — Soundness is valid form plus actually true premises. A valid argument with a false premise can still reach a false conclusion, and strong or cogent are the inductive words.

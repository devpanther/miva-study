## Section B — Short answer (4)

**21.** Set out the chain of events from 15 January 1966 to 12 January 1970, giving the date of each step, the men who acted at each stage, and how power passed from one hand to the next.

**22.** Trace Nigeria's economy from the 1970s oil boom to the change of buyers in 2014, naming each stage in order with the figures attached to it; then give four of the causes of Nigeria's economic woes as the lesson lists them.

**23.** Weeks 7 and 8 turn on five different constitutional references. State what each of Chapter IV, Chapter Three, Section 24, Section 27 and the Seventh Schedule governs; then list the five rights the lesson names and the lesson's six civic responsibilities.

**24.** Name the six reorientation strategies the deck lists. Then for NOA, OFN, WAI and MAMSER give the year or decree, who launched each, and the criticism the deck attaches to it.

## Answers

**1. A** — *the first coup in Africa*: **Egypt, 1952**. Nigeria's own first coup was **15 January 1966**, and Ghana's the same year — both are the tempting swaps, but the question asks for Africa's first. **[Week 5]**
**2. B** — *the surrender*: **Effiong** surrendered to **Gowon in Lagos on 12 January 1970**. **Ojukwu** had already **fled to Ivory Coast**; Nzeogwu and Ademoyega were January 1966 majors. **[Week 5]**
**3. A** — *Decree No. 34, the Unification Decree*: issued by **Ironsi** and **repealed by Gowon** immediately on taking power in July 1966. It is a **cause of the July countercoup**, not a grievance of the January coup (whose stated grievance was **tribalism and corruption**), which rules out C. **[Week 5]**
**4. B** — *the two failed coups*: **Dimka (1976)**, who **assassinated Murtala Muhammed but could not take power**, and **Orkar (20 April 1990)**, who attacked **Dodan Barracks**, announced the **secession of the south** on the **FRCN**, was crushed **within 10 hours**, and whose **42 men were executed in July 1990**. Garba's 1975 coup **succeeded** (bloodless, Murtala in) and Abacha's 1993 coup succeeded against **Shonekan**. **[Week 5]**
**5. A** — *the indigenous trade system*: it thrived between **1500 and 1800 AD**, shaped by **geography and natural resources**, with the **Niger River** as the pivotal artery. **[Week 6]**
**6. A** — *the census figure*: **140,431,790** at the **2006 census**. **180 million** is the **2015 estimate**, which made Nigeria **first in Africa and sixth in the world** after China, India, the USA, Indonesia and Brazil — a different year and a different figure. **[Week 6]**
**7. A** — *output versus employment*: **over fifty per cent of GDP** before independence; **over 75% of the workforce** in **1960**. The two numbers are routinely swapped; one is share of output, the other share of jobs. **[Week 6]**
**8. A** — *the three dollar figures*: **$20bn = debt relief under Obasanjo**; **$64bn = the foreign reserve peak under Obasanjo**; **$34bn = the reserve in 2015 under Jonathan**, with oil above $100 for most of that administration. Only the first is a debt figure. **[Week 6]**
**9. A** — *where the rights sit*: **Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution, Sections 33 to 46**, with the **African Charter on Human and People's Rights** making them **enforceable in Nigerian courts**. **Chapter II** holds the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles; **Chapter Three** is **citizenship**; the **1979** Constitution is the one that *introduced* both the Directive Principles and the Fundamental Human Rights. **[Week 7]**
**10. A** — *the NHRC figures*: **1,701,519 cases in 2021**, higher than the **1,287,280 complaints in 2020** — the figure **rose**. **367,000** is the count of **women and gender-related issues in 2021**, a separate statistic. **[Week 7]**
**11. A** — *child marriage*: **43% before the 18th birthday, 16% before the age of 15** (Aliogo, 2023). The larger percentage goes with the higher age; B and C invert it. **[Week 7]**
**12. B** — *the National Broadcasting Commission*: the vehicle of the press-freedom abuse, monitoring and regulating broadcast media. The **NHRC** receives complaints of violations, and the **NOA** is a Week 9 reorientation body. **[Week 7]**
**13. A** — *the three routes, and only three*: **birth, registration, naturalisation**. Marriage is not a route in its own right — it qualifies a **non-Nigerian woman** for **registration**. **[Week 8]**
**14. A** — *the two registration classes*: the **non-Nigerian woman married to a Nigerian**, and the **person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparent is a citizen**. A foreign **husband** is not in the class as the lesson states it, the qualifying relative is a **grandparent** and not a parent, and the long-resident foreigner is the **naturalisation** route. **[Week 8]**
**15. A** — *deprivation*: a sentence of **not less than 3 years' imprisonment**, imposed **within 7 years** of becoming a citizen by naturalisation. The two numbers are the classic swap in B. Deprivation reaches only **acquired** citizenship, never citizenship **by birth**. **[Week 8]**
**16. A** — *refusing renunciation*: the only two grounds are that the declaration was made **when Nigeria is involved in a war**, or that it is **contrary to public policy**. Wanting to study or move abroad and reaching a given age are not grounds; option D lists the **deprivation** grounds instead. **[Week 8]**
**17. A** — *the NOA*: established by **Decree 100 of 1993**, the **successor of the Directorate for Social Mobilisation – MAMSER**. **1987** is **MAMSER's** launch year, and **Decree 34** is Ironsi's Unification Decree from Week 5. The **NOA absorbed MAMSER**, never the reverse. **[Week 9]**
**18. A** — *OFN*: an **agricultural initiative of 1976-80** under the **military government of General Olusegun Obasanjo**, aimed at **food security** and **reducing dependence on food imports**, with **urban dwellers encouraged to garden undeveloped building plots**. **1987** and the **Public Bureau** belong to MAMSER; the **military dictatorship of Buhari** ran **WAI**. **[Week 9]**
**19. D** — *DFRRI, 1986*: the six OFN programmes are **ADP (1976), ACGS (1977), RBDA (1978), RBS (1978), GR (1980), DFRRI (1986)**. The two **1978** entries are the trap, and **GR (1980)** and **DFRRI (1986)** fall at or after OFN's own end. **[Week 9]**
**20. A** — *WAI*: it **ended in September 1985**, and its success and its **utmost criticism** were the same fact — the **high handedness of the WAI Brigade**. The seedlings-and-tractors failure in D belongs to **OFN**; MAMSER did not absorb WAI. **[Week 9]**

**21.** *From the first coup to the surrender*. **15 January 1966** — the first coup, the **five majors: Nzeogwu (leader), Ifeajuna, Okafor, Anuforo and Ademoyega**, grievance **tribalism and corruption** and the bigotry of First Republic politicians. It was the **bloodiest** coup: **Prime Minister Abubakar Tafawa Balewa**, the **Sardauna of Sokoto**, Finance Minister **Festus Okotie-Eboh**, Western Premier **Ladoke Akintola**, Brigadiers **Maimalari** and **Ademulegun**, and **Shodeinde** were killed. The coup nonetheless **failed** — the plotters could not seize power — so **Nwafor Orizu**, acting President of the Senate, **voluntarily transferred power** to **Major-General Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi**, the highest-ranking officer, who **suspended the constitution** and formed an interim federal military government, appointing military governors including **Ojukwu in the East**. Ironsi's gaffes — **few easterners killed**, the **release of the plotters**, **Igbo advisers and promotions**, and **Decree No. 34, the Unification Decree** — brought the **29 July 1966 countercoup** by **northern NCOs and officers**: **Ironsi was captured and killed in Ibadan**, **Lt-Col Yakubu Gowon** became Head of State and **repealed Decree 34**. The **pogrom** followed — **between 80,000 and 100,000 easterners massacred in the north**. The **Aburi Accord** failed, and on **30 May 1967 Ojukwu declared the East's independence**; Gowon replied with a **state of emergency** and the creation of **twelve states** (three from the East, only **one predominantly Igbo**). After two and a half years of war and up to **3 million dead**, **Ojukwu fled to Ivory Coast** and **Effiong surrendered to Gowon in Lagos on 12 January 1970** — **"no winner, no vanquished"**, and the **3 Rs: reconstruction, reconciliation, rehabilitation**. **[Week 5]**
**22.** *From the oil boom to 2014*. The **January coup, the counter-coup and the civil war truncated economic progress**, the military **destroying the economic structure on which the regional political system was built**. In the **1970s** came the **oil boom**, but the government **did not save for rainy days**; a **crunch in the oil market** followed, and Nigeria had already **abandoned agriculture for easy oil money**, so the nation **struggled in the mid-1980s**. **Austerity measures were introduced under the Buhari regime**, and then the **IMF pressured African countries, Nigeria included, to adopt the Structural Adjustment Programme** — austerity **first**, SAP **after**. **SAP further weakened the economy**: the **currency began a downward movement** that has continued since, and SAP **destroyed the manufacturing base** as the economy was **flooded with imported goods** that made local products uncompetitive on **price and quality**. There was **another oil boom in the 1990s**, but Head of State **Sani Abacha looted the treasury** into **foreign banks**. On the **return to democracy in 1999**, GDP growth rose from **0.58% to 6.59%**; under **Obasanjo** Nigeria won **$20 billion of debt relief** and reserves passed **$64 billion**; after **Yar'Adua's death**, under **Goodluck Jonathan** the savings were spent "with reckless abandon" and reserves **fell to $34 billion in 2015**. Then **US shale oil** cut demand: in **2014** the **US, the largest buyer of Nigeria's crude, announced reduced purchases**, and **India replaced the US** as the major buyer, leaving the budget **in deficit** and the country **borrowing to balance it**. Four of the causes: **over-dependence on crude oil as the major source of government revenue**; **corruption**; **insider abuses, non-adherence to the corporate governance code and non-performing loans in the banking sector, with round-tripping in the capital market**; **poor economic policies**; **the spate of violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; and **lack of economic planning/policy direction, untimely implementation of the national budget and unguided utterances**. **[Week 6]**
**23.** *The five constitutional references*. **Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution, Sections 33 to 46** — **fundamental human rights**, which the **African Charter on Human and People's Rights** guarantees are **enforceable in Nigerian courts**. **Chapter Three of the 1999 Constitution (as amended)** — **citizenship**, on the principle that **every country has the right to decide who will be its citizen**. **Section 24** — the **civic duties of every citizen**, six lettered duties **A to F**. **Section 27** — **naturalisation**, on the fulfilment of which a **certificate of grant of citizenship by naturalisation** is issued. The **Seventh Schedule** — where the prescribed **Oath of Allegiance** is set out, which an applicant for registration must have taken. The **five rights the lesson names** are **freedom of religion, freedom of speech, the right to education, the right to freedom of movement and the right to life**. The **lesson's six civic responsibilities** are **obey the law, protection of public property, payment of taxes, rendering national service, loyalty, and respect for others' rights**. **[Weeks 7 & 8]**
**24.** *The reorientation strategies*. The six the deck lists are the **National Orientation Agency (NOA)**, **Operation Feed the Nation**, **Austerity Measures**, **War Against Indiscipline**, **War Against Indiscipline and Corruption**, and **Mass Mobilisation for Self-reliance, Social Justice and Economic Recovery (MAMSER)**. **NOA** — established by **Decree 100 of 1993** by the Federal Government as the **successor of the Directorate for Social Mobilisation – MAMSER**, mandated to **reorient Nigerians towards positive values, patriotism, national unity and development** and to **create and sustain public awareness, understanding and support for government policies, programmes and initiatives**; the deck attaches no criticism to NOA itself, only its general conclusion of **corruption, poor leadership and improper planning**. **OFN** — **1976-80**, under the **military government of General Olusegun Obasanjo**, to **address food security challenges and reduce dependence on food imports**, with **urban dwellers encouraged to garden undeveloped building plots**; criticised because **resources — seedlings, insecticides, tractors — were not provided as promised**, **activities were not effectively performed**, **farmers were sceptical of the new methods**, **administrative officials did not do the job expected of them**, and the **manpower to expand farming did not materialise**. **WAI** — a **mass mobilisation programme of the military dictatorship of Buhari**, to **correct social maladjustments**, promoting **punctuality, respect for queues, environmental cleanliness and adherence to traffic regulations**; it **ended in September 1985**, and its criticism was the **high handedness of the WAI Brigade** — the very thing that made it succeed. **MAMSER** — launched in **1987 on the recommendation of the Public Bureau headed by Samuel Cookey**, to **mobilise and empower Nigerians to participate in nation-building, promote self-reliance, achieve social justice and drive economic recovery**; it was **eventually phased out** and its functions **absorbed into other agencies**, the successor body being the **NOA**. **[Week 9]**

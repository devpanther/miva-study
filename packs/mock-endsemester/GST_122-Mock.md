## Section A — Multiple choice (20)

**1.** Having completed a full first draft of an essay, a writer returns to the introduction to make sure it captures the reader's attention and accurately previews the essay, then moves two body paragraphs into a better order. This activity is:
a) Editing  b) Revision  c) Proofreading  d) Peer review

**2.** A poet writes the theme "harmattan" in the middle of a blank page and surrounds it with loosely associated words and images in no fixed hierarchy. The pre-writing technique is:
a) Mind mapping  b) Clustering  c) Outlining  d) Storyboarding

**3.** Chidi sets a timer for ten minutes and writes continuously without stopping to correct, plan or judge, producing unbroken prose rather than notes. He is using:
a) Brainstorming  b) Freewriting  c) Listing  d) Drafting questions

**4.** Which sequence of post-writing stages is the correct one?
a) Proofreading → editing → revision
b) Editing → revision → proofreading
c) Revision → editing → proofreading
d) Proofreading → revision → editing

**5.** A student's "summary" is one third the length of the original but is stitched together from the original's own sentences, unchanged. Which step of summary writing has she failed?
a) Maintain the structure  b) Condense the information in her own words  c) Eliminate unnecessary details  d) Check for coherence

**6.** In the lesson's report on global poverty, the statement "governments should invest in education and healthcare infrastructure in the worst-affected regions" belongs under:
a) Findings  b) Conclusions  c) Recommendations  d) Methodology

**7.** In the nine steps of letter writing, the step named **closing** requires the writer to:
a) Write "Yours sincerely" and add a signature
b) Summarise the main points and express gratitude or goodwill
c) State briefly the purpose or reason for writing
d) Choose between a formal, informal and business format

**8.** What most clearly separates a summary from a report?
a) A summary is always shorter than a report
b) A summary is derived entirely from one source text and adds nothing, while a report presents the writer's own research, methodology, conclusions and recommendations
c) A summary is informal while a report is formal
d) A report must use graphs, charts and tables while a summary must not

**9.** The three R in SQ3R stand for:
a) Read, Reflect, Review  b) Read, Recite, Review  c) Read, Recall, Revise  d) Read, Record, Reflect

**10.** After skimming a chapter's headings, subheadings and illustration captions, Fatima writes down four questions of her own before reading a single paragraph. She is at the:
a) Survey stage, since she is still working from headings and captions
b) Question stage, since SQ3R's Q is the reader asking, before reading
c) Read stage, since she now has a purpose for reading
d) Review stage, since generating questions consolidates learning

**11.** Emeka shuts his textbook and explains the key points and terms aloud in his own words, writing nothing down. He is at the:
a) Review stage, because he is consolidating what he learned
b) Recite stage, because it is closed-book retrieval in his own words
c) Read stage, because he is going over the material a second time
d) Survey stage, because he is stating the main ideas

**12.** A reader meets the unfamiliar word "obdurate" and works out its meaning from the surrounding sentences without opening a dictionary. She has used:
a) Inference  b) Context clues  c) Explicit meaning  d) Fact-and-opinion assessment

**13.** By which word-formation process was "brunch" formed?
a) Compounding, since two words are merged into one
b) Blending, since parts of two words are fused
c) Clipping, since a single word has been shortened

**14.** "The lecturer will email the results on Friday." The word "email" here illustrates:
a) Derivation  b) Conversion  c) Affixation  d) Borrowing

**15.** Which of these words carries an **inflectional**, not a derivational, suffix?
a) teacher  b) kindness  c) taller  d) modernise

**16.** "NASA" and "ATM" are both formed from the initial letters of a phrase. NASA counts as an acronym rather than an initialism because:
a) It names an organisation rather than a machine
b) It is pronounced as a word instead of being spelled out letter by letter
c) It contains four letters rather than three

**17.** A speaking prompt is best defined as:

**20.** In the sentence "I didn't say you should bring coffee", placing the stress on **say** conveys that:

## Section B — Short answer (4)

**21.** A student finishes a complete first draft the night before submission and announces that she will now "just proofread it". Name the three post-writing stages in their correct order, state what each one examines, and explain why proofreading alone is not enough and why doing it first would waste work.

**22.** Kemi must write a formal letter of complaint to a company whose officer she cannot name, so she opens "Dear Sir/Madam". State the correct closing salutation and the rule behind it, distinguish the step called *closing* from the step called *closing salutation*, and name the one step that ends all three procedures — summary, letter and report.

**23.** Before reading a chapter headed "Renaissance Humanism", Tunde skims its headings, subheadings and illustration captions, then writes four questions of his own. Name the two SQ3R stages he has completed and what each produces, name the stage that comes next and its **two** goals, and then state the word-formation process behind "humanism" and behind "webinar", giving the test that separates them.

**24.** In a mock interview Ada is asked: "Discuss the impact of remote work on Nigerian graduates, with specific examples." She talks for four minutes, gives no examples, drifts into a long story about her cousin's tailoring business, and stops without closing. Name three guidelines she has broken and state what each requires. Then, using "I didn't say you should bring coffee", explain the difference between word stress and sentence stress, and give the meaning produced by stressing **you**.

## Answers

**1. b** — *revision*: the global, first stage of post-writing, which examines content, structure and argument and permits adding, cutting, reordering or rewriting whole paragraphs. Editing (a) is sentence-level work on grammar, style and word choice; proofreading (c) is the last, narrowest sweep for typos, spelling, punctuation and formatting; peer review (d) is what someone else tells you about the draft. **[Week 5]**

**2. b** — *clustering (concept clustering)*: associated words and images grouped around a central theme with no fixed hierarchy, the technique the deck attaches to creative work such as a poem. Mind mapping (a) is the near-miss: it builds a branching hierarchy — centre, branches, twigs — that shows how ideas relate, and suits analytical and argumentative work. **[Week 5]**

**3. b** — *freewriting*: writing continuously for a set time without stopping, correcting or planning, producing continuous unedited prose. Brainstorming (a) produces fragments, lists and branches and can be done in a group; the timer and the unbroken prose are what mark this as freewriting. **[Week 5]**

**4. c** — *order of post-writing*: revise (global — content, structure, argument), then edit (sentence-level — grammar, style, word choice, redundancy), then proofread (surface — typos, spelling, punctuation, formatting). The order is examinable because working small-to-large wastes effort: correcting commas in a paragraph you are about to delete achieves nothing. **[Week 5]**

**5. b** — *condense the information in your own words* (step 4): a summary must restate, not reproduce. Option (a) is wrong because retaining the original's logical order is exactly what step 5 requires — she has obeyed that step, not broken it; the length shows she did eliminate details (c), and copied sentences may still flow (d). **[Week 6]**

**6. c** — *recommendations*: an actionable suggestion prescribing what someone should now do, based on the conclusions. A conclusion (b) interprets what the findings mean ("eradication requires multifaceted intervention"); findings (a) are the evidence itself; methodology (d) is how the data were gathered. Test: if it can be handed to a person as an instruction with a budget attached, it is a recommendation. **[Week 6]**

**7. b** — *closing*: the final **paragraph**, which summarises the main points and expresses gratitude or goodwill. Option (a) is the next step, the **closing salutation** — the sign-off phrase plus signature; (c) is the introduction step and (d) is the "choose the format" step. **[Week 6]**

**8. b** — *summary vs report*: a summary is derived entirely from one source text and adds no new material, no personal opinion and no quotation; a report is generated by the writer's own research and adds methodology, conclusions and recommendations that exist in no source. Length (a) and formality (c) are incidental, and charts are used in a report only "if necessary" (d). **[Week 6]**

**9. b** — *Survey → Question → Read → Recite → Review*: one S, one Q and three stages beginning with R. "Reflect", "Recall", "Revise" and "Record" (a, c, d) are the standard wrong expansions — none of them is in the acronym. **[Week 7]**

**10. b** — *Question stage*: SQ3R's Q is **you asking, not answering**, and the questions are generated from what the survey discovered so that they guide the reading. She has left Survey (a) because she is no longer skimming; she has not begun Read (c), which is the first stage of actual contact with the prose; Review (d) is the final, written consolidation. **[Week 7]**

**11. b** — *Recite*: oral or mental retrieval of key points, words and concepts in your own words, immediately and without the text. Review (a) is written consolidation — summaries, outlines, notes — with text and notes to hand, repeated over time. Test: Recite has no paper; Review produces paper. Running your eyes over the page again (c) is Read repeated, and reinforces nothing. **[Week 7]**

**12. b** — *context clues*: hints within the text that let a reader infer the meaning of an **unfamiliar word** without a dictionary; the skill developed is vocabulary acquisition. Inference (a) is the tempting distractor: it draws a logical conclusion about the **content or the writer's meaning** from explicit details plus background knowledge. Context clues are one route to an inference, not a synonym for it. **[Week 7]**

**13. b** — *blending*: parts of two words are fused — br(eakfast) + (l)unch. Compounding (a) joins two **whole** words, as in *raincoat*; the lesson's prose slips and calls *brunch* a compound, but its bullet list, which is the authoritative one, files it under blending. Clipping (c) shortens a single word (*laboratory* → *lab*); conversion (d) changes word class with no affix. **[Week 8]**

**14. b** — *conversion*: the noun *email* is used as a verb with **no affix added at all**, which is precisely the deck's own example. Derivation (a) and affixation (c) both require an attached prefix or suffix, and there is none here; borrowing (d) would mean the word was taken from another language. **[Week 8]**

**15. c** — *inflectional suffix*: comparative *-er* in *taller* only marks degree and makes no new dictionary word. *Teacher* (a) uses derivational *-er* ("one who teaches") — note that *-er* is ambiguous, which is why this pair is examined; *kindness* (b) and *modernise* (d) are derivational too, forming a noun and a verb respectively. Test: would it earn its own dictionary entry? **[Week 8]**

**16. b** — *acronym vs initialism*: an acronym is pronounced as a word (NASA), an initialism is spelled out letter by letter (ATM, FBI). What the letters stand for (a), how many there are (c) and the language of origin (d) are irrelevant. The lesson itself calls the whole category "acronyms". **[Week 8]**

**17. b** — *speaking prompt*: "questions or statements designed to elicit a spoken response from you", serving as a stimulus to initiate and guide verbal communication in interviews, discussions, language learning and public speaking. A topic (a) is only the subject matter; a prompt additionally carries the specific instructions you are told to identify. (c) is the Introduction guideline and (d) is Organise Your Thoughts. **[Week 9]**

**18. b** — *Understand the Prompt/Question → Organise Your Thoughts → Introduction*: the first three all happen **before you speak**, and understanding precedes organising, which precedes the opening. Any order that puts planning or speaking ahead of comprehension (a, c, d) is wrong. **[Week 9]**

**19. b** — *Balance*: maintaining a balanced perspective by considering different angles or counter-arguments, which demonstrates a nuanced understanding of the topic. Stay Relevant (a) is the near-miss but concerns avoiding unrelated tangents — a counter-argument on the set topic is balance, not a tangent. Elaborate and Expand (c) is depth on your own points; Provide Examples and Evidence (d) is concrete support. **[Week 9]**

**20. b** — *sentence stress*: stressing **say** shifts the denial onto the verb — the speaker implied, wrote or hinted it, but did not say it. (a) is what stressing *I* conveys, (c) what stressing *coffee* conveys, and (d) what stressing *you* conveys. **[Week 9]**

**21.** *Post-writing stages*: the three stages, in order, are **revision**, **editing** and **proofreading**. **Revision** is global: it examines content, structure and argument — whether the introduction captures attention and accurately previews the essay, whether the paragraphs sit in the right order, whether the argument is coherent, whether transitions and the conclusion do their work; whole paragraphs may be added, cut, reordered or rewritten. **Editing** is sentence-level: grammar, sentence structure, word choice, style and consistency, eliminating redundancies and refining terminology; nothing structural changes. **Proofreading** is the surface sweep and the last one: typographical errors, misspellings and punctuation, plus formatting (font size, margins, spacing) and citations and references. Proofreading alone is not enough because it cannot detect a weak thesis, a paragraph in the wrong place or an incoherent argument — it is the narrowest of the three, not "checking the essay". Doing it first wastes work because a paragraph whose commas you have just corrected may be deleted or rewritten at the revision stage; the effort is thrown away. Big to small, in that order. (Peer review normally sends you back to revision, not to proofreading; publishing or submitting marks the end of the writing process.) **[Week 5]**

**22.** *Letter writing*: the correct closing salutation is **"Yours faithfully"**. The rule is that **"Yours sincerely" goes with a named recipient** ("Dear Mr Azeez"), while **"Yours faithfully" goes with an impersonal salutation** ("Dear Sir/Madam") — Kemi cannot name the officer, so the impersonal opening forces the faithful close. The two steps differ as follows: **closing** is the final **paragraph** of the letter, which summarises the main points and expresses gratitude or goodwill; the **closing salutation** is the **sign-off phrase plus the signature** ("Yours faithfully, / Kemi Adeola"). One is content, the other is form. The step that ends all three procedures is **review and edit** — the final check for grammar, clarity, coherence and accuracy that also confirms the piece achieves its intended purpose; it is step 8 of summary writing, step 9 of letter writing and step 11 of report writing. As a formal letter, Kemi's should also carry her own address and the date, and the recipient's address, above the salutation. **[Week 6]**

**23.** *SQ3R and word formation*: Tunde has completed **Survey** and **Question**. **Survey** is the fast, whole-text skim of headings, subheadings, images and captions carried out before any detailed reading; it produces an overview — a sense of the text's structure and main ideas, nothing more. **Question** turns those survey findings into the reader's **own** questions before reading begins ("this is where you start asking questions, not answering the questions from the passage"); it produces a set of guiding questions that direct attention to the most critical information. The stage that comes next is **Read**, and its two goals are (i) to **read in order to answer the questions you generated** and (ii) to **engage with the material for a fuller understanding** — the second goal is the one students forget when they treat Read as mere question-hunting. On word formation: **"humanism"** is formed by **derivation** (affixation) — the derivational suffix *-ism* is attached to the whole base *human* to create a new noun. **"Webinar"** is formed by **blending** — *web* and *(sem)inar* are fused, and only **parts** of the source words survive. The test that separates them: in derivation an affix is attached to a base that remains whole and recognisable; in blending neither source word survives intact, because fragments of two words are fused into one. **[Weeks 7–8]**

**24.** *Responding to speaking prompts, and stress*: Ada breaks at least three guidelines. **Provide Examples and Evidence** — she was explicitly instructed to give specific examples, and concrete examples are what illustrate ideas and make a response convincing; note that guideline 1, **Understand the Prompt/Question**, requires her to identify the prompt's specific instructions, and "with specific examples" was one. **Stay Relevant** — the tailoring story is an unrelated tangent, and tangents dilute the impact of the answer; balance would have meant giving another angle on remote work, not another subject. **Conclude Effectively** — a response needs a brief conclusion that summarises the main points and reinforces the key message, and she simply stopped. (**Manage Time Effectively** is a fair fourth, since four minutes of which much is tangent is poor use of the allotted time.) On stress: **word stress** is emphasis on a **syllable within a single word** — as in *demonstrate*, where shifting the stressed syllable alters pronunciation and meaning. **Sentence stress** is emphasis on a **word within a sentence**, altering the meaning, rhythm and pronunciation of the whole utterance without changing a single word of it. In "I didn't say you should bring coffee", stressing **you** means that the speaker did say someone should bring coffee, but **someone other than the listener** — the denial lands on the person, not on the request. **[Week 9]**

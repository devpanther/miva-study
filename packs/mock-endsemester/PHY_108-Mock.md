## Section B — Short answer (4)

**21.** State Kirchhoff's voltage law and name the conservation principle it rests on, then give the two sign rules used in traversing a loop. In one row of the table R₃ + R₄ = 9.5 kΩ, R₅ = 5 kΩ and E₁ = 15 V, with the ammeters reading I₁ = 1.215 mA and I₃ = 0.944 mA: test loop 1, find the percentage difference and comment. State also what a negative I₂ means.

**22.** Write the charging and the discharging equations for a capacitor and state what each gives at t = 0. For each case, state exactly what is plotted to obtain a straight line, and what its gradient and intercept represent. A discharge run with R = 20 kΩ gives a ln V against t line of gradient −0.0250 s⁻¹: find τ and the experimental capacitance, and state what happens to τ if R is doubled.

**23.** State Boyle's law in full. A student plots P against V, obtains a smooth falling curve and claims the law is verified: explain why that is not a verification and state what should be plotted instead, together with the gradient and the expected intercept. Then, with ρ = 13 600 kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s², P_a = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa and a uniform bore of A = 1 cm², compute the pressure of the trapped air at h = 150 mm and check the product PV against P₀V₀ = 2.026 J when the air column then measures 16.70 cm.

**24.** In a method-of-mixtures run: m_s = 40.0 g, T_s = 98.0 °C; copper calorimeter m_c = 50.0 g with c_c = 387 J/kg·°C; water m_w = 160.0 g with c_w = 4186 J/kg·°C; T_r = 20.0 °C and T_f = 24.0 °C. Compute the specific heat of the solid, showing the conservation-of-energy step. State what the answer would have been had the calorimeter term been omitted, and say which single measurement is the weakest in the experiment.

## Answers

**1. B** — *the basis of KVL*: KVL is conservation of **energy** applied round a **closed loop** (Σε = ΣIR, or ΣV = 0); KCL is conservation of **charge** applied at a **node**. A and D swap the two laws' halves. **[Week 5]**

**2. A** — *the two sign conventions*: the p.d. across a resistance is positive if it is traversed in the same direction as the current flowing through it, negative otherwise; the emf is positive if the battery is traversed from its positive terminal to its negative terminal, negative otherwise. The loop must also be traversed in one consistent sense throughout. **[Week 5]**

**3. A** — *kΩ × mA = V*: 0.944 mA × 9.5 kΩ = 8.968 V and 1.215 mA × 5 kΩ = 6.075 V, giving **15.04 V ≈ 15.0 V**, which is E₁ and so verifies KVL round loop 1. D is the trap of thinking kΩ × mA gives millivolts; it gives volts directly. **[Week 5]**

**4. C** — *systematic vs random*: the loop equations (2) and (3) assume ideal sources, so the neglected internal resistance biases every computed current the same way. Parallax and reaction time are random and average out, and rounding at three decimals is far too small to matter. **[Week 5]**

**5. A** — *the charging law*: V(t) = V₀(1 − e^(−t/τ)); at t = 0 it gives V = 0, and at t = τ, V = 0.632V₀, i.e. **63.2% gained**. B is the discharging law, C quotes the 36.8% that belongs to discharging, and D confuses τ with the ~5τ needed for effectively full charge. **[Week 6]**

**6. B** — *linearising the charging curve*: from V₀ − V = V₀e^(−t/τ), ln(V₀ − V) = ln V₀ − t/τ, so plot **ln(V₀ − V) on the y-axis against t**, gradient **−1/τ**, intercept **ln V₀**. A is the discharging linearisation; D attaches the gradient to τ instead of 1/τ. **[Week 6]**

**7. B** — *C = τ/R with R in ohms*: C = 22 s ÷ (22 × 10³ Ω) = 1.0 × 10⁻³ F = **1000 μF**. D comes from using R = 22 rather than 22 000 Ω, and C from multiplying (τR) instead of dividing. **[Week 6]**

**8. B** — *discharge the capacitor completely before each trial*: the run must start from V = 0. Starting part-charged shifts the time origin, so the voltage reaches 0.632V₀ sooner and the ln(V₀ − V) line is displaced; **τ is underestimated** and C with it. A is true in itself — τ really is independent of V₀ — but it is the wrong starting *charge*, not the wrong supply voltage, that is at fault. **[Week 6]**

**9. A** — *the discharging law*: V(t) = V₀e^(−t/τ), with no "1 −". At t = 0 the voltmeter reads maximum, and at t = τ, V = 0.3679V₀, so **36.8% remains**. D confuses τ with the ~5τ for effectively complete discharge; the capacitor is never mathematically empty. **[Week 7]**

**10. C** — *order of operations at the bench*: charge first with the key ON until the voltmeter is steady at ≈ the supply voltage (this fixes V₀), then **open the key and start the stopwatch at that same instant**, so that t = 0 corresponds to the maximum reading. A is the charging procedure. **[Week 7]**

**11. B** — *gradient of ln V against t is −1/τ*: τ = −1/(−0.0250 s⁻¹) = **40.0 s**. A attaches the minus sign to τ, which is a time and must be positive; D takes the reciprocal of 0.04 wrongly; C mistakes the gradient itself for τ. **[Week 7]**

**12. A** — *voltmeter loading*: the meter sits in parallel with C and so offers a **second discharge path**. The effective resistance is R∥R_v, which is **less than R**, so the capacitor empties faster, the measured τ is too small and C = τ/R is too small. Leakage through the dielectric acts in the same direction. This is why a voltmeter is built with very high resistance. **[Week 7]**

**13. B** — *the full statement*: both conditions must appear — **constant temperature** and a **definite (fixed) quantity** of gas — with P ∝ 1/V. A is Charles's law, C is Gay-Lussac's law, and D drops both conditions, which makes the statement false as soon as the gas leaks or warms. **[Week 8]**

**14. C** — *P_f = P_a + ρgh, with h in metres*: ρgh = 13 600 × 9.8 × 0.200 = 26 656 Pa; P_f = 101 300 + 26 656 = 127 956 Pa ≈ **1.28 × 10⁵ Pa**. A gives ρgh alone and forgets that the gas already stood at atmospheric pressure when h = 0; B subtracts instead of adding; D leaves h in millimetres, a factor of 1000. **[Week 8]**

**15. B** — *only the linear plot verifies the law*: P against V is a rectangular hyperbola, and no eye can distinguish a hyperbola from any other falling curve, so A proves nothing. **P against 1/V is a straight line through the origin** with **gradient PV = the constant** (2.026 J for the deck's gas). Turning the axes round, 1/V against P is also a straight line, of gradient 1/(PV) = 0.494 J⁻¹ — C is right about the gradient but wrong to call it a curve. **[Week 8]**

**16. B** — *a non-zero intercept means a systematic error*: plotting against ρgh alone shifts every point left by P_a, so the line no longer passes through the origin and cuts the 1/V axis at P_a/(PV). Leakage (A) makes PV fall steadily down the table, warming (C) makes PV drift upward and the line curve, and parallax (D) gives random scatter about the line, not a displaced intercept. **[Week 8]**

**17. B** — *definition and SI unit*: the heat energy required to raise the temperature of **unit mass (1 kg)** of the material by **1 °C**, in **J/kg·°C** (equal numerically to J/kg·K). A defines the heat capacity of the object, mc, in J/°C; it belongs to the sample, not the material. **[Week 9]**

**18. A** — *which differences appear*: the solid falls from T_s to T_f, so its drop is **(T_s − T_f)**; the water and calorimeter rise from T_r to T_f, so their rise is **(T_f − T_r)**. **(T_s − T_r) appears nowhere.** The ordering T_s > T_f > T_r keeps both differences positive; a negative c_s means a difference was subtracted backwards. **[Week 9]**

**19. A** — *what is plotted and what the gradient means*: since Q_gained = c_s · m_s(T_s − T_f), the plot is y = c_s x, a **straight line through the origin whose gradient is c_s in J/kg·°C**. D confuses c_s with the sample's heat capacity; B puts water's specific heat where the solid's belongs. A non-zero intercept signals heat lost in transfer or through the jacket, or a thermometer zero error. **[Week 9]**

**20. B** — *the calorimeter term is not optional*: the vessel warms with the water and absorbs real energy, so dropping m_c c_c understates the heat gained and pushes **c_s down** (391 instead of 410 J/kg·°C on the deck's own data, about 5%). D gives the right direction by the wrong route — that is the mechanism of heat lost during transfer, which inflates the denominator instead. **[Week 9]**

**21.** *Statement, sign rules and a loop check*: **KVL** — around any closed path the **sum of the voltage drops across all the branches in the loop equals the sum of the emfs of the batteries in the loop**; equivalently the **algebraic sum of all voltages round a closed loop is zero** (Σε = ΣIR, ΣV = 0). It rests on the **conservation of energy**: a charge carried once round the loop returns to the same potential. **Sign rules**: the p.d. across a resistance is **positive if the resistance is traversed in the same direction as the current through it, negative otherwise**; the emf of a battery is **positive if the battery is traversed from its positive terminal to its negative terminal, negative otherwise**. The loop must be traversed in one consistent sense throughout. **Loop 1**: I₃(R₃ + R₄) + I₁R₅ = (0.944 mA)(9.5 kΩ) + (1.215 mA)(5 kΩ) = **8.968 V + 6.075 V = 15.04 V** (kΩ × mA = V, no conversion needed). Compared with E₁ = 15.00 V, the percentage difference is |15.04 − 15.00|/15.00 × 100% = **0.27% ≈ 0.3%**, far inside the conventional **5%** tolerance, so **KVL is verified** for this loop; the small residue is experimental — ammeter zero error, contact and lead resistance, resistor heating and the neglected internal resistance of the supplies. A **negative I₂** is **not an error**: it means the actual direction of the current in that branch is opposite to the direction assumed when the diagram was labelled. The magnitude stands, and the sign must be carried unchanged into I₁ + I₂ = I₃. **[Week 5]**

**22.** *Charging and discharging compared, with τ and C*: **Charging** — V(t) = **V₀(1 − e^(−t/τ))**; at **t = 0, V = 0**, and timing starts when the switch key is **closed**. Linearised: ln(V₀ − V) = ln V₀ − t/τ, so plot **ln(V₀ − V) (y) against t (x)**. **Discharging** — V(t) = **V₀e^(−t/τ)**; at **t = 0, V = V₀ (maximum)**, and timing starts when the key is **opened** after the capacitor is fully charged. Linearised: ln V = ln V₀ − t/τ, so plot **ln V (y) against t (x)**. In **both** cases the **gradient is −1/τ** (so τ = −1/gradient) and the **intercept is ln V₀**; the practical difference is only that charging requires V₀ − V to be formed first. **Calculation**: τ = −1/(−0.0250 s⁻¹) = **40.0 s**; C = τ/R = 40.0/(2.0 × 10⁴ Ω) = **2.0 × 10⁻³ F = 2000 μF**. Since **τ = RC**, **doubling R at fixed C doubles τ to 80.0 s** — the capacitor discharges (or charges) more slowly. τ does **not** depend on V₀. **[Weeks 6 & 7]**

**23.** *Boyle's law, the right plot, and the PV check*: **Statement** — at **constant temperature**, the pressure of a **definite (fixed) quantity** of an ideal gas is **inversely proportional to its volume**: PV = constant, P ∝ 1/V, P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Both conditions are part of the law. **Why P against V is not a verification** — it is a **rectangular hyperbola (an isotherm)**, falling steeply and approaching both axes without meeting either, and by eye a hyperbola cannot be told from any other decreasing curve, so the plot is consistent with the law but does not test it. **What to plot instead** — **P (y) against 1/V (x)**, which Boyle's law predicts to be a **straight line through the origin** of **gradient PV, the constant** (2.026 J here). On the deck's own axes, **1/V (y) against P (x)**, the **gradient is 1/(PV) = 0.494 J⁻¹** and the intercept is again **zero**; a **non-zero intercept is a systematic error**, most often atmospheric pressure omitted from P. **Calculation** — h = 150 mm = 0.150 m, so ρgh = 13 600 × 9.8 × 0.150 = **19 992 Pa** and P = P_a + ρgh = 101 300 + 19 992 = **121 292 Pa ≈ 1.21 × 10⁵ Pa**. With A = 1 cm², V = ℓA = 16.70 cm × 1 cm² = 16.70 cm³ = **1.670 × 10⁻⁵ m³**. Then PV = 121 292 × 1.670 × 10⁻⁵ = **2.03 J**, which reproduces P₀V₀ = **2.026 J**; the product is constant, so **Boyle's law is verified**. **[Week 8]**

**24. c_s = 931 J/kg·°C** — *method of mixtures, with the calorimeter term kept*: The conservation-of-energy step is **heat lost by the solid = heat gained by the water and the calorimeter**: m_s c_s(T_s − T_f) = (m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_f − T_r). Converting every mass to kilograms: m_w c_w = 0.1600 × 4186 = **669.8 J/°C**; m_c c_c = 0.0500 × 387 = **19.4 J/°C**; total heat capacity = **689.1 J/°C**. Heat gained = 689.1 × (24.0 − 20.0) = 689.1 × 4.0 °C = **2756 J**. On the solid's side, m_s(T_s − T_f) = 0.0400 × (98.0 − 24.0) = 0.0400 × 74.0 = **2.96 kg·°C**. Hence **c_s = 2756/2.96 = 931 J/kg·°C**, a value close to aluminium. **Omitting the calorimeter term** would give 669.8 × 4.0/2.96 = **905 J/kg·°C**, about **3% too low**, because the heat absorbed by the vessel would be credited to nothing. **The weakest measurement is the temperature rise (T_f − T_r) = 4.0 °C**: it is a small difference read on a thermometer graduated to about 0.1 °C, so its fractional uncertainty is the largest in the calculation and passes straight into c_s — which is why the block should be as massive, and the water as little, as the calorimeter allows. **[Week 9]**

## Answers

**1. B** — *blending*: only **parts** of the two words survive (*br-* + *-unch*). **A** is the classic slip, and the lesson's own prose commits it — but its bullet list, the authoritative one, files *brunch* under blending, and compounding needs both words **whole**, as in *raincoat*. **C** would require a source language other than English. **D** would require a change of word class with nothing added or removed at all.

**2. C** — *conversion*: the word class changes (noun → verb) with **no affix attached**, which is exactly how the lesson defines it. **A** and **B** both require an added prefix or suffix, and nothing has been added. **D** needs a second whole word merged in.

**3. A** — *derivation*: the lesson's definition is "adds a derivational suffix to create a new word", and *teacher* is its own example. **B** is excluded because an affix *was* added, and conversion adds nothing. **C** needs fragments of two words. **D** needs a word imported from another language.

**4. D** — *un-* is negation, so *unadulterated* means "**not** adulterated" — pure, unmixed. **A** is the sense of *re-*, **B** of *pre-*, **C** of *mis-*. Each is a real prefix; none of them is this one. Here the shortest option is the correct one.

**5. B** — *academic achievement*: the lesson says students with a strong vocabulary perform better in academic settings, can **comprehend and analyse complex texts more easily**, and that a robust vocabulary is **particularly beneficial in language arts, literature and standardized tests**. **A**, intellectual growth, is about meanings, contexts and usage stimulating critical thinking. **C**, professional success, is about conveying ideas clearly and persuasively and about writing. **D**, reading and writing proficiency, is the general tie between vocabulary and comprehension — the standardized-tests clause is what pins this item to academic achievement.

**6. D** — *borrowing*: the lesson's definition is that borrowing "introduces words from other languages", with *sushi* as its example. **A** would need two source words cut down and fused. **B** would need two whole English words merged. **C** would need a phrase reduced to its initial letters.

**7. A** — the lesson's two bullets are exact: compounding "involves **merging words**" (*raincoat* = *rain* + *coat*, both intact), while blending "combines **parts** of words to form a new one" (*brunch* = *br(eakfast)* + *(l)unch*). The test is whether you can still see both complete words. **B** is false — neither process is defined by word class. **C** and **D** invent rules about spelling and about source languages that the lesson never states; **D** is the longest option and is wrong.

**8. C** — *metacognition*: "thinking about one's own thinking processes, including awareness and understanding of one's cognitive abilities", and the lesson's usage example is a teacher prompting students to reflect on their problem-solving strategies before discussing answers. **A** synthesis is combining different ideas into a new integrated understanding; **B** exegesis is critical explanation or interpretation, especially of a text; **D** elocution is the skill of clear, expressive speech.

**9. B** — *syllogism*: "a form of reasoning in which a conclusion is drawn from two given or assumed propositions (premises); a common form of deductive reasoning". **A** epistemology is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, origin and scope of knowledge; **C** semiotics is the study of signs and symbols; **D** didacticism is the practice of emphasising moral or educational messages.

**10. D** — *etymological analysis*: the case study's method is to investigate the origin of each term and "determine if they result from affixation, compounding, conversion, derivation, blending, or other linguistic phenomena". **A** only assembles the term list. **B**, contextual usage, looks at how the terms are used and at shifts in meaning, which comes after the process has been identified. **C**, expert interviews, gathers insight into intentional creation and adoption, not the formation process itself.

**11. A** — the lesson names **engaging in conversations, discussions and debates** to apply and reinforce newly learned words. **B** is how new words are *met*, not applied. **C** is dictionaries, thesauruses and language-learning apps. **D** is word games and language-related activities. All four are genuine named strategies — the question tests which one *productive use in argument* belongs to.

**12. C** — *epistemology*: the branch of philosophy concerned with the **nature, origin and scope of knowledge**, and the lesson's own usage example is a doctoral thesis on the epistemology of scientific breakthroughs. **A** semiotics studies signs and symbols; **B** pedagogy is the method and practice of teaching; **D** exegesis is critical interpretation of a text.

**13.** *The seven word-formation processes.* **Affixation** — prefixes and suffixes modify existing words (*unadulterated*, *correctly*), altering meaning and function while expanding vocabulary. **Compounding** — merging words to form new ones (*raincoat*). **Conversion** — altering a word's grammatical category without affixes (*email* used as a verb). **Derivation** — adding a derivational suffix to create a new word (*teacher*). **Blending** — combining parts of words to form a new one (*brunch*). **Acronyms** — taking the initial letters of a phrase or title to create a new word (*NASA*). **Borrowing** — introducing words from other languages (*sushi*). *Test separating conversion from derivation*: look for an affix. Derivation **adds** a derivational suffix; conversion adds **nothing at all** and works purely by moving the word into a new grammatical category. If any material has been attached, the process cannot be conversion.

**14.** *The brunch contradiction.* The **bullet list is right and the prose is wrong**: *brunch* is a **blend**. The prose paragraph says compounding "involves merging words to form new ones, such as 'raincoat' and 'brunch'", but the bullet list on the same sheet gives the two processes separately — "Compounding involves merging words (e.g., 'raincoat')" and "Blending combines parts of words to form a new one (e.g., 'brunch')" — and the bullet list is the authoritative statement. *The test*: ask whether **both source words survive whole**. If they do, it is compounding; if only **parts** of them survive, it is blending. *Applied*: **raincoat** = *rain* + *coat*, and both words are complete and still visible, so it is a **compound**. **Brunch** = *br(eakfast)* + *(l)unch*, and material has been cut out of both, so it is a **blend**. If an exam question quotes the prose sentence, answer **blending** and, if there is room, note that the lesson's own bullet list files it there.

**15.** *Vocabulary expansion.* **Definition**: the process of **increasing one's repertoire of words and expressions in a language** — a crucial aspect of language development and communication skills, and a fundamental part of language development contributing to effective communication, cognitive growth, academic success and overall linguistic proficiency. **The four strategies**: **reading widely and regularly**, which exposes you to new words in different contexts; **using vocabulary-building tools** such as dictionaries, thesauruses and language-learning apps; **engaging in conversations, discussions and debates** to apply and reinforce newly learned words; and **playing word games and participating in language-related activities**. **Benefit headings (any three)**: **communication skills** — expressing yourself more precisely and effectively, reducing the likelihood of misunderstandings and misinterpretations; **intellectual growth** — learning new words involves understanding their meanings, contexts and usage, which stimulates critical thinking; **academic achievement** — complex texts are comprehended and analysed more easily, especially in language arts, literature and standardized tests; **professional success** — ideas conveyed clearly and persuasively, and better writing; **cultural competence** — exposure to different cultures and ideas, and appreciation of linguistic diversity; **lifelong learning** — expansion continues beyond formal education; **reading and writing proficiency** — vocabulary underlies reading comprehension, and choosing the right words enhances the quality and impact of written expression.

## Section B — Short answer (3)

**13.** Name the seven word-formation processes the lesson lists, give one example of each, and state the one test that separates conversion from derivation.

**14.** Starting from the base word **form**, build four new words using derivational affixes. For each, name the affix, say whether it is a prefix or a suffix, give the word class it produces and the meaning it adds. Then state why *forms* and *formed* do not count as new words.

**15.** Define vocabulary expansion as the lesson defines it, name the four strategies the lesson gives for achieving it, and name three of the benefit headings with one line on each.

## Answers

**1. b** — *blending*: only **parts** of the two words survive (*br-* + *-unch*). (a) is the classic slip, and the lesson's own prose commits it — compounding needs both words **whole**, as in *raincoat*; (c) would require a source language other than English; (d) requires a change of word class with no material added at all.
**2. c** — *conversion*: the word class changes (noun → verb) with **no affix attached**. (a) and (b) both require an added prefix or suffix, and nothing has been added; (d) needs a second whole word merged in.
**3. b** — *derivation*: a derivational suffix creates a **new word**, here shifting a verb to a person-noun. (a) is the standard error — inflection (*teaches, taught, teaching*) marks grammar and creates no new lexeme; (c) is excluded because an affix was added; (d) needs fragments of two words.
**4. b** — *prefixes and their meanings*: *un-* is negation, so *unadulterated* = "not adulterated", i.e. pure, unmixed. (a) is *re-*, (c) is *pre-*, (d) is *mis-* — each a real prefix, none of them this one.
**5. b** — *acronym vs initialism*: *NASA* is said as a word; *FBI*, *ATM* and *UN* are spelled out letter by letter and are initialisms. Choosing any of the others treats "made of initial letters" as sufficient, which is the definition of the wider category, not of an acronym.
**6. a** — *borrowing*: a word imported from another language, retaining its foreign form. (b) coinage invents a word from nothing, which is not what happened; (c) blending needs two source words; (d) needs a phrase reduced to its initial letters.
**7. b** — *compounding*: *black* + *board*, both words intact. *Smog* (smoke + fog), *motel* (motor + hotel) and *brunch* (breakfast + lunch) are all blends in which material has been cut out.
**8. b** — *class-changing suffixes*: *-ify* forms verbs meaning "make or cause to become" (*simplify, clarify, purify*). (a) *-ity* gives an abstract noun (*simplicity*); (c) *-ness* also gives a noun (*simpleness*); (d) *-ly* gives an adverb (*simply*). Each wrong option is a real suffix attached to this very base, so the error being tested is confusing what class a suffix produces.
**9. b** — *-able*: "able to be Ved" → *readable*. (a) *-ive* gives "tending to" (*creative*); (c) *-ist* gives a person-noun (*novelist*); (d) *-ous* gives "full of / characterised by" (*dangerous*). All are adjective- or noun-forming, but only *-able* carries the passive-capability meaning asked for.
**10. a** — *derivational vs inflectional*: *-er* in *teacher* makes a new lexeme with its own dictionary entry; *-s* in *teaches* only marks third-person singular. (b) reverses the two; (c) *-er* and *-est* are both inflectional (comparative and superlative), so neither is derivational; (d) reverses them again — *books* is inflectional plural, *bookish* is derivational.
**11. c** — *strategies for vocabulary expansion*: the lesson names engaging in conversations, discussions and **debates** to apply and reinforce newly learned words. (a) is how new words are *met*, not applied; (b) is dictionaries, thesauruses and apps; (d) is word games and language activities. All four are genuine named strategies — the question tests which one an act of *productive use in argument* belongs to.
**12. b** — *academic lexicon*: epistemology is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, origin and scope of knowledge. (a) semiotics studies signs and symbols; (c) pedagogy is the method and practice of teaching; (d) exegesis is critical interpretation of a text.
**13.** *Word formation processes*: **affixation** — attaching a prefix or suffix to an existing base (*unadulterated*, *correctly*); **compounding** — merging two whole words (*raincoat*); **conversion** — changing grammatical category with no affix (*email* as a verb); **derivation** — adding a derivational affix to create a new word (*teacher*); **blending** — fusing parts of two words (*brunch*); **acronyms** — forming a word from the initial letters of a phrase or title (*NASA*); **borrowing** — taking a word from another language (*sushi*). *Test*: look for an affix. Derivation adds one; conversion adds nothing and relies entirely on the new syntactic position (*a run* / *to run*). If any material has been attached, the process cannot be conversion.
**14.** *Derivational affixation applied*: **re-** + *form* → *reform*, a **prefix**, produces a verb (also a noun), meaning "form again / change for the better"; *form* + **-al** → *formal*, a **suffix**, produces an adjective, "relating to outward form or convention"; *formal* + **-ity** → *formality*, a **suffix**, produces an abstract noun, "the state or quality of being formal"; *formal* + **-ise** → *formalise*, a **suffix**, produces a verb, "make formal"; (*form* + **-ation** → *formation*, noun, "the act or result of forming", is equally acceptable). *Forms* and *formed* do not count because *-s* and *-ed* are **inflectional**: they mark number and tense only, produce no change of word class and no new dictionary entry — the lexeme is still *form*.
**15.** *Vocabulary expansion*: the process of increasing one's repertoire of words and expressions in a language; a fundamental part of language development that supports effective communication, cognitive growth, academic success and overall linguistic proficiency. *Four strategies*: reading widely and regularly, so new words are met in different contexts; using vocabulary-building tools such as dictionaries, thesauruses and language-learning apps; engaging in conversations, discussions and debates to apply and reinforce new words; playing word games and taking part in language-related activities. *Benefit headings* (any three): **communication skills** — precise expression, fewer misunderstandings and misinterpretations; **intellectual growth** — learning words means learning meanings, contexts and usage, which stimulates critical thinking; **academic achievement** — complex texts are comprehended and analysed more easily, especially in language arts, literature and standardized tests; **professional success** — ideas conveyed clearly and persuasively, and better writing; **cultural competence** — exposure to other cultures and appreciation of linguistic diversity; **lifelong learning** — expansion continues beyond formal education; **reading and writing proficiency** — vocabulary underlies comprehension, and word choice determines the quality and impact of writing.

## Section A — Multiple choice (12)

**1.** A student claims to have isolated a single magnetic north pole. The strongest objection is:
A. it would be attracted to the Earth's magnetic pole and could not be held still
B. north and south poles always have equal strength, so one alone would violate conservation of energy
C. every line of B is a closed loop, so the flux through a surface enclosing the pole would be non-zero, contradicting ΦB = 0
D. a pole is a region of a magnet rather than a point, and cannot be detached from the metal

**2.** A flat coil, free to turn and initially at rest, is held in a uniform field with the **plane of the coil containing B**, then released. It will:
A. remain at rest, since the torque vanishes when the plane of the coil contains B
B. turn until its normal lies along B, overshoot, and oscillate about that orientation
C. turn until its normal lies along B and stop there, the torque having brought it to rest
D. turn to the orientation of maximum torque and stay there, that being the most stable position

**3.** A coil of 200 turns, each of area 12 cm², carries 0.50 A. Its magnetic dipole moment is:
A. 1.2×10³ A·m²
B. 6.0×10⁻⁴ A·m²
C. 1.2 A·m²
D. 0.12 A·m²

**4.** For a dipole in a uniform field, the orientation at which the torque is greatest is also the orientation at which:
A. the potential energy is zero
B. the potential energy is least
C. the potential energy is greatest
D. the flux through the loop is greatest

**5.** A dipole of moment 0.80 A·m² lies in a uniform field of 0.15 T. The work that must be done on it to turn it from alignment (θ = 0) to θ = 90° is:
A. 0.24 J
B. 0.19 J
C. 0.12 J
D. zero

**6.** A circular loop lies flat on a table, the current running **clockwise as seen from above**. Its magnetic dipole moment points:
A. vertically downward, into the table
B. vertically upward, out of the table
C. horizontally, in the direction of the current where it crosses the near edge of the loop
D. clockwise, following the current round the loop

**7.** The field 5.0 cm from a long straight wire is measured as 3.0×10⁻⁵ T. The current in the wire is:
A. 750 A
B. 3.8 A

D. 7.5 A

**8.** A solenoid carrying a fixed current produces 8.0 mT inside it. The winding is then stretched to twice its original length, no turns being added or removed. The interior field becomes:
A. 8.0 mT
B. 4.0 mT
C. 16 mT
D. 2.0 mT

**9.** The magnetic moment an electron gets from its orbital motion is **antiparallel** to its orbital angular momentum because:
A. the g-factor for orbital motion is negative

**10.** A small bar magnet is released at rest at the centre of a large coil pair producing a highly **uniform** field, its axis initially at 45° to the field. It will:

**12.** A current loop is held in a uniform field in the orientation for which its potential energy is +μB. Then:

## Section B — Short answer (3)

**13.** Define a magnetic dipole and give the **two** models of one, saying where they agree and where they differ. State the formula for the magnetic dipole moment of a coil, including its direction and its unit, and explain what "there are no magnetic monopoles" implies both for the shape of magnetic field lines and for what happens when a bar magnet is cut in half.

**14.** State the torque on and the potential energy of a magnetic dipole in a uniform field, defining the angle precisely. Then explain three things: why the orientation of greatest torque is the orientation of zero energy; why the torque vanishes at **two** orientations of which only one is an equilibrium worth the name; and why the dipole turns but does not translate.

**15.** A rectangular coil of 60 turns, 5.0 cm × 3.0 cm, carries 4.0 A in a uniform field of 0.20 T. Find (i) its magnetic dipole moment, (ii) the torque on it when the plane of the coil contains B, (iii) its potential energy when the normal makes 120° with B, and (iv) the work done **by the field** in bringing it from that orientation into alignment. Then find the field inside a 20 cm long solenoid of 400 turns carrying the same 4.0 A, and comment on how it compares with the 0.20 T used above.

## Answers

**1. C** — *No magnetic monopoles; Gauss's law for magnetism.* An isolated pole would be a source at which field lines begin, so a closed surface drawn round it would have net outward flux — and the whole of magnetostatics rests on ΦB = 0, the statement that B has no sources or sinks. A is a practical inconvenience, not a physical objection, and would apply equally to an ordinary magnet. B invokes the wrong conservation law: nothing about energy forbids a monopole. D confuses the *geometry* of a magnet with the *reason* poles cannot be separated — a pole is not a piece of metal at all, but the place where lines emerge, and cutting the magnet simply creates two new emergence faces.

**2. B** — *Stable equilibrium plus inertia gives oscillation, not arrest.* With the plane containing B the normal is at 90° to the field, so the torque is at its **maximum**, not zero; the coil accelerates towards θ = 0, arrives there with kinetic energy equal to μB, and swings past. A is the topic's central error — the angle in sin θ is measured from the **normal**, so "plane contains B" is θ = 90°, not θ = 0. C forgets that the torque brings the coil to the minimum of energy, not to rest; something must dissipate the energy before it settles. D confuses maximum torque with stability, when in fact maximum torque is the orientation furthest from equilibrium.

**3. D** — *μ = NIA, with the area converted to m².* A = 12 cm² = 12×10⁻⁴ m², so μ = (200)(0.50)(1.2×10⁻³) = **0.12 A·m²**. A leaves the area in cm² (factor 10⁴). B drops N and gives the moment of a single turn. C uses 1 cm² = 10⁻³ m², a factor of ten in the conversion — the commonest quiet slip, because the answer still looks reasonable.

**4. A** — *τ = μB sin θ and U = −μB cos θ peak in complementary places.* Maximum torque is at θ = 90°, where cos θ = 0 and therefore U = 0. B and C put the energy at an extremum, which happens at θ = 0 and 180° — exactly where the torque **vanishes**; the two errors are mirror images of each other. D is wrong twice: flux is greatest at θ = 0, and that is where the torque is zero, not greatest.

**5. C** — *W = ΔU = μB(cos θ₁ − cos θ₂).* μB = (0.80)(0.15) = 0.12 J. U(0°) = −0.12 J, U(90°) = 0, so the work is 0 − (−0.12) = **0.12 J**. A is 2μB, the work for a full **flip** to 180° — the right formula applied to the wrong final angle. B is τ_max × (π/2) = 0.19 J, treating the torque as constant through the turn when it varies as sin θ. D reads "U = 0 at 90°" as "no work", forgetting the initial energy was −μB.

**6. A** — *Right-hand rule: curl the fingers along the current, the thumb gives μ.* Curling the right hand clockwise as seen from above sends the thumb **downward**, so μ points into the table — that face of the loop is its south pole. B is the left-hand version, or the right hand used with the fingers along B instead of I. C puts μ in the plane of the loop; the current has a different direction at every point of the loop, so it cannot define one. D confuses a vector with a sense of rotation: μ is a vector along an axis, not a circulation.

**7. D** — *Rearranging B = μ₀I/2πr.* I = 2πrB/μ₀ = rB/(2×10⁻⁷) = (0.050)(3.0×10⁻⁵)/(2×10⁻⁷) = 1.5×10⁻⁶/2×10⁻⁷ = **7.5 A**. A leaves r as 5.0 cm, a factor of 100. B loses the 2 from 2πr and so halves the current. C uses the loop-centre formula B = μ₀I/2R instead of the straight-wire one, dropping π.

**8. B** — *B = μ₀nI depends on turns per unit length.* Stretching without adding turns keeps N fixed and doubles L, so n = N/L halves, and B halves to **4.0 mT**. A is the error of believing B depends on the total number of turns, which would make stretching irrelevant. C inverts the dependence. D squares the effect, as though B ∝ n².

**9. C** — *The moment of a negative charge opposes its angular momentum.* Conventional current is the flow of positive charge, so an electron going one way round its orbit is a current going the other way; μ = IA therefore points opposite to L = mvr. That is what the minus sign in M = g(−eL/2m) records. A double-counts the sign: g is a positive number (≈1 orbital, ≈2 spin), which is precisely why the minus is written out separately. B is false in the way it is stated — both L and μ are axial vectors, which is exactly why they can be parallel or antiparallel at all. D invents a rule about bound states; a free electron in a circular path behaves identically.

**10. B** — *A uniform field exerts a torque but no net force.* The forces on opposite sides of any current loop, or on the two poles of a magnet, are equal and opposite in a uniform field, so ΣF = 0; they nonetheless form a couple, and at 45° the torque μB sin 45° is substantial. A describes what happens in a **non-uniform** field, which is what "magnetic attraction" always means. C is right about the force and wrong about the torque, and would leave compasses useless. D imagines a repulsion that has no source: there is no second magnet, only a field.

**11. D** — *Only the product NA matters.* μ = NIA, and coil P has NA = (100)(2.0×10⁻⁴) = 2.0×10⁻² m², while coil Q has NA = (10)(20×10⁻⁴) = 2.0×10⁻² m². The two NA products are equal, so equal moments require **equal currents**. A and B each compensate for one factor of ten while ignoring the other — the answer of a student who noticed the turns differ by ten but did not notice the areas differ by ten the other way. C compounds both errors in the same direction.

**12. A** — *U = +μB is the anti-aligned orientation, θ = 180°.* There cos θ = −1, so U = +μB, and sin θ = 0, so the **torque is zero** — but the energy is a **maximum**, so the slightest displacement is amplified: unstable equilibrium, a pencil on its point. B confuses the extremum of energy with the extremum of torque; they never coincide. C is the trap the question is built on — it correctly identifies an extremum and then assumes any extremum is stable, when only a *minimum* is. D is wrong: at 180° the loop is perpendicular to B and the flux through it is **maximum** in magnitude, merely negative in sign.

**13.** *Concept: the magnetic dipole, its moment, and the absence of monopoles.* A **magnetic dipole** is the elementary magnetic object: a north–south pair, equivalently a circulating current, that produces the characteristic two-lobed field of a bar magnet and that a field acts on by turning it. The **two models** are (i) a **current loop** and (ii) a **pair of separated magnetic poles**. They give the **same predictions far from the source** — both produce a field falling as 1/r³ with identical angular shape — but they **differ inside the source region**: in the pole model the field between the poles runs from N to S, opposite to the moment, whereas in the loop model the field through the loop is parallel to the moment. The loop model is the physically correct one, because magnetic charges do not exist; every real moment is a current or a spin. **The moment** of a flat coil of N turns each of area A carrying current I is **μ = NIA**, a **vector directed along the normal to the plane of the coil**, in the sense given by the **right-hand rule** (fingers curled along the current, thumb along μ). Its unit is the **ampere metre squared (A·m²)**, identical to the **joule per tesla (J/T)** because U = −μ·B. **No monopoles** means B has no sources or sinks: field lines **never begin or end**, so **every line of B is a closed loop** (N to S outside a magnet, S to N through its interior), and the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero. It follows that a bar magnet cut in half does **not** yield a separate N and a separate S: the lines that used to pass through the cut plane now leave one new face and enter the other, so **each half is immediately a complete dipole**, and this remains true down to a single atom.

**14.** *Concept: τ = μ × B and U = −μ·B, and everything that follows from the angle.* **Torque**: τ = μ × B, magnitude **τ = μB sin θ**, where **θ is the angle between μ and B** — that is, between the **normal to the loop** and the field, *not* between the loop's plane and the field. **Energy**: **U = −μ·B = −μB cos θ**, with U taken as zero at θ = 90°. **(i) Greatest torque, zero energy.** Torque carries sin θ and energy carries cos θ, and the two are never large together: at θ = 90° sin θ = 1 and cos θ = 0, so τ = μB is maximum while U = 0. Physically, θ = 90° is where the field has the greatest turning leverage — the two forces on opposite sides of the loop are separated by the full lever arm — and simultaneously the point exactly halfway between the deepest and the highest energy, so U passes through zero there. **(ii) Two zeros of torque, one equilibrium.** sin θ = 0 at both θ = 0 and θ = 180°. At **θ = 0**, U = −μB is a **minimum**: displace the dipole and the torque restores it, so this is **stable** — it is where a compass needle settles. At **θ = 180°**, U = +μB is a **maximum**: displace it and the torque drives it further, so it is **unstable**, and no real dipole is found there. Zero torque alone therefore cannot identify an equilibrium; you must look at whether U curves up or down about the point. **(iii) Turning without translating.** For a rectangular loop the two sides carrying current in opposite directions feel forces F = BIa that are equal and opposite. Their vector sum is zero, so **there is no net force**, but their lines of action are separated by b sin θ, so they form a **couple** and there is a net torque. This is a property of a **uniform** field only: where B varies from place to place the two forces no longer cancel, and it is that residual force — never a uniform-field force — that pulls a magnet towards a piece of iron.

**15.** *Concept: μ = NIA, τ = μB sin θ, U = −μB cos θ, and B = μ₀nI, with a comparison of scales.* **(i)** A = (0.050)(0.030) = 1.5×10⁻³ m²; μ = NIA = (60)(4.0)(1.5×10⁻³) = **0.36 A·m²**. **(ii)** With the plane of the coil containing B the normal is perpendicular to B, so θ = 90° and the torque is at its maximum: τ = μB = (0.36)(0.20) = **0.072 N·m**. **(iii)** U(120°) = −μB cos 120° = −(0.072)(−0.500) = **+0.036 J**. It is **positive** because 120° is past the halfway point, on the anti-aligned side. **(iv)** U(0°) = −μB = −0.072 J, so ΔU = (−0.072) − (+0.036) = −0.108 J. The work done **by the field** is W = −ΔU = **+0.108 J**: the field releases that energy as the coil swings into alignment, and if nothing damps the motion the coil will arrive at θ = 0 with 0.108 J of kinetic energy and overshoot. (An external agent guiding it slowly would have to absorb the same 0.108 J.) **Solenoid**: n = 400/0.20 = 2000 turns per metre, so B = μ₀nI = (4π×10⁻⁷)(2000)(4.0) = **1.0×10⁻² T = 10 mT**. **Comparison**: that is only about **one twentieth** of the 0.20 T field assumed in the first part. A simple air-cored solenoid of ordinary dimensions and current produces a field in the tens of millitesla; reaching 0.20 T by winding alone would need twenty times the current (80 A, which the wire would not survive) or twenty times the turns per metre. In practice one inserts a **ferromagnetic core**: a relative permeability of only 20 would do it, and iron offers hundreds. This is why every electromagnet, motor and relay has an iron core rather than more copper.

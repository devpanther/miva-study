## Answers

**1. C** — *statement of Boyle's law*: at **constant temperature**, for a **fixed mass** of gas, P ∝ 1/V, i.e. PV = constant. Option A is **Charles's law** (constant pressure, V ∝ T) and option B is **Gay-Lussac's law** (constant volume, P ∝ T) — both are other members of the same family of empirical gas laws, and the quantity held constant is the giveaway. Option D has the proportionality the right way up but the relationship the wrong way round: direct instead of inverse, which would mean squeezing a gas lowers its pressure.

**2. A** — *linearising the law*: PV = k gives P = k(1/V), of the form y = mx with m = k and no intercept, so P against 1/V is a straight line through the origin. Option B is the **rectangular hyperbola** (the isotherm) — a smooth falling curve that approaches both axes and meets neither. Option C is a straight line but a **horizontal** one at height k, which does not pass through the origin. Option D is the plot for **Charles's law**, and T is not even varied here.

**3. D** — *gradient of the linearised plot*: with P on the y-axis and 1/V on the x-axis, gradient = P/(1/V) = PV = the constant = 2.026 J. Option A is the gradient of the **reciprocal plot** (1/V against P, gradient 1/PV = 0.494 J⁻¹) — the classic error of not checking which quantity is on which axis. Option B is P₀ and option C is V₀; each is one of the two factors, not their product.

**4. B** — *the zero of the pressure scale*: with the levels equal there is no excess mercury column, so the trapped air is supporting nothing but the atmosphere pressing on the open limb, P₀ = P_a = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa. Option A forgets that the atmosphere is already pressing on the mercury. Option C misreads h, which is the **difference in level of the two mercury surfaces**, not the length of the air column. Option D double-counts: the mercury already standing in the closed limb is balanced by the mercury at the same level in the open limb.

**5. D** — *P_f = P_a + ρgh*: ρgh = 13 600 × 9.8 × 0.150 = 19 992 Pa, so P_f = 101 300 + 19 992 = 121 292 ≈ 1.21 × 10⁵ Pa. Option A is ρgh alone — the excess is the *increase*, not the pressure. Option B subtracts instead of adding, which would mean raising the open limb rarefies the gas. Option C uses ρ = 1000 kg/m³, the density of **water** rather than mercury, giving only 1 470 Pa of extra pressure.

**6. C** — *volume from a length*: for a tube of uniform bore V = ℓA, so V ∝ ℓ, and with A = 1 cm² the number read off the scale in cm is the volume in cm³. This is why a non-uniform bore is a genuine source of error. Option A is true but irrelevant — incompressible mercury explains why the mercury does not change volume, not why a length may stand for the gas volume. Option B invokes ideality, which has nothing to do with the geometry of the tube. Option D confuses a unit used for pressure with a measurement of volume.

**7. A** — *why the law is linearised*: verification means testing a prediction that could fail. Any curve that falls as V rises "looks like" a hyperbola, so drawing one proves nothing; a straight line, and specifically one whose intercept is zero, either appears or does not. Option B is simply false — at constant T the P–V graph is a hyperbola, not a line. Option C is false: both are measured in every row of the table. Option D inverts the truth — the hyperbola is the *ideal*-gas result, and real gases depart from it.

**8. D** — *diagnosing a non-zero intercept*: if only ρgh is plotted, the x-values are all short by P_a, so the line slides sideways and meets the 1/V axis at P_a/PV = 101 300/2.026 = 5.0 × 10⁴ m⁻³ instead of at the origin. The **gradient is still right**, which is exactly why the error survives a casual check — the tell-tale is the intercept, not the straightness. Option A would make the line **curve** (PV drifting upward), not offset it. Option B would make PV fall steadily down the table. Option C rescales the y-axis by 10⁶ and so changes the **gradient**; a pure unit error can never create an intercept.

**9. B** — *P₁V₁ = P₂V₂*: V₂ = P₁V₁/P₂ = (1.0 × 10⁵ × 20)/(2.5 × 10⁵) = 8 cm³. Option A multiplies by the pressure ratio instead of dividing by it — treating the relation as direct proportion. Option C halves the volume, i.e. rounds the pressure ratio 2.5 down to 2. Option D divides by 10, a decimal slip in handling the powers of ten.

**10. C** — *kinetic-theory mechanism*: temperature fixes the mean molecular speed, so each collision carries the same mean momentum; halving the volume doubles the number density and therefore the **rate** of collisions with the walls, and pressure is force per unit area produced by that collision rate. Option A is the single most common misconception — molecular speed is set by temperature alone and is unchanged here. Option B treats molecules as squashable, whereas in the ideal-gas picture they have negligible size and are rigid. Option D appeals to intermolecular forces, which the ideal-gas model neglects entirely and which in any case would *lower* the pressure, not raise it.

**11. A** — *pressure units and conversions*: atmospheric pressure supports a 76 cm (= 760 mm) column of mercury, and 76 cmHg = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa; the pascal is one newton per square metre. Option B swaps cm for mm, a factor of 10. Option C is out by a factor of 100 in the pascal value and writes the pascal as N·m² instead of N/m². Option D makes the mercury column ten times too short and defines the pascal as a force per unit *length*.

**12. B** — *protecting the constant-temperature condition*: compressing a gas does work on it and warms it, so a volume read immediately after the limb is raised is too large; waiting for thermal equilibrium (and watching the thermometer) is what makes the process isothermal. Option C is a real and important precaution, but it protects the **fixed-mass** condition. Option D protects the measurement of **h**. Option A protects the **reading** of the scales. All four are genuine precautions — the question is which condition each one defends.

**13.** *Statement, mathematical forms and the purpose of each item of apparatus.* **Statement**: at **constant temperature**, the pressure of a **definite (fixed) quantity** of an ideal gas is **inversely proportional to its volume**; equivalently, the product of the pressure and the volume of a fixed mass of gas is constant provided the temperature does not change. Both conditions — constant temperature and fixed mass — are part of the law, not optional context. **Forms**: (i) **PV = constant**; (ii) **P ∝ 1/V**; (iii) **P₁V₁ = P₂V₂**. (For n moles the constant is nRT, which is why fixing T and n fixes it.) **Apparatus**: J-tube, thermometer, pressure sensor, gas sample, and the Praxilabs virtual laboratory module for Boyle's law. The **thermometer** exists to verify the constant-temperature condition — without evidence that T did not change, no reading of P and V says anything about Boyle's law. The **closed end of the J-tube** is what traps a fixed quantity of air, so that the mass of gas stays constant while its pressure is varied by adding or raising mercury at the open end.

**14. P = 1.146 × 10⁵ Pa and V = 17.67 cm³ at h = 100 mm; P = 1.346 × 10⁵ Pa and V = 15.05 cm³ at h = 250 mm; gradient = 0.4936 J⁻¹ = 1/(P₀V₀)** — *the linearised graph and what its gradient means*. First the constant: P₀V₀ = 1.013 × 10⁵ × 20 × 10⁻⁶ = **2.026 J**. At **h = 0.100 m**: ρgh = 13 600 × 9.8 × 0.100 = 13 328 Pa, so P = 101 300 + 13 328 = **114 628 Pa**, and V = 2.026/114 628 = 1.767 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = **17.67 cm³** (an air column ℓ = 17.67 cm, since A = 1 cm²), giving 1/V = 5.658 × 10⁴ m⁻³. At **h = 0.250 m**: ρgh = 33 320 Pa, P = **134 620 Pa**, V = 2.026/134 620 = 1.505 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = **15.05 cm³**, giving 1/V = 6.645 × 10⁴ m⁻³. **Gradient** = (6.645 − 5.658) × 10⁴ / (134 620 − 114 628) = 9 868/19 992 = **0.4936 J⁻¹**. Boyle's law predicts 1/V = P/(P₀V₀), a straight line through the origin of gradient 1/(P₀V₀) = 1/2.026 = **0.4936 J⁻¹** — the measured and predicted gradients agree, and the line extrapolates to the origin, so the law is verified. (With the axes interchanged, P against 1/V has gradient P₀V₀ = 2.026 J, the constant itself.)

**15.** *Procedure, precautions and errors.* **Procedure**: (1) start with the mercury at the **same level in both limbs**, h = 0, so the trapped air is at atmospheric pressure; record V₀ and the temperature; (2) raise the open (right) limb through **h = 50 mm = 0.05 m**, which increases the pressure on the trapped air; (3) note that the trapped air column has **shortened**; (4) click **Record** to log that volume V_f; (5) repeat in 50 mm steps at **h = 100, 150, 200 and 250 mm**, recording V_f at each; (6) finish, and download the Excel data sheet; (7) compute **P_f = P_a + ρgh** and **1/V_f** for each row; (8) **plot 1/V_f against P_f** — a straight line through the origin, together with a constant product P_f V_f down the table, verifies the law. **Precautions** (any three): allow the gas to **return to room temperature after each compression** before reading the volume, since compression warms the gas — this protects the **constant-temperature** condition; check that the **closed limb is gas-tight** and that no air bubbles past the mercury — this protects the **fixed-mass** condition; keep the tube **vertical** and take h as the **vertical difference between the two mercury surfaces**, not the distance the limb was dragged — this protects the value of ρgh; read both levels and the air column with the **eye perpendicular to the scale**, at the top of the convex mercury meniscus, to avoid parallax; take P_a from a **barometer** rather than assuming 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa. **Sources of error** (any three, with effect): **failure to let the compressed gas cool** leaves V too large at high P, so PV drifts upward and the plot curves away from a straight line; **omitting atmospheric pressure** from P shifts every point sideways and gives a positive intercept on the 1/V axis with the gradient unchanged; **leakage past the seal** reduces the mass of gas, so PV falls steadily down the table; **dead space or a non-uniform bore** breaks V = ℓA and introduces a volume offset that shows as an intercept or curvature; **moisture in the trapped air** adds a saturated vapour pressure that does not obey Boyle's law; **parallax and zero errors** scatter the points about the line; and at high pressure **real-gas deviation** from ideal behaviour makes PV genuinely non-constant.

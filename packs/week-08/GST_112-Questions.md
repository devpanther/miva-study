## Section A - Multiple choice (12)

**1.** The part of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended) that provides extensively for citizenship is:
a) Chapter II, the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy
b) Chapter IV, Sections 33 to 46
c) Chapter Three
d) The Seventh Schedule

**2.** Under the 1999 Constitution as the lesson states it, a person is a Nigerian citizen by birth if:
a) He was born on Nigerian soil, whatever the nationality of his parents
b) Both parents are Nigerian, or either parent is Nigerian, or any one of the grandparents is Nigerian
c) Both parents are Nigerian, and only where both are
d) Either parent is Nigerian and he was in fact born within Nigeria

**3.** Citizenship of Nigeria by registration is open to:
a) A non-Nigerian man married to a Nigerian woman, and any person born outside Nigeria whose parent is a citizen of Nigeria
b) Any person issued with a certificate of grant of citizenship under Section 27 of the 1999 Constitution
c) Any foreigner who has resided in Nigeria for a long period and is of good character
d) A non-Nigerian woman married to a Nigerian, and every person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparent is a citizen of Nigeria

**4.** To qualify for registration, the foreigner must prove to the President:
a) Good character, a clear intention of his desire to be domiciled or to reside in Nigeria, and that he has taken the prescribed Oath of Allegiance
b) Good character, ten years' continuous residence in Nigeria, and renunciation of his former citizenship
c) A clean criminal record, fluency in an indigenous Nigerian language, and five years of tax receipts
d) Good character, ownership of landed property in Nigeria, and sponsorship by the governor of a state

**5.** The prescribed Oath of Allegiance to Nigeria is contained in:
a) Section 24 of the 1999 Constitution
b) Section 27 of the 1999 Constitution
c) The Seventh Schedule to the 1999 Constitution
d) Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution

**6.** A Ghanaian man has lived and worked in Nigeria for twenty-two years. He has no Nigerian parent or grandparent and is unmarried. On the lesson's account, his route to Nigerian citizenship is:
a) Registration, as a person of full age and capacity ordinarily resident in Nigeria
b) Naturalisation, on the requirements stipulated in Section 27, ending in a certificate of grant of citizenship by naturalisation
c) Citizenship by birth, long residence being treated as equivalent to descent
d) None; the Constitution provides only for citizenship by birth and by registration

**7.** A man became a Nigerian citizen by naturalisation in 2015. In 2019 he is convicted of an offence and sentenced to four years' imprisonment. On the lesson's statement of the law:
a) The President may deprive him of his Nigerian citizenship, the sentence being not less than 3 years and imposed within 7 years of his naturalisation
b) He cannot be deprived, because deprivation reaches only citizens by registration
c) He cannot be deprived, because the sentence falls outside the 3-year period that runs from the date of naturalisation
d) He loses his citizenship automatically on conviction, without any act of the President

**8.** Which of these is a ground on which a person who acquired Nigerian citizenship by registration or naturalisation may lose it?
a) Failing to vote in two consecutive national elections
b) Residing outside Nigeria for more than five continuous years
c) Marrying a citizen of a country that does not permit dual citizenship
d) Trading with, or associating in any business with, an enemy country at war with Nigeria

**9.** A Nigerian of full age makes the prescribed declaration renouncing his Nigerian citizenship in order to take up another. He makes it while Nigeria is involved in a war. On the lesson's account:
a) The renunciation takes effect automatically, the President having no role in it
b) The President may refuse to register the declaration, war being one of the two stated grounds of refusal
c) The renunciation is void, because a citizen by birth may never renounce his citizenship
d) The declaration must instead be made to the National Assembly for approval

**10.** On dual citizenship, the lesson states that:
a) A foreigner who is a citizen of another country by birth may also be a Nigerian by registration or naturalisation, and a Nigerian by birth may take another citizenship without renouncing his own
b) It is prohibited outright by the 1999 Constitution, which requires a single allegiance
c) It is permitted to citizens by birth only, who may hold another citizenship, but never to those who acquire Nigerian citizenship
d) It is permitted only where the President issues a certificate of dual nationality to that effect

**11.** The lesson warns that citizenship is often confused with nationality. Of the two it says that:
a) Citizenship is of a wider scope than nationality
b) The two terms are legally identical in Nigeria
c) Nationality is of a wider scope than citizenship
d) Nationality can be acquired only by naturalisation

**12.** Which of these is one of the duties Section 24 of the 1999 Constitution imposes on every citizen, in the Constitution's own words?
a) Vote in every federal, state and local government election held in his constituency
b) Serve in the armed forces of the Federation for a minimum period of one year
c) Renounce any other citizenship he holds within twelve months of attaining full age
d) Declare his income honestly to appropriate and lawful agencies and pay his tax promptly

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Set out the three routes to Nigerian citizenship. For each, give the exact conditions the lesson states and the constitutional reference attached to it.

**14.** Distinguish renunciation of citizenship from deprivation of citizenship: say who acts in each case, on what grounds, and which classes of citizen each can reach.

**15.** State the six civic responsibilities the lesson lists, then the six duties of Section 24 of the 1999 Constitution, and explain the lesson's conclusion that civic responsibility is a moral as well as a legal obligation.

## Answers

**1. c** — *where citizenship sits in the Constitution*: **Chapter Three** of the CFRN 1999 (as amended), on the principle that **every country has the right to decide who will be its citizen**. **Chapter IV, ss.33–46** is **fundamental human rights** (last week's material); **Chapter II** is the **Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles**; the **Seventh Schedule** carries only the **Oath of Allegiance**, one item inside the citizenship scheme, not the scheme itself.

**2. b** — *citizenship by birth is disjunctive*: **both parents**, **or either parent**, **or any one grandparent** being Nigerian. Option (a) encodes **jus soli** — birth on the soil alone, which the lesson does not give. Option (c) reads the three limbs as cumulative when they are alternatives. Option (d) adds a **birthplace** requirement that is not there; the qualification is by **descent**.

**3. d** — *the two registration classes*: the **non-Nigerian woman married to a Nigerian**, and **every person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparent is a citizen of Nigeria**. Option (a) reverses the sexes in the marriage limb and downgrades **grandparent** to **parent** — both are changes the lesson does not make. Option (b) describes **naturalisation**, which ends in the s.27 certificate. Option (c) is the **long-resident foreigner**, again naturalisation, not registration.

**4. a** — *the three proofs to the President*: **good character**; a **clear intention of his desire to be domiciled or to live or reside in Nigeria**; and that he **has taken the prescribed Oath of Allegiance**. Option (b) imports a residence period and a renunciation requirement — but Nigeria **permits dual citizenship**, so renunciation is never demanded. Options (c) and (d) invent language, property and sponsorship tests that appear nowhere in the lesson.

**5. c** — *the Seventh Schedule*: this is where the **prescribed Oath of Allegiance to Nigeria** is set out. Keep the four references apart: **Chapter Three** = citizenship generally, **Section 24** = the duties of every citizen, **Section 27** = naturalisation, **Seventh Schedule** = the oath.

**6. b** — *naturalisation is the long-residence route*: it is the means by which **any foreigner who has lived for a long time in Nigeria** may become a citizen, on the requirements **stipulated in Section 27**, after which a **certificate of grant of citizenship by naturalisation** is issued. Option (a) fails because registration is confined to the **two named classes**, and he is in neither. Option (c) confuses **residence** with **descent**. Option (d) forgets the third route entirely.

**7. a** — *the deprivation trigger, and the two numbers*: the President may deprive a person **who is not a Nigerian by birth** where he is **sentenced to not less than 3 years' imprisonment within 7 years of becoming a citizen by naturalisation**. Four years is **not less than 3**; 2019 is **within 7 years** of 2015. Option (c) swaps the two numbers, treating 3 as the window and ignoring the 7. Option (b) is wrong on class — the disloyalty and enemy-country grounds reach citizens **by naturalisation or by registration**, and this criminal-sentence ground is stated of **naturalisation** in particular. Option (d) makes it automatic: deprivation is always an **act of the President**, never a consequence that follows on its own.

**8. d** — *the enemy-country grounds*: a person who acquired citizenship **by naturalisation or registration** may lose it if he is **disloyal to Nigeria by act or speech**; **traded with, engaged in or associated in any business with an enemy country at war with Nigeria**; **assisted such an enemy country in any manner**; or **unlawfully communicated with it** to Nigeria's detriment or with intent to cause damage. The other three options are civic failings or private choices that the lesson nowhere makes grounds of loss — non-voting, long absence and marriage abroad all leave citizenship intact.

**9. b** — *renunciation and the President's two grounds of refusal*: a citizen **of full age** has the right to renounce **by the prescribed declaration**, but the President **may refuse to register** it where it is made **when Nigeria is involved in a war** or **contrary to public policy**. Option (a) removes the registration step altogether. Option (c) invents a bar on citizens by birth — it is **deprivation**, not renunciation, that spares them. Option (d) puts the power in the wrong branch: the declaration is registered by the **President**, not approved by the legislature.

**10. a** — *dual citizenship is allowed both ways*: a **foreigner who is a citizen of another country by birth** may **also** be a Nigerian by **registration or naturalisation**, and a **Nigerian by birth** may acquire another citizenship **without renouncing** his Nigerian one. Option (b) is the flat prohibition many students assume. Option (c) grants the concession in one direction only. Option (d) invents a certificate; the only certificate in the lesson is the one issued on **naturalisation**.

**11. c** — *citizenship against nationality*: the lesson says citizenship is often confused with **nationality, which is of a wider scope than citizenship**. Options (a) and (b) invert or collapse the relation; (d) confuses **nationality** with one of the three **routes** to citizenship.

**12. d** — *Section 24(F)*: the duty to **declare his income honestly to appropriate and lawful agencies and pay his tax promptly**. Voting is a right the lesson attaches to citizenship, not one of the six Section 24 duties; the duty in (b) is **"render such national service as may be required"**, not a fixed one-year term of military service; (c) contradicts the lesson, since Nigeria **permits dual citizenship**.

**13.** *The three patterns of acquisition.* Citizenship is provided for in **Chapter Three of the CFRN 1999 (as amended)**, and a person may be a Nigerian **by birth, by registration or by naturalisation**. **By birth** — where **both parents are Nigerians**, **or either parent is a Nigerian**, **or any one of the grandparents is a Nigerian**; one qualifying relative suffices. **By registration** — open to **a non-Nigerian woman who is married to a Nigerian**, and to **every person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparent is a citizen of Nigeria**; the foreigner must prove **to the President** that he or she is **a person of good character**, has **shown a clear intention of his desire to be domiciled or to live or reside in Nigeria**, and has **taken the prescribed Oath of Allegiance to Nigeria contained in the Seventh Schedule to the 1999 Constitution**. **By naturalisation** — the **process by which an individual becomes a legal citizen of a specific country**, the **formal act of granting citizenship to a foreign national who meets the predetermined eligibility criteria established by the country's laws and regulations**; in Nigeria this means fulfilling the requirements **stipulated in Section 27 of the 1999 Constitution**, after which a **certificate of grant of citizenship by naturalisation** is issued. It is the route by which **any foreigner who has lived for a long time in Nigeria** may become a citizen.

**14.** *Renunciation against deprivation.* **Renunciation is the citizen's own act.** A **citizen of Nigeria who is of full age** has the **right to renounce and abandon his Nigerian citizenship** and take up the citizenship of **any other country of his choice**, by **making the prescribed declaration to that effect**. It reaches **any** citizen of full age, including a citizen by birth. The only check is that the **President may refuse to register the declaration** in **two** situations: where it was made **when Nigeria is involved in a war**, or where it is **contrary to public policy**. **Deprivation is the President's act**, imposed on the citizen rather than sought by him, and it reaches **only acquired citizenship — never citizenship by birth**. Its grounds are, first, that the person is **sentenced to a term of imprisonment of not less than 3 years within a period of 7 years after becoming a citizen of Nigeria by naturalisation**; and second, for persons who acquired citizenship **by naturalisation or by registration**, that the person **is disloyal to Nigeria by act or speech**, **traded with, engaged in or associated in any business with an enemy country at war with Nigeria**, **assisted that enemy country in any manner**, or **unlawfully communicated with it to the detriment of, or with the intention to cause damage to, Nigeria**. The clean contrast to hold: **who initiates** (citizen vs President), **on what grounds** (free choice vs criminal sentence or disloyalty), and **who is exposed** (every citizen of full age vs only those who acquired citizenship).

**15.** *Civic responsibility.* **Civic responsibility** is the **duties and obligations that citizens are expected to fulfil for the betterment of their society and the functioning of their country**, outlined in **various laws, policies and cultural expectations**. The lesson's six are: **obey the law; protection of public property; payment of taxes; rendering national service; loyalty; respect for others' rights.** These are contained in **Section 24 of the CFRN 1999 (as amended)**, which provides that it shall be the duty of every citizen to — **(A) abide by this Constitution, respect its ideals and its institutions, the National Flag, the National Anthem, the National Pledge, and legitimate authorities; (B) help to enhance the power, prestige and good name of Nigeria, defend Nigeria and render such national service as may be required; (C) respect the dignity of other citizens and the rights and legitimate interests of others and live in unity and harmony and in the spirit of common brotherhood; (D) make positive and useful contribution to the advancement, progress and well-being of the community where he resides; (E) render assistance to appropriate and lawful agencies in the maintenance of law and order; and (F) declare his income honestly to appropriate and lawful agencies and pay his tax promptly.** The conclusion the lesson draws is that civic responsibility is **not only a legal obligation but also a moral duty**: several of the Section 24 duties — respect for the dignity of others, contribution to the community where one resides, living in the spirit of common brotherhood — could not realistically be enforced by a court, so they bind as **moral** commitments rather than as sanctions. Understood that way, fulfilling them **promotes active citizenship** and **contributes to the overall development of Nigeria**, allowing citizens to build **a thriving and inclusive society**.

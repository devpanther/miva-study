# GST_122 — Week 8 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 8 (26 Oct – 1 Nov 2026) · Topic: Vocabulary Expansion and Word Formation Processes*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Vocabulary expansion** = "the process of **increasing one's repertoire of words and expressions in a language**." It is not learning definitions; it is enlarging the store of words *and expressions* actually available for use.
2. **The seven word-formation processes the lesson names, in its own order: affixation, compounding, conversion, derivation, blending, acronyms, borrowing** — with the deck's own examples *unadulterated / correctly, raincoat, email (as verb), teacher, brunch, NASA, sushi*. Expect to be given a word and asked which process made it.
3. **Affixes modify an existing word by attaching a bound form to it**: a **prefix** before the base (*un-* + *adulterated* → **unadulterated**, "not adulterated"), a **suffix** after it (*correct* + *-ly* → **correctly**). Stated function: **altering meaning and function while expanding vocabulary**.
4. **Conversion** changes a word's **grammatical category with no affix at all** — *email* (noun) used as *to email* (verb). If any affix is added, it is not conversion.
5. **Derivation** adds a **derivational affix to create a new word**, usually of a new class: *teach* (v.) + *-er* → *teacher* (n.). It is affixation used to make a **new lexeme**, which is why the lesson lists both.
6. **Blending fuses parts of two words** (*breakfast* + *lunch* → **brunch**); **compounding joins two whole words** (*rain* + *coat* → **raincoat**). The lesson's prose slips and calls *brunch* a compound; its bullet list — the authoritative one — files it under blending. Answer "blend".
7. **Acronym** = a word made from **the initial letters of a phrase or title** (*NASA*); **borrowing** = a word taken **from another language** (*sushi*).
8. **Four named strategies for vocabulary expansion:** reading widely and regularly (new words in different contexts); using **vocabulary-building tools** — dictionaries, thesauruses, language-learning apps; **engaging in conversations, discussions and debates** to apply and reinforce new words; **playing word games** and language-related activities.

## The seven processes — test, deck example, extra example

| Process | What exactly happens | Deck's example | Another |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Affixation** | A prefix and/or suffix is attached to an existing base | *unadulterated*, *correctly* | *misinform*, *hopeless* |
| **Compounding** | Two (or more) **whole** words merge into one | *raincoat* | *blackboard*, *bookshop* |
| **Conversion** | Word class changes with **no** affix added | *email* (n.) → *to email* (v.) | *google*, *to text*, *a run* |
| **Derivation** | A derivational affix creates a **new word**, usually a new class | *teacher* | *happiness*, *nationalise* |
| **Blending** | **Parts** of two words fuse | *brunch* | *smog*, *motel*, *webinar* |
| **Acronym** | **Initial letters** of a phrase form a word | *NASA* | *UNICEF*, *laser* |
| **Borrowing** | A word is taken from **another language** | *sushi* | *safari*, *déjà vu*, *danfo* |

Standard additions worth knowing: an **initialism** is also made of initial letters but is **spelled out** (*FBI*, *ATM*), whereas an **acronym** is **pronounced as a word** (*NASA*); **clipping** shortens one word without fusing anything (*advertisement* → *advert/ad*, *laboratory* → *lab*); **coinage** invents a wholly new word (*byte*).

## Affixes and their meanings

**Prefixes change meaning, rarely word class:**

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-/im-/il-/ir-, non-, dis- | not, absence of | *unadulterated, impossible, illegal, non-verbal, dishonest* |
| re- / mis- | again, back / wrongly | *rewrite, misinform* |
| pre- / post- | before / after | *prerequisite, postgraduate* |
| inter- / intra- / trans- | between / within / across | *interdisciplinary, intranet, transcribe* |
| sub- / super- / hyper- | under / above / excessive | *substandard, supervise, hyperactive* |
| anti- / pro- / co- | against / for / together | *antisocial, pro-democracy, co-author* |
| mono- / bi- / multi- | one / two / many | *monolingual, bilateral, multimedia* |
| over- / under- | too much / too little | *overestimate, underpaid* |

**Suffixes usually change word class — this is what makes them examinable:**

| Suffix | Produces | Meaning added | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| -tion/-sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ance/-ence | noun | act, state or quality of | *formation, judgement, kindness, ability, tolerance* |
| -er/-or, -ist, -ee | noun (person) | one who does / one who receives | *teacher, scientist, employee* |
| -ship, -ism, -hood | noun (abstract) | condition, doctrine, state | *mentorship, didacticism, childhood* |
| -ful, -less | adjective | full of / without | *hopeful, careless* |
| -able/-ible | adjective | able to be | *readable, edible* |
| -ive, -ous, -al, -ic, -ish | adjective | having the nature of | *creative, dangerous, national, historic, childish* |
| -ise/-ize, -ify, -en, -ate | verb | make, cause to become | *modernise, simplify, darken, activate* |
| -ly | adverb (from adj.) | in the manner of | *correctly, quickly* |

**Derivational vs inflectional** (the distinction the exam turns on): a **derivational** affix makes a **new word**, often of a new class — *teach* → *teacher*. An **inflectional** affix only marks grammar — plural *-s*, third-person *-s*, past *-ed*, *-ing*, comparative *-er*/*-est* — and makes **no new word**: *teaches*, *taught*, *teaching* are all still the verb *teach*. Note *-er* is ambiguous: *teacher* (derivational, "one who teaches") vs *taller* (inflectional, comparative).

## Why vocabulary expansion matters — the seven headings

**Communication skills** (precise, effective expression, fewer misunderstandings) · **intellectual growth** (learning words means learning meanings, contexts and usage, which stimulates critical thinking) · **academic achievement** (complex texts comprehended and analysed more easily; especially language arts, literature and standardized tests) · **professional success** (clear, persuasive expression and better writing) · **cultural competence** (exposure to other cultures, appreciation of linguistic diversity) · **lifelong learning** (expansion continues beyond formal education) · **reading and writing proficiency** (vocabulary underlies comprehension, and word choice determines the quality and impact of writing).

**Objectives stated:** express thoughts and ideas clearly and precisely through a diverse vocabulary; improve academic performance by acquiring and applying **specialized vocabulary** for specific disciplines; critically analyse and interpret texts, concepts and information.

## Commonly confused

- **Compounding vs blending** — compounding keeps both words **whole** (*rain* + *coat*); blending keeps only **parts** (*br(eakfast)* + *(l)unch*). Test: can you still see both complete words? Then it is a compound.
- **Conversion vs derivation** — conversion adds **nothing** and changes class (*email* → *to email*); derivation **adds an affix** to make the new word (*teach* → *teacher*). Test: is there an affix? If yes, it cannot be conversion.
- **Affixation vs derivation** — affixation names the *mechanism* (attaching a prefix or suffix); derivation names the *result* (a new lexeme). All derivation by affix is affixation, but inflectional affixation (*-s*, *-ed*) is not derivation.
- **Derivational vs inflectional suffix** — derivational makes a new dictionary word and usually a new class (*happy → happiness*); inflectional only marks number, tense or degree (*book → books*). Test: would it get its own dictionary entry?
- **Acronym vs initialism** — both use initial letters; an acronym is **pronounced as a word** (*NASA*), an initialism is **spelled out** (*ATM*). The lesson calls the whole category "acronyms".
- **Blending vs clipping** — blending fuses parts of **two** words (*smog*); clipping shortens **one** (*lab*). **Borrowing vs coinage** — borrowing imports an existing foreign word (*sushi*); coinage invents one that existed nowhere before.
- **Vocabulary expansion vs word formation** — expansion is what the *learner* does to their own repertoire (reading, tools, conversation, word games); word formation is what the *language* does to make new words.
- **Didactic vs didacticism** — the adjective for instruction-heavy delivery vs the noun for the practice of stressing moral or educational messages. **Thesis vs dissertation vs synthesis** — the claim to be proved (or the long essay) vs the long essay required for a higher degree vs the combining of ideas into one integrated understanding.

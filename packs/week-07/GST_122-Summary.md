# GST_122 — Week 7 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 7 (19–25 Oct 2026) · Topic: Comprehension Strategies — SQ3R*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Reading strategies** = "techniques and approaches that individuals use to enhance their reading **comprehension, retention and overall effectiveness** when engaging with written text." They help the reader **process information, extract meaning and gain a deeper understanding**. SQ3R is *one* such strategy, not the whole category.
2. **SQ3R expands to five stages in this fixed order: Survey → Question → Read → Recite → Review.** The name is read as **S + Q + 3R**: one S, one Q, and three stages beginning with R. Anyone who writes "Reflect", "Revise", "Record" or "Recall" as one of the 3R has lost the acronym.
3. **Survey** — quickly scan the **entire** text *before* reading in detail: headings, subheadings, images and any other feature giving an overview. It yields **a sense of the text's structure and main ideas**, nothing more. Source example: in a history textbook, chapter titles, subtitles and the **captions of illustrations**.
4. **Question** — "this is where you start **asking** questions, **not answering** the questions from the passage." You generate them **from what the survey discovered**, and they **guide your reading and keep you focused on the most critical information**. Source examples from a research paper: *"What is the primary hypothesis being tested?"*, *"What methods are used to collect data?"*
5. **Read** — the first stage at which you actually read the text, and it has **two goals**: (1) **read to answer the questions you generated**, and (2) **read to engage with the material to gain a fuller understanding**. Goal 2 is examinable precisely because students forget it and treat Read as question-hunting.
6. **Recite** — "**vocalise your thoughts**": recite or **verbally explain** the key points, words and concepts. The summary slide widens this to "verbally **or mentally** summarise what they've learned **to reinforce understanding**" — the point is retrieval in your own words, not re-reading.
7. **Review** — "create **summaries, outlines or notes**" to reinforce understanding, revisiting both your notes and the text itself. Its stated payoff is **long-term** retention: it "ensures that you maintain a deep understanding of the material **over time** and can **recall and apply** it when needed, such as in exams or discussions."
8. The week's other comprehension strategies (from the course summary sheet): **context clues, fact vs opinion, drawing inferences, explicit vs implicit meaning** — and the framing claim that **reading is not merely decoding words**; it is a cognitive process requiring active mental work.

## The five stages — what each requires

| Stage | When | What you actually do | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Survey** | Before any detailed reading | Skim the whole text: headings, subheadings, images, captions, structural features | An overview: the text's structure and main ideas |
| **Question** | Immediately after surveying | Turn the survey findings into *your own* questions | A set of guiding questions that direct attention |
| **Read** | First full contact with the prose | Read to answer those questions **and** to engage for fuller understanding | Answers plus identification of key information |
| **Recite** | After reading (a section or the whole) | Say aloud, in your own words, the key points, words and concepts | Retrieval that reinforces understanding |
| **Review** | Last, and repeatedly over time | Build summaries, outlines and notes; revisit notes and text | Long-term retention and the ability to recall and apply |

**Why your own questions?** (the deck's mid-lesson question.) Questions you generate come from *your* gaps, so they set a purpose before you read, convert passive scanning into an active search, and give an immediate test of whether the reading worked — you either answered them or you did not. Questions supplied by the book test the book's priorities, and can only be attempted *after* reading.

## Worked pass — SQ3R on the deck's own example

*Text: "Chapter 4: The Renaissance Period in Europe."*

- **Survey:** the chapter title alone yields the topic (the Renaissance), the region (Europe) and the span (14th–17th century), plus the flagged names Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Shakespeare, and the closing sentence announcing the chapter's three parts: key characteristics, impact on society, and celebrated artists and thinkers.
- **Question** (the deck's four): *What were the primary characteristics of the Renaissance period? How did the Renaissance impact European society and culture? What is the significance of the shift from medieval traditions to humanism and individualism? Can you provide examples of the contributions made by da Vinci, Michelangelo and Shakespeare?*
- **Read:** work through the passage answering those four, while noting what they did not anticipate — e.g. that the movement was *cultural and intellectual*, and that the revival was of **classical** art, literature and learning.
- **Recite:** aloud, without the text — "a cultural and intellectual movement, 14th to 17th century, revival of classical learning, shift from medieval tradition to humanism and individualism, exemplified by da Vinci, Michelangelo and Shakespeare."
- **Review:** compress that to a five-line outline, then return to it later; the Review stage is what makes the material survive to the exam.

## The other comprehension strategies (course summary sheet)

- **Context clues** — hints *within the text* that let a reader **infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word** without a dictionary. Skill developed: vocabulary acquisition.
- **Inference** — a **logical conclusion drawn from explicit details plus the reader's background knowledge**. An inference is not a guess and not stated on the page; it must be supported by details that are.
- **Fact vs opinion** — a fact is **objective and verifiable**; an opinion is **subjective**. Separating them is the stated core of critical thinking and is what allows information to be evaluated and well-informed perspectives formed.
- **Explicit meaning** — **directly stated in the text; requires no inference**. **Implicit meaning** — **unspoken or suggested; requires the reader to infer it**. Together they give "a nuanced understanding of textual content beyond surface-level comprehension."
- **Named competencies for the lesson:** vocabulary acquisition, critical thinking, inference drawing, textual analysis, decision-making, effective communication.
- **Case study:** *Impact of Reading Strategies on Critical Analysis* — participants read diverse texts containing unfamiliar words, applied context clues, fact–opinion assessment and inference drawing, then performed critical-analysis exercises and gave feedback on how the strategies affected their interpretation and decision-making.
- **References given:** Adler & Van Doren (1972), *How to Read a Book*; Armstrong (1982), *Study Is Hard Work*.

## Commonly confused

- **Survey vs Read** — Survey is deliberately **fast, whole-text and structural** (headings, captions, layout) and happens **before** detailed reading; Read is **linear and complete**. Test: if you are extracting meaning from sentences, you have left Survey.
- **Question vs answering the passage's questions** — SQ3R's Q is **you asking, before reading**. The questions printed at the end of a chapter are answered **after** reading and are not this stage.
- **Recite vs Review** — Recite is **oral/mental retrieval in your own words, immediately, without the text**; Review is **written consolidation — summaries, outlines, notes — with the text and notes to hand, repeated over time**. Test: Recite has no paper; Review produces paper.
- **Recite vs re-reading** — reciting means *saying it back closed-book*; running your eyes over the paragraph again is Read repeated, and reinforces nothing.
- **Explicit vs implicit meaning** — explicit is **on the page, quotable**; implicit is **suggested, must be inferred**. Test: can you underline the sentence that says it? If yes, explicit.
- **Inference vs opinion** — an inference is **anchored to stated details** and can be defended from the text; an opinion is **subjective** and needs no textual anchor.
- **Context clues vs inference** — context clues resolve the meaning of a **word**; inference draws a conclusion about the **content or the writer's meaning**. Context clues are one route to an inference, not a synonym for it.

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Define human rights and human rights abuse as the lesson defines them. State where fundamental human rights are located in the 1999 Constitution, name the regional instrument that makes them enforceable in Nigerian courts, and list the five rights the lesson names for Nigeria.

**14.** Name the five common human rights abuses the lesson identifies in Nigeria, and for each attach the specific event, figure, institution or statute the lesson gives.

**15.** State the lesson's conclusion on how a nation's human rights record should be judged, and explain why, on that account, the number of international treaties a government has signed is not the measure. Say what the lesson demands instead.

## Answers

**1. B** — *the constitutional location*: **Chapter IV, Sections 33 to 46** of the **1999 Constitution**. Option A encodes the standard confusion with **Chapter II**: the **Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy** are a separate head, introduced alongside fundamental rights by the **1979** Constitution, and are not the fundamental rights themselves.

**2. C** — *the regional instrument*: the **African Charter on Human and People's Rights** is what the lesson credits with guaranteeing enforceability **in Nigerian courts of law**. A picks the **UDHR**, which supplies the *definition* and the **Article 1** quotation but is a declaration, not the enforcement route named here. B picks the **2009 Rules**, which the lesson mentions only to say they still carry **shortcomings and inhibitions**. D picks a 2015 domestic-violence statute.

**3. A** — *the NHRC figures and their direction*: **1,701,519 cases in 2021**, and the lesson says explicitly that this figure was **higher than the 1,287,280 complaints received in 2020**. B reverses the direction; C substitutes the **367,000 women and gender-related issues** figure; D shifts both years back one.

**4. B** — *the 1979 Constitution*: it introduced **Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy** *and* **Fundamental Human Rights**. C is the **2009 Rules**; A wrongly separates the two heads the 1979 Constitution introduced together; D confuses the African Charter's domestication with the constitutional history.

**5. C** — *Article 1, UDHR*: "endowed with **reason and conscience**, and should act towards one another in the spirit of brotherhood." A and D are drawn from the lesson's separate list of **common human rights** (right to life and liberty; freedom of opinion and expression), not from Article 1.

**6. B** — *extra-judicial killing*: **#EndSARS, October 2020**, brought international attention to **police brutality**. The lesson attaches it to the security forces heading — police and military implicated in **extrajudicial killings, torture and excessive use of force** — not to the media, child-marriage or domestic-violence headings.

**7. B** — *the five-part child-marriage test*: a State violates international human rights law if it fails to **establish** a minimum age, **enforce** it, **provide for and enforce the same minimum age for boys and girls**, **act with due diligence to prevent** child marriage, or if it **involves public officials in it**. A and C encode the belief that having *any* enforced minimum age discharges the obligation; D encodes the belief that the five limbs are cumulative rather than each sufficient on its own.

**8. A** — *the Aliogo (2023) figures*: **43% before the 18th birthday, 16% before the age of 15**. The larger percentage necessarily goes with the higher age, since every girl married before 15 is also married before 18 — B and C invert this and are arithmetically impossible, not merely wrong.

**9. C** — *freedom of expression as an abuse*: the **National Broadcasting Commission (NBC)** is responsible for **monitoring and regulating broadcast media**, and the government used that **regulatory oversight** to restrict press freedom. A is the **NHRC**, which receives complaints of violations — the two are routinely swapped because both are federal commissions with initials beginning "N".

**10. C** — *the domestic-violence statute*: the **Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act, 2015**. B is the year of the **Rules** on rights enforcement; D and A attach to nothing in the lesson.

**11. B** — *the rape/gender figure*: **about 367,000 women and gender-related issues in Nigeria in 2021**. A confuses it with the **NHRC's 1,701,519**; D confuses it with the **43%/16%** child-marriage percentages, which the lesson gives as proportions, not counts.

**12. C** — *Nigeria on the Democracy Index*: a **hybrid state**, because it does not meet the criteria of a **full democracy**; democracy in Nigeria is described as "a work in progress".

**13.** *Definition and legal framework.* **Human rights** are **rights inherent to all human beings, regardless of race, sex, nationality, ethnicity, language, religion, or any other status**; **Article 1 of the UN Declaration of Human Rights** puts it that "all human beings are **born free and equal in dignity and rights**", **endowed with reason and conscience**. A **human rights abuse is anything that harms someone's human rights**, and includes **harm to people, communities, and the environment**. In Nigeria the rights are contained in **Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution, Sections 33 to 46**; the **African Charter on Human and People's Rights** also guarantees that they are **enforceable in Nigerian courts of law**. The five named: **freedom of religion, freedom of speech, right to education, right to freedom of movement, right to life**.

**14.** *The five abuses and their tags.* **Extra-judicial killing** — the **police and military** implicated in killings, **torture** and **excessive use of force**; **#EndSARS, October 2020**, drew international attention to **police brutality**; the categories are **law enforcement officials, non-state actors, death sentences, and deaths in custody**. **Rape** — obstructed by **social stigma, underreporting, inadequate legal frameworks, lack of access to justice and limited support services for survivors**; **about 367,000 women and gender-related issues in 2021**. **Freedom of expression** — restriction of **speech and press, including online media**; journalists faced **threats, intimidation, arrest, detention and sometimes violence**, particularly over **political corruption and security**; government control exercised through the **National Broadcasting Commission**. **Child marriage** — **prohibited under international law**, **43% of girls married before 18 and 16% before 15** (Aliogo, 2023). **Domestic violence** — the **Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act 2015** criminalises abuse and provides for **reporting, prosecution and support services**, but **underreporting, weak enforcement, limited awareness and inaccessible support** persist.

**15.** *The conclusion.* A nation's **attitudes towards human rights are indicators of the values its political and other leaders have espoused regarding human life**. Human rights are **not validated by the number of international treaties countries and their governments have signed**, because the **international documents are only standards** — statements of the **additional safeguards individuals demand from the state** — and a signature creates no protection by itself. What the lesson demands instead is domestic enforcement: **every government agency has to enforce the norms established by the Constitution**, and **penalties should be imposed regardless of socioeconomic class**. The measure is therefore what the state actually does to and for people, not its treaty record.

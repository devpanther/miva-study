## Section A — Multiple choice (12)

**1.** The five stages of SQ3R, in the order the strategy requires, are:
a) Scan, Query, Read, Record, Recall
b) Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review
c) Survey, Question, Read, Reflect, Revise
d) Skim, Question, Recite, Read, Review

**2.** Before reading a chapter in detail, a student flips through it noting the chapter title, the subtitles and the captions of the illustrations. This is the Survey stage, and its stated purpose is to give:
a) The answers to the chapter's end-of-chapter questions
b) A sense of the text's structure and main ideas
c) A verbal summary of the key concepts in the student's own words
d) A written outline for long-term retention

**3.** The lesson is emphatic about one thing in the Question stage. It is that at this point you:
a) Answer the questions printed at the end of the passage
b) Ask your own questions, generated from what the survey revealed
c) Ask the lecturer for the questions that will be examined
d) Write questions only after you have finished reading the passage

**4.** The Read stage is said to have two goals. They are:
a) To read quickly, and to read again slowly
b) To read for unfamiliar vocabulary, and to look those words up
c) To read to answer the questions you generated, and to engage with the material for a fuller understanding
d) To read the headings, and then to read the captions

**5.** "Vocalise your thoughts" describes which stage, and what does it require?
a) Survey — reading the headings aloud so they register
b) Question — asking the questions aloud rather than writing them
c) Recite — verbally explaining the key points, words and concepts
d) Review — reading the finished summary aloud to a study partner

**6.** In the Review stage the reader is told to:
a) Re-read the passage from the beginning at normal speed
b) Create summaries, outlines or notes, revisiting them and the text
c) Generate a fresh set of questions about the material
d) Explain the material to another person without notes

**7.** Reading strategies are defined in the lesson as:
a) Rules of grammar that govern how written text is constructed
b) Techniques and approaches used to enhance comprehension, retention and overall effectiveness when engaging with written text
c) The speed at which a competent reader processes an academic page
d) The five stages of SQ3R

**8.** A student surveys, questions, reads attentively and recites the key points aloud the same evening. A week later she can recall almost nothing of the chapter. Which stage did she most likely omit, and why does that explain the loss?
a) Survey — without an overview nothing has a structure to attach to
b) Question — without guiding questions the reading was unfocused
c) Recite — she reinforced nothing at the time of reading
d) Review — it is the stage the lesson ties specifically to maintaining understanding over time and recalling it in exams

**9.** A passage states that the Renaissance "marked a shift from medieval traditions to a focus on humanism and individualism." A reader who concludes from this that medieval European thought had been less centred on the individual is working with:
a) Explicit meaning, because the shift is directly stated
b) Implicit meaning, reached by inference from an explicitly stated detail
c) An opinion, because it cannot be verified

**10.** Context clues are best described as:
a) Background knowledge the reader brings from outside the text

**11.** Which of the following is the distinction the lesson draws between a fact and an opinion?
a) A fact appears early in a text; an opinion appears in the conclusion

**12.** An inference, as the lesson defines it, is:
a) A guess made when the text gives no usable information

## Section B — Short answer (3)

**13.** Name the five stages of SQ3R in order and state, in one line each, what the stage actually requires the reader to do.

**14.** Distinguish explicit meaning, implicit meaning and inference, giving a one-line test that identifies each.

**15.** SQ3R insists that the reader generate their own questions after surveying. Explain why this stage exists, and say what specifically is lost by a reader who goes straight from Survey to Read.

## Answers

**1. b** — *the SQ3R acronym*: S + Q + 3R = Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review. (a) replaces four of the five terms with plausible study-skills verbs; (c) is the common error of substituting "Reflect" and "Revise" for Recite and Review, which loses the closed-book retrieval step entirely; (d) inverts Read and Recite, which is impossible — you cannot recite a text you have not yet read.

**2. b** — *Survey*: a fast scan of the whole text for headings, subheadings, images and captions, yielding the text's structure and main ideas. (a) belongs to no SQ3R stage; the printed questions are answered after reading. (c) is Recite. (d) is Review. Each wrong option encodes the same error — treating Survey as producing an outcome that only a later stage produces.

**3. b** — *the Question stage*: "this is where you start asking questions, not answering the questions from the passage"; they are generated from the survey and guide the reading. (a) is the exact confusion the lesson warns against. (c) mistakes a study strategy for exam prediction. (d) collapses Question into Recite by moving it after reading, destroying its function of directing attention.

**4. c** — *the two goals of Read*: answer your generated questions, and engage with the material for a fuller understanding. (a) confuses Read with a survey-then-read sequence. (b) reduces Read to vocabulary work, which is what context clues handle. (d) is Survey, not Read. Option (c) matters because students who drop the second goal read only for their own questions and miss everything they failed to anticipate.

**5. c** — *Recite*: "vocalise your thoughts" — verbally explaining the key points, words and concepts, in your own words and without the text. The wrong options each attach vocalising to a stage where it does no retrieval work: reading headings aloud (a) is still Survey, saying questions aloud (b) is still Question, and reading a finished summary aloud (d) is Review with a voice, not retrieval.

**6. b** — *Review*: creating summaries, outlines or notes and revisiting them and the text. (a) is re-reading, which consolidates nothing. (c) restarts the Question stage. (d) is Recite — the common Recite/Review swap: Recite is closed-book and oral, Review produces written notes.

**7. b** — *reading strategies defined*: techniques and approaches enhancing comprehension, retention and overall effectiveness, helping readers process information, extract meaning and gain deeper understanding. (d) is the trap — SQ3R is one strategy within the category, not the category itself. (a) confuses strategy with grammar; (c) confuses strategy with reading rate.

**8. d** — *Review as the retention stage*: the lesson ties Review specifically to maintaining a deep understanding "over time" and being able to recall and apply it in exams or discussions. The scenario explicitly credits her with Survey, Question, Read and Recite, so (a), (b) and (c) each blame a stage she performed; (c) is the sharpest distractor because Recite does reinforce — but only at the moment of reading, which is why Review exists as a separate, repeated stage.

**9. b** — *implicit meaning and inference*: the shift is stated, but the conclusion about medieval thought is not; the reader reaches it by combining the stated detail with background knowledge. (a) mistakes the stated premise for the unstated conclusion. (c) confuses a text-anchored inference with a subjective opinion. (d) misapplies context clues, which resolve unfamiliar *words*, not conclusions.

**10. b** — *context clues*: hints within the text enabling the reader to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word. (a) is background knowledge, which contributes to inference but is not *within* the text — this is the key discrimination. (c) is Survey material. (d) confuses a comprehension aid with a subjective response.

**11. b** — *fact vs opinion*: objective and verifiable against subjective; the lesson makes separating the two the core of critical thinking. (a) invents a positional rule. (c) crosses two different distinctions — an opinion can be stated explicitly and a fact can be implied. (d) misassigns the terms to writer and reader instead of to verifiability.

**12. b** — *inference*: a logical conclusion drawn from explicit details plus background knowledge. (a) makes it a guess, which removes the requirement that it be supported by stated details. (c) confuses inference with disagreement. (d) describes paraphrase of explicit meaning, which requires no inference at all.

**13.** *The five stages of SQ3R.* **Survey** — quickly scan the entire text before detailed reading, taking in headings, subheadings, images and captions, to establish its structure and main ideas. **Question** — generate your own questions from what the survey revealed, to guide the reading and focus it on the most critical information; you are asking, not answering the passage's questions. **Read** — read the text properly, with two goals: to answer the questions you generated, and to engage with the material for a fuller understanding. **Recite** — vocalise your thoughts, verbally explaining the key points, words and concepts in your own words without the text in front of you. **Review** — create summaries, outlines or notes and revisit them along with the text, so understanding is maintained over time and can be recalled and applied in exams or discussion.

**14.** *Explicit vs implicit vs inference.* **Explicit meaning** is directly stated in the text and requires no inference — test: you can underline the sentence that says it. **Implicit meaning** is unspoken or merely suggested and must be worked out by the reader — test: the idea is clearly there but no sentence states it. **Inference** is the reader's act of reaching that implicit meaning: a logical conclusion drawn from explicit details in the text combined with background knowledge — test: you can name the stated details that support it, which is what separates an inference from a guess or an opinion. Together explicit and implicit meaning give a nuanced understanding of a text beyond surface-level comprehension.

**15.** *Purpose of the Question stage.* The stage converts a passive reader into an active one. Questions the reader generates come from their own gaps and from what the survey has just revealed, so they set a purpose before reading begins, direct attention to the most critical information rather than to whatever the eye lands on, and provide an immediate self-test — after reading you either answered them or you did not. A reader who goes straight from Survey to Read loses all three: they read without a target, so attention distributes evenly over important and unimportant material; they have no criterion for what counts as key information; and they finish with no way of checking comprehension except a vague sense of having read it, which is exactly the failure the lesson opens with — reading a text and forgetting it minutes later. They are also left dependent on the passage's own end-of-chapter questions, which test the book's priorities rather than the reader's gaps and can only be attempted after the reading is already done.

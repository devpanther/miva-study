# GST_122 — Week 7 Questions

*Comprehension strategies: SQ3R, explicit and implicit meaning, inference, context clues, fact and opinion — sit this seven days after the Week 7 lecture, notes closed, as a retention test.*

## Multiple choice

**1.** The five stages of SQ3R, in the order the strategy requires, are:
A. Scan, Query, Read, Record, Recall
B. Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review
C. Survey, Question, Read, Reflect, Revise
D. Skim, Question, Recite, Read, Review

**2.** Before reading a chapter in detail, a student flips through it noting the chapter title, the subtitles and the captions of the illustrations. This is the Survey stage, and its stated purpose is to give:
A. A sense of the text's structure and main ideas
B. A verbal explanation of the key concepts in the student's own words
C. Answers to the questions printed at the end of the chapter
D. A written outline that will be revisited to maintain understanding over time

**3.** The lesson is emphatic about one thing in the Question stage. It is that at this point you:
A. Answer the questions printed at the end of the passage
B. Ask the lecturer for the questions that will be examined
C. Write questions only after you have finished reading the passage
D. Ask your own questions, generated from what the survey revealed

**4.** The Read stage is said to have two goals. They are:
A. To read quickly, and then to read again slowly
B. To read for unfamiliar vocabulary, and to look those words up
C. To read to answer the questions you generated, and to engage with the material for a fuller understanding
D. To read the headings, and then to read the captions

**5.** "Vocalise your thoughts" describes which stage, and what does it require?
A. Survey — reading the headings aloud so that they register before detailed reading begins
B. Question — asking your generated questions aloud rather than writing them down
C. Review — reading the finished summary aloud to a study partner who checks it

**6.** In the Review stage the reader is told to:
A. Create summaries, outlines or notes, revisiting them and the text
B. Re-read the passage from the beginning at normal speed

**7.** Reading strategies are defined in the lesson as:

**8.** A student surveys, questions, reads attentively and recites the key points aloud the same evening. A week later she can recall almost nothing of the chapter. Which stage did she most likely omit, and why does that explain the loss?

**9.** A passage states that the Renaissance "marked a shift from medieval traditions to a focus on humanism and individualism." A reader who concludes from this that medieval European thought had been less centred on the individual is working with:

## Short answer

**13.** Name the five stages of SQ3R in order and state, in one line each, what the stage actually requires the reader to do.

**14.** Distinguish explicit meaning, implicit meaning and inference, giving a one-line test that identifies each.

**15.** SQ3R insists that the reader generate their own questions after surveying. Explain why this stage exists, and say what specifically is lost by a reader who goes straight from Survey to Read.

## Answers

**1. B** — *the SQ3R acronym*: S + Q + 3R = **Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review**. **A** replaces four of the five terms with plausible study-skills verbs. **C** is the common error of substituting "Reflect" and "Revise" for Recite and Review, which loses the closed-book retrieval step entirely. **D** inverts Read and Recite, which is impossible — you cannot recite a text you have not yet read.

**2. A** — *Survey*: a fast scan of the whole text for headings, subheadings, images and captions, yielding the text's **structure and main ideas**. **B** is Recite, **C** belongs to no SQ3R stage (the printed questions are answered after reading), and **D** is Review. Each wrong option makes Survey produce an outcome that only a later stage produces — and note that the correct answer here is the shortest of the four.

**3. D** — *the Question stage*: "this is where you start asking questions, **not answering the questions from the passage**"; the questions are generated from the survey and guide the reading. **A** is the exact confusion the lesson warns against. **B** mistakes a study strategy for exam prediction. **C** collapses Question into Recite by moving it after reading, destroying its function of directing attention.

**4. C** — *the two goals of Read*: **answer the questions you generated**, and **engage with the material to gain a fuller understanding**. **A** confuses Read with a survey-then-read sequence. **B** reduces Read to vocabulary work, which is what context clues handle. **D** is Survey, not Read. The second goal is the one students drop, and dropping it means reading only for your own questions and missing everything you failed to anticipate.

**5. D** — *Recite*: "vocalise your thoughts" — **verbally explaining the key points, words and concepts** in your own words, without the text. The wrong options each attach vocalising to a stage where it does no retrieval work: reading headings aloud (**A**) is still Survey, saying your questions aloud (**B**) is still Question, and reading a finished summary aloud (**C**) is Review with a voice, not retrieval. The right answer is the shortest option here.

**6. A** — *Review*: **creating summaries, outlines or notes and revisiting them and the text**. **B** is plain re-reading, which consolidates nothing. **C** restarts the Question stage. **D** is Recite — the common Recite/Review swap: Recite is closed-book and oral, Review produces written material that is returned to.

**7. B** — *reading strategies defined*: **techniques and approaches used to enhance comprehension, retention and overall effectiveness** when engaging with written text, helping readers process information, extract meaning and gain deeper understanding. **D** is the trap — SQ3R is one strategy *within* the category, not the category itself. **A** confuses strategy with grammar; **C** confuses strategy with reading rate.

**8. C** — *Review as the retention stage*: the lesson ties Review specifically to maintaining a deep understanding **over time** and being able to recall and apply it in exams or discussions. The scenario explicitly credits her with Survey, Question, Read and Recite, so **A**, **B** and **D** each blame a stage she performed. **D** is the sharpest distractor because Recite does reinforce — but only at the moment of reading, which is precisely why Review exists as a separate, repeated stage.

**9. D** — *implicit meaning and inference*: the shift is stated, but the conclusion about medieval thought is not; the reader reaches it by combining the stated detail with background knowledge. **A** mistakes the stated premise for the unstated conclusion. **B** confuses a text-anchored inference with a subjective opinion. **C** misapplies context clues, which resolve unfamiliar **words**, not conclusions.

**10. A** — *context clues*: **hints within the text** that enable the reader to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word. **B** is background knowledge, which contributes to inference but is not *within* the text — that is the key discrimination. **C** is Survey material. **D** confuses a comprehension aid with a subjective response.

**11. B** — *fact vs opinion*: **objective and verifiable** against **subjective**; the lesson makes separating the two the core of critical thinking. **A** invents a positional rule. **C** crosses two different distinctions — an opinion can be stated explicitly, and a fact can be implied. **D** misassigns the terms to writer and reader instead of to verifiability.

**12. C** — *inference*: **a logical conclusion drawn from explicit details plus background knowledge**. **A** makes it a guess, which removes the requirement that it be supported by stated details. **B** confuses inference with disagreement. **D** describes paraphrase of explicit meaning, which requires no inference at all.

**13.** *The five stages of SQ3R.* **Survey** — quickly scan the entire text before detailed reading, taking in headings, subheadings, images and captions, to establish its structure and main ideas. **Question** — generate your own questions from what the survey revealed, to guide the reading and focus it on the most critical information; you are asking, not answering the passage's questions. **Read** — read the text properly, with two goals: to answer the questions you generated, and to engage with the material to gain a fuller understanding. **Recite** — vocalise your thoughts, verbally explaining the key points, words and concepts in your own words without the text in front of you. **Review** — create summaries, outlines or notes and revisit them along with the text, so that understanding is maintained over time and can be recalled and applied in exams or discussion.

**14.** *Explicit vs implicit vs inference.* **Explicit meaning** is directly stated in the text and requires no inference — test: you can underline the sentence that says it. **Implicit meaning** is unspoken or merely suggested and must be worked out by the reader — test: the idea is clearly there but no sentence states it. **Inference** is the reader's act of reaching that implicit meaning: a logical conclusion drawn from explicit details in the text combined with background knowledge — test: you can name the stated details that support it, which is what separates an inference from a guess or an opinion. Together, explicit and implicit meaning give a nuanced understanding of a text beyond surface-level comprehension.

**15.** *Purpose of the Question stage.* The stage converts a passive reader into an active one. Questions the reader generates come from their own gaps and from what the survey has just revealed, so they set a purpose before reading begins, direct attention to the most critical information rather than to whatever the eye lands on, and provide an immediate self-test — after reading, you either answered them or you did not. A reader who goes straight from Survey to Read loses all three: they read without a target, so attention distributes evenly over important and unimportant material; they have no criterion for what counts as key information; and they finish with no way of checking comprehension except a vague sense of having read it, which is exactly the failure the lesson opens with — reading a text and forgetting it minutes later. They are also left dependent on the passage's own end-of-chapter questions, which test the book's priorities rather than the reader's gaps and can only be attempted once the reading is already done.

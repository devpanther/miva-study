## The three learning objectives

Enhance the ability to **actively listen in various contexts**, leading to **better comprehension and retention** of information in lectures, discussions and conversations; **apply listening skills and organise notes systematically and review them**; **adapt note-taking strategies to various contexts** — **academic lectures, business meetings, or social interactions**.

## Importance of active listening — the three headings

- **Enhanced Understanding** — the listener grasps "not only the **words spoken** but also the underlying **emotions, intentions, and context**."
- **Conflict Resolution** — "a key skill"; lets individuals **acknowledge each other's perspectives**, a crucial step in **finding common ground** and resolving disputes.
- **Problem Solving** — by **fully comprehending the details** of a problem, the listener is better equipped to give **informed solutions or suggestions**.

Framing line: active listening is "a **fundamental component of effective communication**" playing a crucial role in "both **personal and professional** interactions."

## The six techniques for becoming a more active listener

1. **Maintain Eye Contact** — conveys attention and interest; a **non-verbal cue** signalling active engagement.
2. **Avoid Interrupting** — let them finish; interruptions **hinder the flow of communication** and make the speaker **feel unheard**.
3. **Minimise Distractions.**
4. **Ask Open-Ended Questions.**
5. **Use Verbal and Non-Verbal Affirmations** — **nodding, smiling**, or cues like **"I see," "I understand," "Tell me more"** encourage the speaker to continue.
6. **Paraphrase and Summarise** — periodically restating shows you are **trying to comprehend accurately** and "provides an opportunity for **clarification**."

## Tips on note-taking (deck order)

- **Have writing materials**: pen/pencil · book/jotter/sticky notes · highlighters · paper clips, etc.
- **Use abbreviations and symbols** — a personal system to write faster **without sacrificing clarity**.
- **Do not write verbatim** — main ideas, key concepts, supporting evidence only.
- **Headings and Subheadings** — organise notes to **create a clear structure**; helps in **finding specific information later**.
- **Highlighting or Underlining** — different colours or underlines to emphasise key points/terms; a **visual cue** that eases review.
- **Cornell Note-Taking Method** — notes / cues / summary.
- **Bullet points** — make information **scannable and easier to review**.
- **Mind mapping** — visual, main ideas → subtopics → supporting details.

**Further reading:** Emilia Hardman (2012), *Active Listening 101: How to Turn Down Your Volume to Turn Up Your Communication Skills*; The Open University (2016), *Reading and note taking – preparation for study*.

## The Summary card (labelled "Critical Listening", actually reasoning in presentations)

Establish a **central thesis** and structure the presentation logically — **introduction, main points, supporting evidence, counterarguments, compelling conclusion**. Use **deductive reasoning** (general premises → specific conclusions) and **inductive reasoning** (specific observations → broader generalizations). Watch for **logical fallacies** such as **strawman arguments** and **hasty generalizations**; **eliminate faulty reasoning**; **cite reliable sources** to build trust; **seek external feedback**.

## Commonly confused

- **Cornell vs mind mapping** — Cornell is a **page layout** in three named zones (cues, notes, summary) with a summary written **shortly after the lecture**; mind mapping is a **visual web** linking main ideas to subtopics and supporting details. Anything mentioning "cue column" or "summary section" is Cornell.
- **Headings/subheadings vs bullet points vs highlighting** — headings give **structure for later retrieval**; bullet points make notes **scannable**; highlighting/underlining is the **colour-based visual cue** for key terms. Three separate tips, do not merge.
- **Paraphrase and Summarise (listening technique) vs the Cornell summary** — the first is spoken back to the speaker for **clarification**; the second is written on your own page **after** the lecture.
- **Verbal and non-verbal affirmations vs maintaining eye contact** — affirmations include **nodding and smiling as well as "I see"/"Tell me more"**; eye contact is its own separate technique.
- **Avoid interrupting vs minimise distractions** — interrupting is about **the speaker feeling unheard** and the flow breaking; distractions are about **your own attention**.
- **Abbreviations in notes vs in formal writing** — allowed and encouraged in personal notes; **may not be tolerated in formal writings**. Exam questions like to hinge on that caveat.
- **Deductive vs inductive** (Summary card) — deductive = **general → specific**; inductive = **specific → general**. Strawman and hasty generalization are the two named fallacies.
- **Week 11 vs Week 12 listening lists** — Week 11 gave six *Importance of Listening* headings and six listening strategies; Week 12 gives **three** importances of *active* listening and **six techniques**. Don't cross-answer.

# Wednesday — GST_122 fast-hour check

*Active listening's three benefits and six techniques, the note-taking tips, and deductive versus inductive reasoning.*
*Five questions, straight after the fast hour. Recall only. Score out of 5.*

**1.** The Cornell method divides your paper into which three sections?
A. Notes, cues and a summary
B. Headings, bullets and highlights
C. Ideas, subtopics and details
D. Thesis, evidence and conclusion

**2.** Which named benefit of active listening is about grasping a problem's details?
A. Enhanced Understanding
B. Conflict Resolution
C. Problem Solving
D. Continuous Improvement

**3.** In the deck's note-taking examples, the arrow symbol stands for what?
A. "with"
B. "leads to"
C. "therefore"
D. "important"

**4.** Which tip creates a visual web linking main ideas to subtopics and details?
A. Highlighting or underlining
B. Bullet points
C. Headings and subheadings
D. Mind mapping

**5.** Deductive reasoning, on the summary card, moves in which direction?
A. General premises to specific conclusions
B. Specific observations to broad generalizations
C. Evidence to counterargument
D. Audience analysis to thesis

## Answers

**1. A** — Cornell's three zones are cues, notes and a summary written shortly after the lecture. Main ideas linked to subtopics and details is mind mapping; the last option belongs to the summary card on presentations.

**2. C** — Fully comprehending the details equips the listener to give informed solutions. Enhanced Understanding is about words plus emotions, intentions and context; Conflict Resolution is acknowledging perspectives to find common ground.

**3. B** — The arrow is given as shorthand for leads to, and w/ for with. The deck warns that such abbreviations are for personal notes and may not be tolerated in formal writings.

**4. D** — Mind mapping builds visual representations linking main ideas to subtopics and supporting details. Highlighting is a colour cue for key terms, bullets make notes scannable, and headings give structure for later retrieval.

**5. A** — Deductive reasoning draws specific conclusions from general premises; the reverse, specific observations to broader generalizations, is inductive. The two named fallacies to watch for are strawman and hasty generalization.

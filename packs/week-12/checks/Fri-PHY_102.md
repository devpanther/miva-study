**1.** A 1200 W appliance designed for a 230 V supply is to be run from a 115 V line through an ideal transformer. What turns ratio N₂/N₁ is needed, and what kind of transformer is it?
A. N₂/N₁ = 1/2, step-down
B. N₂/N₁ = 2, step-down
C. N₂/N₁ = 4, step-up
D. N₂/N₁ = 2, step-up

**2.** A 1000 W hair dryer rated for 120 V is run from a 240 V outlet through an ideal transformer. What current does it draw from the outlet?
A. 8.3 A
B. 4.2 A
C. 2.1 A
D. 0.24 A

**3.** A 120 V supply connected to the primary of an ideal transformer sees an effective resistance of 12 Ω. The turns ratio is N₂/N₁ = 3. What is the resistance of the load on the secondary?
A. 108 Ω
B. 36 Ω
C. 4.0 Ω
D. 1.3 Ω

**4.** A 1000 W hair dryer rated for 120 V is run from a 240 V outlet through an ideal transformer. What resistance does the dryer appear to have to the 240 V outlet?
A. 14 Ω

**5.** The secondary of an ideal transformer has 60 turns and gives 9.0 V. The primary has 1600 turns. What voltage is applied to the primary?
A. 9.0 V
B. 0.34 V
C. 27 V

**6.** An ideal transformer has N₂/N₁ = 1/6 and its primary draws 0.50 A. What current flows in the secondary?
A. 0.083 A
B. 3.0 A

**7.** An ideal transformer draws 0.50 A from a 240 V supply. Its secondary feeds a heater of resistance 7.5 Ω. What current flows in the heater?
A. 4.0 A

**9.** A 720 W appliance rated for 240 V is run from a 120 V line through an ideal transformer. Calculate the current drawn from the line and the appliance's own resistance.

**10.** A 1200 W appliance rated for 110 V is run from a 220 V outlet through an ideal transformer. Calculate the resistance the appliance appears to have to the outlet, and its own resistance.

C. 40 Ω apparent, 10 Ω own

**11.** An ideal transformer has N₂/N₁ = 4, a 30 V rms supply on the primary and a 60 Ω resistor across the secondary. Calculate the primary current and the resistance the supply sees.
A. 2.0 A, 3.75 Ω

C. 0.50 A, 3.75 Ω
D. 8.0 A, 15 Ω

**12.** A 500 W appliance rated for 250 V runs from a 125 V line through an ideal transformer. A student writes: I₁ = 500/125 = 4.0 A, so V₁/I₁ = 125/4.0 = 31.25 Ω; N₂/N₁ = 2, so the appliance's resistance is 31.25 × 2 = 62.5 Ω. What is the appliance's resistance?
A. 62.5 Ω, and the working is right
B. 31.25 Ω, which is what the line sees
C. 15.6 Ω, the ratio being 1/2 rather than 2
D. 125 Ω, the turns ratio having to be squared

---

## Answers

**1. D** — *Turns ratio from the appliance rating and the line voltage.* The line is the primary (V₁ = 115 V) and the appliance is the load on the secondary (V₂ = 230 V). N₂/N₁ = V₂/V₁ = 230/115 = 2. Since N₂ > N₁ it is a step-up transformer.

1/2 puts the appliance's rating on the primary, swapping the two sides; '2, step-down' has the ratio right but the definition wrong, since more secondary turns means step-up; 4 squares the ratio, a square that belongs only in the resistance formula.

**2. B** — *Line current from the average power.* The outlet is the primary, so P_av = V₁I₁ gives I₁ = 1000/240 = 4.17 A, about 4.2 A.

8.3 A is I₂ = 1000/120, the current in the dryer's own element, not what the outlet supplies; 2.1 A halves the line current a second time, applying the 1/2 ratio to a current that was already the primary's; 0.24 A is V/P, the ratio inverted.

**3. A** — *Load resistance from the resistance seen by the supply.* V₁/I₁ = R/(N₂/N₁)², so R = (V₁/I₁)(N₂/N₁)² = 12 × 3² = 12 × 9 = 108 Ω. Check: V₂ = 360 V, I₁ = 10 A so I₂ = 10/3 A, and 360/(10/3) = 108 Ω.

36 Ω multiplies by the ratio 3 instead of by its square; 4.0 Ω divides by the ratio; 1.3 Ω divides by the square, the formula applied backwards.

**4. C** — *Apparent resistance from the supply voltage and current.* The apparent resistance is what the supply sees: V₁/I₁ with I₁ = P/V₁ = 1000/240 = 4.17 A, so V₁/I₁ = 240/4.17 = 57.6 Ω, about 58 Ω. Equivalently V₁²/P = 57 600/1000 = 57.6 Ω.

14 Ω is the dryer's own resistance, V₂²/P = 120²/1000 = 14.4 Ω, the answer to a different question; 29 Ω divides 57.6 by the ratio 2 instead of leaving it alone; 115 Ω multiplies it by 2.

**5. D** — *Primary voltage from the turns ratio.* V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁, so V₁ = V₂ (N₁/N₂) = 9.0 × (1600/60) = 9.0 × 26.7 = 240 V.

9.0 V assumes the voltages are equal; 0.34 V is 9.0 × (60/1600), the ratio inverted, which would make the 1600-turn winding the low-voltage side; 27 V is the turns ratio 1600/60 quoted as a voltage.

**6. B** — *Current ratio as the inverse of the turns ratio.* I₁V₁ = I₂V₂ and V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁ = 1/6, so I₂ = I₁ (V₁/V₂) = I₁ (N₁/N₂) = 0.50 × 6 = 3.0 A. Stepping the voltage down 6 times steps the current up 6 times.

0.083 A is 0.50/6, applying the turns ratio to the current the same way as to the voltage; 0.50 A assumes the currents are equal, which would lose five sixths of the power; 18 A multiplies by 6², a square that belongs in the resistance formula only.

**7. A** — *Secondary current from power and load resistance.* Power in = I₁V₁ = 0.50 × 240 = 120 W, and for an ideal transformer this is the power in the heater: I₂²R = 120, so I₂ = √(120/7.5) = √16 = 4.0 A. Check: V₂ = 4.0 × 7.5 = 30 V, and 30 × 4.0 = 120 W.

16 A is P/R without the square root, a current squared; 0.50 A assumes the secondary current equals the primary's; 32 A is V₁/R, connecting the heater straight to 240 V.

**8. A** — *Voltage is bought with current.* I₁V₁ = I₂V₂ is the whole energy statement: if V₂ = 2V₁ then I₂ = I₁/2, and the power I₂V₂ is unchanged. A transformer redistributes voltage and current; it never adds watts.

'Double the current' would give four times the power out of nothing; 'same current, double the power' likewise creates energy; 'half the power' would mean half the input vanished inside an ideal device, which by definition has no losses.

**9. C** — *Line current and load resistance in a step-up transformer.* The 120 V line feeds the primary, so P = V₁I₁ gives I₁ = 720/120 = 6.0 A. The line sees V₁/I₁ = 20 Ω, and R = (V₁/I₁)(N₂/N₁)² with N₂/N₁ = 240/120 = 2, so R = 20 × 4 = 80 Ω. The secondary side agrees: I₂ = 720/240 = 3.0 A and R = 240/3.0 = 80 Ω.

3.0 A is the current in the appliance, not in the line; 40 Ω leaves the turns ratio unsquared; 20 Ω is the resistance the line sees, not the appliance's own.

**10. C** — *Apparent versus actual resistance in a step-down.* I₁ = P/V₁ = 1200/220 = 5.45 A, so the outlet sees V₁/I₁ = 220/5.45 = 40 Ω. The turns ratio is N₂/N₁ = 110/220 = 1/2, and R = (V₁/I₁)(N₂/N₁)² = 40 × 1/4 = 10 Ω. Check from the other side: I₂ = 1200/110 = 10.9 A and R = 110/10.9 = 10 Ω.

20 Ω uses the ratio without squaring it; 161 Ω divides by (1/2)² instead of multiplying; and swapping the two confuses what the outlet sees with what the appliance is.

**11. B** — *Loaded step-up transformer from the supply side.* V₂ = V₁(N₂/N₁) = 30 × 4 = 120 V, so I₂ = V₂/R = 120/60 = 2.0 A. Power is conserved, I₁V₁ = I₂V₂, giving I₁ = (2.0)(120)/30 = 8.0 A. The supply therefore sees V₁/I₁ = 30/8.0 = 3.75 Ω, which agrees with R/(N₂/N₁)² = 60/16 = 3.75 Ω.

2.0 A takes the primary current to equal the secondary current; 0.50 A applies the turns ratio to the current the same way as to the voltage; 15 Ω divides R by 4 rather than by 4².

**12. D** — *Diagnosing an unsquared turns ratio.* The current, the apparent resistance 31.25 Ω and the ratio 2 are all correct; the error is in the last line, because V₁/I₁ = R/(N₂/N₁)² means R = (V₁/I₁)(N₂/N₁)² = 31.25 × 2² = 125 Ω. The secondary side confirms it: I₂ = 500/250 = 2.0 A and R = V₂/I₂ = 250/2.0 = 125 Ω, or V₂²/P = 125 Ω.

62.5 Ω keeps the unsquared ratio; 31.25 Ω is what the line sees, not the appliance; and 1/2 inverts the ratio, since the appliance sits on the higher-voltage side.

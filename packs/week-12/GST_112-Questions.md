## Answers

**Section A**

1. **a** — Crime violates social laws; deviant behaviour violates social norms and rules. Deviance is the broader term; crime carries legal consequences such as fines or imprisonment.

2. **b** — Conflict Theory. Structural Functionalism says deviance plays a constructive role; Labelling Theory blames the label applied to a person.

3. **c** — Social Learning Theory — a theory of crime, alongside Rational Choice (weighing cost/risk against benefits) and Strain (social structures pressure citizens into crime).

4. **d** — The University of Ibadan, as a social group with patriotic and philanthropic founding beliefs — not intended to be a secret cult.

5. **d** — About forty-five (45), all with an elaborate hierarchy, insignia and distinct attire.

6. **a** — 21 years for membership or soliciting members of an unlawful society.

7. **b** — A 3-year sentence; the 21-year term is for membership, not for hosting a meeting.

8. **c** — 838 in 2018 to 4,600 in 2022 — an indication that kidnap cases had quadrupled.

9. **c** — Reduced to 10,249; the armed robbery figures fall while the kidnapping figures rise.

10. **d** — Unemployment — frustrated graduates searching for jobs for three or more years turn to it for survival; the political factor is the other cause named.

11. **a** — Proliferation of firearms, with socio-economic factor, corruption, weak law enforcement, drug abuse and societal factor. Erosion of trust and psychological impact are effects; humanitarian crisis is an effect of kidnapping.

12. **b** — The Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC).

**Section B**

**13.** Social vices are actions that stray from accepted moral norms; Asmau lists prostitution, indecent dressing, robbery, cultism, pocket-picking, drug addiction, exam fraud, hooliganism, thuggery, gambling, smoking, premarital sexual activity and rape as common among young men and women. They classify into two, crime and deviance: committing a crime violates social laws, while deviant behaviour violates social norms and rules. Theories of deviance: Structural Functionalism (deviance plays a constructive role because it brings different parts of the population together to agree what is acceptable and what is not); Social Strain Typology (deviance rests on two criteria — a person's motivations or adherence to cultural goals, and a person's belief in how to attain her goals); Conflict Theory (deviance as a consequence of material inequality); Labelling Theory (deviance often follows from a deviant-like label being applied to a person). Theories of crime: Rational Choice Theory (criminals weigh cost or risk against benefits before deciding); Strain Theory (social structures within society may pressure citizens to commit crime); Social Learning Theory (criminal behaviour is acquired by observing and imitating others).

**14.** Campus cultism traces back to the early 1950s, when Prof. Wole Soyinka and friends — Ralph Opara, Ikhehare Aig-Imokhuede, Sylvanus Egbuchie, Nathaniel Oyelola, Pius Oleghe and Olumuyiwa Awe — founded the Pirates Confraternity as a social group at the University of Ibadan. Its founding beliefs were patriotic and philanthropic and it was not intended to be a secret cult: the major goals were to resist foreign norms nonviolently but intellectually and successfully, to resurrect the period of chivalry, and to find a lasting solution to tribalism and ethnicity. Over time such organizations grew to deadly proportions, and today about forty-five secret cults operate in tertiary institutions, all equipped with an elaborate hierarchy, insignia and distinct attire, meeting in odd places such as cemeteries, valleys and abandoned buildings, using knives, daggers, guns, axes and arrows, taking hard drugs, drinking human blood and acid-bathing victims. Because cultism costs lives, disrupts academic pursuits and fosters a culture of fear, it is classified as a crime. Penalties: anyone who allows cult meetings in their residence or a structure belonging to them commits a felony and receives a 3-year sentence; anyone found to be a member of a cult group or who solicits members for an unlawful society faces 21 years.

**15.** Kidnapping is the unlawful abduction and detention of individuals, usually for financial gain or motives such as ransom or revenge. Reported cases rose from 838 in 2018 to 4,600 in 2022 — quadrupled. Causes: unemployment is the major one, with frustrated graduates who have searched for jobs for three or more years turning to it for survival, and the political factor, as many politicians engage kidnappers to deal with opponents and their political power, popularity and economic muscle. Effects: humanitarian crisis, loss of lives, economic impact, security concerns, negative image, ransom culture, deterioration of social trust, escalation of violence, impact on education and human trafficking — it undermines personal security, disrupts businesses and tourism and pressures law enforcement. Armed robbery is the use of weapons to steal from individuals, homes, businesses or vehicles, often with violence and brutality; prison records give 11,858 cases in 2013, reduced to 10,249 in 2014. Causes: socio-economic factor, corruption, weak law enforcement, drug abuse, proliferation of firearms and societal factor. Effects: loss of lives and injuries, psychological impact, economic consequences, disruption of daily life and erosion of trust; it creates fear, reduces trust in law enforcement and hampers economic growth.

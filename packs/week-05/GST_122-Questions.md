## Multiple choice (12)

**2.** A novelist writes continuously about a character's childhood without stopping to plan or self-edit, hoping to discover unexpected details. Which technique is this, and why is "outlining" the wrong label?
a) Outlining — because it involves writing
b) Research

d) Drafting questions

**3.** A student planning an argumentative essay on the pros and cons of social media draws a central topic with branches for positive and negative points. Which technique is this, and why isn't it "clustering"?
a) Clustering
b) Listing

d) Storyboarding

**4.** A student preparing a paper on renewable energy gathers information on different energy types, their benefits, and their challenges before writing. Which pre-writing technique is this?
a) Brainstorming
b) Research

**5.** Before delivering a speech, a speaker writes out key points, supporting evidence, and a logical order of ideas. Which technique is this, and how does it differ from "listing"?
a) Outlining — it sequences points and evidence into a logical flow, while listing itemises points (often two-sided comparisons) without necessarily ordering them into an argument
b) Listing
c) Drafting questions
d) Storyboarding

**6.** A poet associates loose words and images around a central theme to spark ideas for a new poem. Which technique is this, and why is "mind mapping" the wrong label?
a) Mind mapping
b) Brainstorming

d) Freewriting

**7.** After finishing a first draft on a historical event, a writer revisits the introduction to sharpen its hook and reorders paragraphs to improve the essay's flow. Which post-writing stage is this, and why is "editing" the wrong label?
a) Editing

c) Revision — this works at the content/structure level (rewriting, reordering), while editing works at the sentence/grammar/style level

**8.** A journalist goes through a finished article correcting grammatical errors, improving sentence structure, and making the style consistent throughout. Which stage is this?
a) Revision
b) Editing for grammar and style

**9.** Just before submission, a novelist does one last pass over the manuscript purely to catch typos, misspellings, and punctuation slips — no rewriting, no restructuring. Which stage is this, and why is "editing" wrong here?

## Short answer (3)

**13.** Explain how revision, editing, and proofreading differ in what they focus on, and state the order in which the lesson places them within the post-writing sequence.

**14.** Choose two pre-writing techniques from this list that were not the correct answer in any multiple-choice question above — identifying audience and purpose, storyboarding, drafting questions, listing — and for each, give the example situation the lesson uses and explain what the technique is for.

**15.** According to the "Enhancing Business Proposal Writing" case study, name the three pre-writing activities and the one post-writing activity described for the proposal, and explain in one sentence why the case study needed both phases to produce an effective proposal.

## Answers

**1. b** — *brainstorming*: the source's own example ("a writer might brainstorm a list of related topics, such as the causes of climate change...") is explicitly brainstorming, not listing — listing in this course means organising already-identified points (e.g., similarities/differences between two figures), not generating a first rough topic list.
**2. c** — *freewriting*: continuous, unstructured writing used to discover unexpected material. Outlining is wrong because it requires a structured sequence of key points built in advance — the opposite of what's happening here.
**3. c** — *mind mapping*: branching a central topic (social media) into organised, opposing categories (pros/cons). Clustering is wrong because it is looser and associative (words/images around a theme), not organised branches from one topic.
**4. b** — *research*: gathering information — types, benefits, challenges — on a subject before writing is the defining activity of the research technique, distinct from brainstorming (generating ideas) or outlining (sequencing points).
**5. a** — *outlining*: sequencing key points and supporting evidence into a logical flow for a speech. Listing would only itemise points without necessarily ordering them into an argument.
**6. c** — *clustering*: associating words/images around a theme for creative exploration is the poet example given for clustering. Mind mapping is wrong because it organises branches from a central topic rather than free-associating loosely.
**7. c** — *revision*: revisiting the introduction and reordering paragraphs is content/structure work. Editing is wrong because it operates at the sentence/grammar/style level, not the structural level.
**8. b** — *editing for grammar and style*: correcting grammar, improving sentence structure, and ensuring stylistic consistency is exactly this stage's definition, distinct from revision (content/structure) and proofreading (final surface pass).
**9. d** — *proofreading*: a final surface-level pass for typos, misspellings, and punctuation, done after editing's grammar/style work — not editing itself, since no sentence-structure or style correction is happening here.
**10. a** — *peer review*: feedback from a classmate on clarity and argument strength. It differs from seeking feedback from instructors/editors in both source (peer vs authority figure) and typical depth (clarity/argument vs plot/character/storytelling craft).
**11. b** — *checking citations and references*, positioned before polishing transitions and before formatting and presentation in the ten-stage sequence given in the lesson — not after finalising conclusions, and not the same as proofreading or editing.
**12. c** — *polishing transitions*: refining paragraph-to-paragraph and idea-to-idea links, which in the given sequence comes after checking citations and references and before formatting and presentation.
**13.** Revision works at the content/structure level — reconsidering the introduction, reordering paragraphs, improving overall flow. Editing works at the sentence level — correcting grammar, improving sentence structure, ensuring consistent style. Proofreading is the final surface-level pass — catching typos, misspellings, and punctuation mistakes right before the piece is finished. In the lesson's sequence, revision comes first, editing follows (with peer review in between), and proofreading comes after that, closer to submission.
**14.** Identifying audience and purpose — example: a business professional preparing a report considers the target audience (executives, stakeholders) and purpose (inform, persuade), which shapes tone, style, and content. Storyboarding — example: a filmmaker plans the sequence of scenes in a movie visually, to organise the narrative structure. Drafting questions — example: a journalist preparing for an interview writes out questions in advance to make sure the interview covers relevant topics. Listing — example: a student preparing a compare-and-contrast essay on two historical figures creates a list of their similarities and differences to guide the essay's structure. (Any two of these four qualify.)
**15.** Pre-writing activities: research on the proposal's subject matter, brainstorming sessions to generate ideas and strategies, and a detailed outline (introduction, problem statement, solution, conclusion). Post-writing activity: editing, to refine language, ensure coherence, and strengthen the overall structure. Both phases were needed because careful preparation (pre-writing) gave the proposal depth and creativity, while meticulous refinement (post-writing) gave it the professionalism and clarity a business proposal requires — one phase without the other would leave it either unfocused or unpolished.

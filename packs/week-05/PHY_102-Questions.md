## Answers

**1. B** — *A junction needs three or more conductors; a loop is any closed conducting path.* Those are the lesson's definitions verbatim, and the "three or more" matters: in the lesson's own figures, points a and b are junctions while c, d, e and f are not, because only two conductors meet there and the same current simply passes through. A drops the threshold to two, which would make every bend a junction and give the empty equation I = I, and it makes a loop open-ended. C defines a junction by a symptom rather than a topology, and confines loops to battery-to-battery paths. D has the junction right but adds a condition to the loop: a closed path containing **no** source at all is still a perfectly valid loop, and loop equations round such paths are often the most useful ones.

**2. C** — *ΣI = 0 at a junction, in positive and out negative; conservation of charge.* Whatever charge arrives per second must leave per second, because in a steady current there is nowhere at a point for charge to accumulate. A has the right statement but the wrong conservation law — energy conservation is the **loop** rule. B replaces a local statement with a global one about "the battery"; the junction rule says nothing about where the current came from, and in a multi-source network there is no single battery current to equate to. D invents an equality between the incoming currents that has no basis; the incoming currents can be anything at all provided their algebraic total balances the outgoing ones.

**3. A** — *ΣV = 0 round any closed conducting path; conservation of energy.* Potential is a property of a point, so a charge carried round a closed path must return to the potential it started at; equivalently, the energy per unit charge supplied by the emfs equals the energy per unit charge lost in the IR drops. B confuses "sum of the potential changes" with "sum of the emfs" — the emfs alone do not sum to zero, they sum to ΣIR — and misattributes the conservation law. C attaches a false restriction (a loop with no source in it obeys the rule just as well, and such loops are routinely used) and cites Ohm's law, which supplies the IR terms but not the rule itself. D states equality of the individual drops instead of the vanishing of their algebraic sum; that would only be true in the special case of identical elements in series.

**4. A** — *The emf sign is a polarity-versus-travel test, and nothing else.* Travelling from **− to +** through a source you are climbing to a higher potential, so the term is **+ε**; from **+ to −** it is **−ε**. B is the classic corruption: it ties the emf's sign to the assumed current, but a battery keeps its polarity whether it is discharging or being charged, so the current has no vote — only the **IR** term consults the current. C is the correct test inverted, and it produces answers with exactly the right magnitude and the wrong sign, which is why it survives so long undetected. D grafts an extra condition onto a rule that has none, and it is unusable in practice: you do not know whether a battery is charging until *after* you have solved the system that this sign rule is needed to write.

**5. A** — *Travelling with the assumed current means going in the direction of decreasing potential, so the term is −IR; against it, +IR.* Current flows from high to low potential through a resistor, so moving along the assumed current is moving downhill (a drop, −IR) and moving against it is moving uphill (a rise, +IR). B is the reflex "IR is always negative": it happens to be right whenever you traverse with the current, which is most of the time in a simple loop, and it then fails silently on any outer or return path. C reverses the rule with a plausible-sounding reason — the resistor does dissipate energy, but that fact does not determine the sign of a term in a potential balance, the direction of travel does. D confuses the sign **convention**, which is fixed before the equations are solved, with the **solved value** of the current, which is not known until afterwards; if the sign rule needed the answer, the equations could never be written.

**6. B** — *n − 1 junction equations and b − n + 1 loop equations: 3 and 3, for 6 unknowns.* With n = 4 junctions, only 4 − 1 = **3** junction equations are independent, because each branch current appears once positively and once negatively across the full set, so the four equations sum identically to 0 = 0. That leaves 6 − 3 = **3** more equations, and the number of independent loops is exactly b − n + 1 = 6 − 4 + 1 = **3**. A uses all four junction equations, one of which is redundant, so the set is dependent and the elimination collapses. C over-counts the loops: only three are independent, and every further closed path traceable in the diagram is an algebraic combination of those three. D discards the junction rule entirely; a network with 6 branches simply does not possess 6 independent loops, and the loop rule alone cannot produce the current-splitting information the junction rule carries.

**7. A** — *V = IR; series resistances add, parallel reciprocals add.* Current I is in amperes (A), potential difference V in volts (V) and resistance R in ohms (Ω); in **series** the same current passes through everything and the total resistance is the sum, R_total = R₁ + R₂ + …; in **parallel** the same potential difference is across everything and 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …, so the total is smaller than the smallest branch. B swaps the two combination rules, the single most common slip and one that always produces a total on the wrong side of the individual values — a useful self-check. C inverts Ohm's law and writes a parallel formula with a **minus** sign, which would blow up for equal resistors. D gives current in coulombs (that is charge, not current — the ampere is a coulomb per second) and replaces the parallel rule by an average, which for two equal resistors would give R instead of the correct R/2.

**8. A** — *Opposing emfs subtract; all four resistances, internal ones included, add.* Travelling clockwise: through the 20 V battery from **− to +** gives +20; through the 8 V battery from **+ to −** gives −8; every resistor is traversed along the assumed current, so all IR terms are negative. 20 − 1I − 3I − 8 − 1I − 5I = 0 → 12 = 10I → **I = 1.2 A**. B (2.8 A) adds the emfs, 28/10 — reading the second battery as helping when it is entered at its + terminal and therefore opposes. C (1.5 A) has the net emf right but omits the two internal resistances, 12/8. D (3.5 A) makes both errors at once, 28/8.

**9. C** — *The 8 V battery has current forced into its + terminal, so it is being charged and reads V = ε + Ir.* With I = 1.2 A and r = 1 Ω, V = 8 + (1.2)(1) = **9.2 V** — above its own emf, which is the signature of a battery under charge. A (6.8 V) applies the discharging formula ε − Ir; that is the right formula for the **20 V** battery, whose terminal voltage is 20 − 1.2 = 18.8 V (option D, the right calculation attached to the wrong battery). B (8.0 V) is the emf itself, which would be the terminal voltage only if no current flowed or the internal resistance were zero.

**10. B** — *Solve the node potential first, then read every branch current off it.* Junction rule at a with all currents taken as leaving: (V − 18)/2 + (V − 9)/3 + V/6 = 0. Multiply by 6: 3V − 54 + 2V − 18 + V = 0 → 6V = 72 → **V = 12.0 V**, so the 6 Ω carries 12/6 = **2.0 A**. A (3.0 A) is the current in the **18 V branch**, (18 − 12)/2 — one contribution reported as the answer. C (4.0 A) adds the two branch currents 3.0 and 1.0 as though both fed the load, which ignores that the 9 V branch is a *sink* here, not a source. D (1.0 A) is the current in the **9 V branch**, again the wrong branch.

**11. C** — *The node sits at 12 V, above the 9 V emf, so current is driven backwards into that battery and it is being charged.* From question 10, V_a = 12.0 V, so the 9 V branch carries (12 − 9)/3 = **1.0 A flowing from node a into the battery**, entering its + terminal: it is **being charged**. Its internal resistance is negligible, so its terminal potential difference is just its emf, **9 V**; the 3 V difference between it and node a is the drop across the 3 Ω of its own branch, and that is precisely what drives the charging current (had r not been negligible, the terminal voltage would be ε + Ir). The junction check is 3.0 A in = 1.0 A + 2.0 A out ✓. A assumes every battery must deliver — the magnitude is right, the direction and the physics are not. B invents a delivery of 3.0 A from the smaller source and gives the 18 V branch the 1.0 A, exactly swapping the two branches. D confuses "smaller emf" with "no current": the current is set by the *difference* between the node potential and the emf divided by the branch resistance, and it would be zero only if the node happened to sit at exactly 9 V.

**12. C** — *A negative solution means the same current flowing the other way; the algebra corrects your guess.* Every term was signed relative to the assumed direction, so the solved value is the current **measured in that direction**; −1.8 A therefore reads as 1.8 A in the reverse direction. That is precisely why the lesson says to assume a direction and mark it — any guess will do. A is the standard panic; the assumed direction is a bookkeeping choice, not a physical claim, so a negative result is information, not an error. B confuses the sign of a **current** with the direction of **energy flow**; whether an element absorbs or delivers is decided by which terminal the (correctly directed) current enters, and is a separate question answered after the currents are known. D misunderstands what reversing a traversal does: it multiplies **every** term of that loop equation by −1, giving the identical equation and the identical solution — the sense of traversal can never change a physical answer.

**13.** *The two rules, their conservation laws, the complete sign conventions, and the irrelevance of the traversal sense.*
**Junction rule.** At any junction — a point where **three or more** conductors meet — the **algebraic sum of the currents is zero**, ΣI = 0, with currents **into** the junction counted positive and currents **out of** it negative; equivalently, ΣI_in = ΣI_out. It expresses **conservation of charge**: charge is neither created nor destroyed, and in a **steady** current it cannot accumulate at a point, so whatever arrives per second must leave per second.
**Loop rule.** Round **any closed conducting path**, the **algebraic sum of the potential changes is zero**, ΣV = 0, equivalently Σε = ΣIR. It expresses **conservation of energy**: electric potential is single-valued — it is a property of a point, not of the route to it — so a unit charge carried round a closed loop returns to the potential it started from, having gained exactly as much energy from the emfs as it lost in the resistive drops.
**Sign conventions.** First **assume a current direction in every branch and mark it**; any guess will do. Then choose a starting point and a **sense of travel** and collect terms as you meet them:
- source traversed **from − to +**: **+ε**; source traversed **from + to −**: **−ε**;
- resistor traversed **in the same direction as the assumed current**: **−IR** (you are moving in the direction of decreasing potential); resistor traversed **opposite to the assumed current**: **+IR** (a rise of potential).
A battery's **internal resistance** is an ordinary resistor in that branch and takes the **IR** rule, so a real battery contributes **two** terms to the loop equation, signed by two different tests.
**Why the two tests differ.** The emf term asks *did I climb or fall through this source?*, and that is settled entirely by which terminal I entered — the battery holds its polarity whether it is discharging or being charged, so the assumed current has no bearing on it. The IR term asks *is the potential falling along my direction of travel?*, and since current flows from high to low potential through a resistor, that question needs **two** inputs: my direction of travel **and** the assumed current direction.
**Traversal sense.** Traversing the same loop the opposite way reverses every one of those tests simultaneously: each +ε becomes −ε and each −IR becomes +IR. The whole equation is therefore multiplied by **−1**, which is the same equation and yields the same solution. What matters, as the lesson stresses, is only **consistency**: once the current directions and the traversal sense are chosen, every term must be signed by that same convention throughout.

**14.** *Counting the independent equations, and why a bridge forces you to use them.*
**The counts.** For **b** unknown branch currents in a network with **n** junctions, write **n − 1** junction equations and **b − (n − 1) = b − n + 1** loop equations: **b** equations for **b** unknowns.
**Why n − 1 junction equations.** Write the junction equation at every one of the n junctions, currents in positive. Each branch current appears in exactly **two** of them — with a + at the junction it enters and a − at the junction it leaves — so adding all n equations cancels every term and gives the identity 0 = 0. The n equations therefore satisfy one exact linear relation and only **n − 1** carry information; in the simplest case, with two junctions, the equation at the second is just the first multiplied by −1.
**Why b − n + 1 loop equations.** Having used up n − 1 equations, exactly b − n + 1 more are needed, and that is precisely the number of independent loops the network has — for a planar circuit, its **meshes**, the "windows" of the diagram. Every other closed path that can be traced is an algebraic sum or difference of those, so its equation adds nothing.
**Why a bridge resists series/parallel.** In a bridge — two arms from the top node down to B and to C, two more from B and C down to the bottom node, and a fifth (galvanometer) branch straight across from B to C — no two resistors are in **series**, because the cross branch drains current away at B and at C, so the arms above and below those nodes do **not** carry the same current; and no two are in **parallel**, because arms that share one end node do not share the other. With the reduction blocked, Kirchhoff's rules are the only route: b = 6 branches, n = 4 junctions, giving 3 junction equations and 3 loop equations.
**Balance condition.** The galvanometer branch carries **no** current when the two sides divide the potential in the same ratio: **R_AB/R_BD = R_AC/R_CD**, equivalently **R_AB·R_CD = R_AC·R_BD**. It is a condition on **ratios**, not on individual resistances, and only when it holds may the bridge be collapsed by series/parallel (because the cross branch is then irrelevant).
**Spare loops.** A dependent loop equation — the outer loop, for example — is worthless as an equation (it is a combination of ones you already have, and it collapses to 0 = 0 on substitution) but it is an excellent **check**: substitute the solved currents into it, and if it does not come to zero, a sign has been dropped somewhere. A power audit, ΣεI = ΣI²R, is the other standard check.

**15.** *Concept: with a current deliberately assumed the "wrong" way, the junction and loop equations still work unchanged and hand back a negative value; both batteries here turn out to be discharging.*
**Count first:** b = 3 branches, n = 2 junctions → **1** junction equation and **3 − 2 + 1 = 2** loop equations.
**(a) Junction rule at a**, currents in positive and out negative: I₁ (in) − I₂ (out, as assumed) − I₃ (out) = 0, i.e. **I₁ = I₂ + I₃**.
*Loop 1*, travelling b → 10 V battery → 2 Ω → a → 4 Ω load → b: through the source **− to +** → **+10**; through the 2 Ω **along** I₁ → **−2I₁**; through the load **along** I₃ → **−4I₃**. So **10 − 2I₁ − 4I₃ = 0**.
*Loop 2*, travelling b → 8 V battery → 4 Ω → a → 4 Ω load → b: through the source **− to +** → **+8**; through the branch 4 Ω **against** the assumed I₂ (which was assumed to run from a towards the battery, i.e. b-wards, while I am travelling a-wards) → **+4I₂**; through the load **along** I₃ → **−4I₃**. So **8 + 4I₂ − 4I₃ = 0**.
**(b)** Substitute I₃ = I₁ − I₂. From loop 1: 10 − 2I₁ − 4(I₁ − I₂) = 0 → 10 = 6I₁ − 4I₂. From loop 2: 8 + 4I₂ − 4(I₁ − I₂) = 0 → 8 = 4I₁ − 8I₂. Halve the second: 4 = 2I₁ − 4I₂; subtract from the first: 6 = 4I₁ → **I₁ = 1.5 A**. Then 4 = 3.0 − 4I₂ → **I₂ = −0.25 A**, and **I₃ = 1.5 − (−0.25) = 1.75 A**.
**(c)** **I₂ = −0.25 A** means 0.25 A flowing **opposite to the assumption** — out of the 8 V battery and into node a. So both batteries are **discharging**, and the 8 V battery is *not* being charged despite its smaller emf. Node potential: **V_a = 4I₃ = 4 × 1.75 = 7.0 V**, which lies **below** both emfs — the reason both branches deliver current. (Check each branch off the node: (10 − 7)/2 = 1.5 A ✓ and (8 − 7)/4 = 0.25 A into a ✓.)
**(d) Outer-loop check** (b → 10 V → 2 Ω → a → 4 Ω → 8 V → b): 10 − 2I₁ + 4I₂ − 8 = 10 − 3.0 + 4(−0.25) − 8 = 10 − 3.0 − 1.0 − 8 = **0** ✓ — and note it is simply loop 1 minus loop 2, a check rather than a fourth equation.
**Power audit.** Emfs deliver 10 × 1.5 = 15.0 W and 8 × 0.25 = 2.0 W, total **17.0 W**. Dissipation: 2(1.5)² = 4.50 W, 4(0.25)² = 0.25 W, 4(1.75)² = 12.25 W, total **17.0 W** ✓. The books balance, which they would not if any single sign had been mis-assigned.

## Fallback topic 1 — MTH_102: The chain rule and the inner derivative: why a composite function's derivative ends in a multiplication by g'(x)

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** The chain rule is the rule students can recite and cannot apply, because the part that is easy to say aloud - 'differentiate the outside, keep the inside' - is precisely the half that does not need saying, while the half that carries the mathematics gets treated as an optional flourish. Three things conspire. First, on the simplest examples the omission is invisible in shape: 6(4x - 7)⁵ and 24(4x - 7)⁵ look like the same answer with a different number in front, so nothing on the page announces that a whole factor has been lost, and if the inner function happens to be x itself the missing factor is 1 and the sloppy method is accidentally right - which is exactly how the habit gets reinforced through a term of practice on xⁿ. Second, the reason for the factor is never internalised: the chain rule is a statement about rates composing, so if u moves g'(x) times as fast as x, then any rate measured per unit of u must be rescaled to become a rate per unit of x. Students who learn it as a two-step ritual instead of as a rescaling have no way to notice when the rescaling is absent, and no way to reconstruct the rule when it is half-remembered. Third, the error compounds silently inside the product and quotient rules, where the chain rule appears not as the main event but as a sub-computation for u' or v'. A student who differentiates x²(2x + 1)⁵ and reaches x(2x + 1)⁴(9x + 2) instead of 2x(2x + 1)⁴(7x + 1) has applied the product rule perfectly; the mark is lost several layers down, in a place the student is no longer looking. The diagnostic that fixes it is cheap and should be made a reflex: expand a small case and differentiate it directly. (3x² + 5)² expands to 9x⁴ + 30x² + 25, whose derivative 36x³ + 60x is a cubic, so the 'answer' 6x² + 10 is not merely numerically wrong but of the wrong degree - and no student who has once watched a degree fail to match forgets the inner derivative in quite the same way again.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why is d/dx of (4x - 7)⁶ equal to 24(4x - 7)⁵ and not 6(4x - 7)⁵ - where does the 4 come from?
2. Why does the chain rule MULTIPLY the two derivatives rather than adding them or composing them?
3. Why must the outer derivative be evaluated at g(x) rather than at x, and what would f'(x) even mean when f's argument is u?
4. Why does the sloppy method - outer derivative only - give the right answer for d/dx of (x + 3)⁵ but the wrong answer for d/dx of (2x + 3)⁵?
5. Why is a student who differentiates x²(2x + 1)⁵ and gets x(2x + 1)⁴(9x + 2) making a chain-rule error and not a product-rule error, and how would expanding a smaller case reveal it?

**What a good answer contains:**

- Line 1: the rule with its condition - if y = f(u) and u = g(x), with g differentiable at x and f differentiable at g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx) = f'(g(x)) times g'(x), the outer derivative evaluated at the INNER FUNCTION and then multiplied by the INNER DERIVATIVE. Line 2: the mechanism - rates through an intermediate variable compose multiplicatively, so a rate measured per unit of u must be rescaled by how fast u moves per unit of x; when g is linear that scale factor is simply g's slope, which is why the missing factor on (2x + 3)⁵ is 2 and on (x + 3)⁵ is the invisible 1. Line 3: the check and the named errors - expand a small case and match degrees, and be able to name the three standard failures: dropping g'(x) entirely, multiplying by the inner FUNCTION instead of its derivative (which changes the power), and composing as f'(g'(x)) instead of multiplying.

**Worked example.** Differentiate y = (3x² + 5)⁴ by the chain rule, then verify the method on a case small enough to expand. STEP 1 - name the pieces. The outer function is f(u) = u⁴ and the inner function is u = g(x) = 3x² + 5. STEP 2 - the two derivatives. dy/du = 4u³ and du/dx = 6x. STEP 3 - multiply and substitute back. dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx) = 4u³ times 6x = 4(3x² + 5)³ times 6x = 24x(3x² + 5)³. Note that the outer derivative is evaluated at 3x² + 5, never at x, and that the final factor 6x is the inner derivative and is not optional. STEP 4 - verify the method on a case you can expand. Take y = (3x² + 5)². The chain rule gives 2(3x² + 5) times 6x = 36x³ + 60x. Independently, (3x² + 5)² = 9x⁴ + 30x² + 25, which differentiates term by term to 36x³ + 60x - the same function, obtained with no chain rule anywhere. STEP 5 - watch the error fail. Forgetting the inner derivative would give 2(3x² + 5) = 6x² + 10. That is a quadratic, but the derivative of the quartic 9x⁴ + 30x² + 25 must be a cubic; and at x = 1 the truth is 96 while the error gives 16, short by exactly the dropped factor 6x = 6. STEP 6 - the same rule nested. For y = x²(2x + 1)⁵ the outermost structure is a PRODUCT, so u = x² with u' = 2x, and v = (2x + 1)⁵ whose derivative needs the chain rule: v' = 5(2x + 1)⁴ times 2 = 10(2x + 1)⁴. Then y' = 2x(2x + 1)⁵ + 10x²(2x + 1)⁴ = 2x(2x + 1)⁴[(2x + 1) + 5x] = 2x(2x + 1)⁴(7x + 1). Dropping the inner derivative 2 would have produced x(2x + 1)⁴(9x + 2) instead - a wrong answer several layers below a correctly applied product rule.

## Fallback topic 2 — COS_102: Selection versus repetition in a flowchart: both are drawn with the same diamond, and only where the arms go - forward and rejoining, or back up to a box already executed - tells them apart; and what that means for which boxes sit inside a branch

**Why it tends to be misunderstood.** Four things pile up on this one point. The first is that the course gives no loop symbol. Sequence has no shape of its own (it is just boxes in a line), selection has the diamond, and repetition has the diamond too - so a student who has learned 'diamond = decision = selection' has learned a rule that is right half the time and has no way of knowing which half he is in. The second is that flowcharts are read as pictures rather than followed as machines. A chart looks like it means what it was clearly intended to mean, so the eye supplies the loop where a loop obviously belongs and supplies the YES on the arm where YES obviously belongs; the course's actual mechanism is the opposite of impressionistic - the answer to the question determines the path the algorithm flow will take, so the LABEL on the arm and the DESTINATION of the arm are the whole content, and a chart with the two labels swapped is a different chart, not a badly drawn version of the intended one. The third is scope. In pseudocode, indentation shows what is inside a branch and what is inside a loop, and Week 4's trace questions turn on exactly that. A flowchart has no indentation: whether the counter is inside the IF or after it is expressed solely by whether the box sits above or below the point where the two arms rejoin, and that point is often the least visually prominent thing on the page. So the classic error - counting only the items that passed the test, or totalling only the expensive ones - is invisible in the picture and obvious in the trace. The fourth is that the two constructs are usually nested, and the nesting can go either way round: a selection inside a loop (test each of forty students, count them all) and a loop inside a selection (if the student failed, stop; otherwise keep going) are drawn from the same four boxes and behave completely differently. Because none of this produces an error message - a wrongly drawn chart is still a perfectly readable chart that computes something - the student gets a confident wrong answer with no warning, and the same instinct then survives into the code he writes from it.

**Five escalating "why" questions for the listener:**

1. Why can you not tell whether a diamond is a selection or a repetition just by looking at the diamond itself?
2. Why does swapping the YES and NO labels on a decision's two arms change what the chart computes, rather than being a cosmetic slip?
3. Why does it matter whether a process box sits above or below the point where the two arms of a selection rejoin?
4. Why does putting the counter on the YES arm of the test in a 'read 40 scores, print PASS or FAIL' chart make the program read an unpredictable number of scores?
5. Why is a loop nested inside a selection a different program from a selection nested inside a loop, when both are drawn with the same two diamonds and the same boxes?

**What a good answer contains:**

- Line 1: the shape does not decide the construct - follow the arms. Two arms that both go forward and rejoin is selection; an arm that is a flow line back up to a box already executed is repetition, and the loop consists of that diamond plus everything between it and the box the arm returns to. A single diamond can be both at once, as in a sentinel-controlled loop.
- Line 2: the label on an arm is the mechanism, not decoration - the answer to the question determines the path the flow will take - so a chart whose arms are labelled the wrong way round takes the exit on the pass where it was meant to loop, and must be traced as drawn rather than as intended.
- Line 3: a flowchart expresses scope only through the rejoin point, so a box above the rejoin runs only on that arm (conditional) and a box below it runs on every pass (unconditional); the test for where a box belongs is whether the problem says the operation happens ONLY when that answer is given.

**Worked example.** Take the chart: [ Start ] → ( Total = 0 ) → ( Count = 0 ) → / Enter n / → < Is n > 10? > with YES → ( Total = Total + n ) and NO going straight down, both arms rejoining, then ( Count = Count + 1 ) → < Is Count < 4? > with YES a flow line back up to / Enter n / and NO → / Print Total / → [ Stop ]. There are two diamonds and they are different constructs. The first is a selection: both arms go forward and meet again before the counter. The second is the control point of a repetition: its YES arm returns to / Enter n /, so the loop is that diamond plus the input box and everything between them - which is why the chart reads four numbers rather than one. Now trace it on 12, 4, 30, 9, 15. Pass 1: n = 12; 12 > 10 YES so Total = 12; the arms rejoin and Count = 1; 1 < 4 true, back up. Pass 2: n = 4; NO, so Total stays 12; Count = 2 anyway, because the counter is BELOW the rejoin point; true, back up. Pass 3: n = 30; YES, Total = 42; Count = 3; true, back up. Pass 4: n = 9; NO, Total stays 42; Count = 4; 4 < 4 is false, so the NO arm prints 42 and stops - the 15 is never entered. Answer: 42, four numbers read. Now move ONE box to see the scope point: put ( Count = Count + 1 ) on the YES arm, above the rejoin, and nothing else changes on the page. Trace again: 12 counts (Count = 1), 4 does not (Count still 1), 30 counts (Count = 2), 9 does not, 15 counts (Count = 3) - and the chart is still asking for numbers after the user has run out, because it now counts only the values above 10 and needs four of them. One box moved a few millimetres up the page turned 'read four numbers' into 'read until four of them exceed 10'. Finally swap the labels on the second diamond, YES to / Print Total / and NO back up: pass 1 gives Count = 1, 1 < 4 is true, the YES arm is taken, and the chart prints 12 after a single number. Nothing about the drawing looks wrong in any of the three versions; only the trace distinguishes them.

## Reserve topic (if both fallbacks are already used) — PHY_102: The loop-rule sign conventions: the emf sign is fixed by the direction of travel through the battery's terminals, while the IR sign is fixed by the direction of travel relative to the ASSUMED current - two different tests applied to two different terms of the same branch

This is the step where almost every Kirchhoff answer is lost, and it is lost silently. Four things pile up. First, the student is handed four rules that look like one family - +emf, -emf, +IR, -IR - and so assumes a single test decides all four, usually 'which way is the current going?'. It does not: an emf term asks only 'did I enter at the minus terminal or the plus terminal as I travelled?', and a battery holds its polarity whether it is delivering current or being charged, so the current is irrelevant to it; an IR term asks 'is the potential falling along my direction of travel?', which needs BOTH the travel direction and the assumed current direction, because current runs from high to low potential through a resistor. Second, a battery with internal resistance contributes TWO terms to the same loop equation, ±emf and -/+I*r, signed by those two different tests, and the instinct to merge them into a single '±(emf - I*r)' works only for a discharging battery traversed one particular way. Third, in the simplest single-loop problems the student always travels WITH the assumed current, so the lazy rule 'IR is always negative' gives the right answer every time and is never punished - until the first two-loop problem, where some branch must be traversed against its assumed current and the outer-loop check quietly fails. Fourth, the whole convention is a bookkeeping choice, not a physical claim, and students do not believe that: they treat a negative solved current as an error to be corrected rather than as the algebra telling them their guess was backwards, and they think reversing the direction of traversal will change the answer, when in fact it multiplies every term of the equation by -1 and leaves the solution identical. The result is a clean, confident, wrong number with no arithmetic warning at all - exactly the same failure mode as the zeroing rules in Week 4.

**Probe with:** Why do we have to assume and MARK a current direction in every branch before writing a single equation, when we do not yet know which way any of the currents actually flow? → Why is the emf term positive when you travel from - to + through a source, and why does that sign not care which way the current in that branch was assumed to flow? → Why is the IR term negative when you travel WITH the assumed current and positive when you travel against it, and why does that test need two pieces of information rather than one? → Why does a battery with internal resistance contribute two separately signed terms to the same loop equation instead of one combined term? → Why does traversing the same loop in the opposite sense, or discovering that a solved current is negative, change nothing physical about the answer?

**A good answer contains:** Line 1: the emf sign is a polarity-versus-travel test - travelling - to + through a source is a rise, +emf; travelling + to - is a fall, -emf - and it is the same whether the battery is discharging or being charged, so the assumed current never enters into it. Line 2: the IR sign is a travel-versus-current test - current flows from high to low potential through a resistor, so travelling WITH the assumed current is going downhill, -IR, and travelling against it is going uphill, +IR - which is why internal resistance takes the IR rule and a real battery contributes two separately signed terms, ±emf and -/+I*r. Line 3: the convention is bookkeeping, not physics - traversing the loop the other way multiplies every term by -1 and gives the identical solution, and a solved current that comes out negative simply means that current has the magnitude found but flows opposite to the direction assumed, so nothing needs rewriting.

**Worked example.** Two branches feed a common top node a; node b is the bottom rail. Branch 1: a 12 V battery (- terminal on the rail) in series with 4 Ω, assumed current I1 from b to a. Branch 2: a 6 V battery (- terminal on the rail) in series with 2 Ω, assumed current I2 from b to a. Branch 3: 3 Ω from a down to b, assumed current I3 from a to b. Count first: b = 3 branches, n = 2 junctions, so 1 junction equation and 3 - 2 + 1 = 2 loop equations. Junction rule at a, in positive and out negative: I1 + I2 - I3 = 0. Loop 1, travelling b → 12 V battery → 4 Ω → a → 3 Ω → b: through the source - to + gives +12; through the 4 Ω ALONG I1 gives -4*I1; through the 3 Ω ALONG I3 gives -3*I3; so 12 - 4*I1 - 3*I3 = 0. Loop 2, by the same tests: 6 - 2*I2 - 3*I3 = 0. Eliminating I3 = I1 + I2 gives 12 = 7*I1 + 3*I2 and 6 = 3*I1 + 5*I2, so I2 = 3/13 = 0.231 A, I1 = 21/13 = 1.615 A and I3 = 24/13 = 1.846 A - all positive, so all three guesses were right. Checks: node a sits at 3*I3 = 5.54 V, which is the Week 4 nodal answer for the same circuit; the outer loop b → 12 V → 4 Ω → a → 2 Ω → 6 V → b gives 12 - 4*I1 + 2*I2 - 6 = 12 - 6.462 + 0.462 - 6 = 0, and note the +2*I2 there, because that branch is now traversed AGAINST its assumed current - the term that the lazy rule 'IR is always negative' gets wrong. That outer loop is loop 1 minus loop 2, so it is a check, not a fourth equation. Power check: sources 12(1.615) + 6(0.231) = 20.77 W, dissipation 4*I1² + 2*I2² + 3*I3² = 20.77 W. Finally, had I2 been assumed downwards (a to b), loop 2 would read 6 + 2*I2 - 3*I3 = 0 and the answer would come out I2 = -3/13 A: same physics, sign flipped by your own labelling.

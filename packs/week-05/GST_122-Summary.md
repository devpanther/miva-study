## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **Pre-writing** is the phase before drafting that generates and organises ideas, sets purpose, and builds a preliminary framework; **post-writing** is everything after the first draft that revises, refines, and polishes it. Both are named stages either side of the actual writing.
2. **Brainstorming vs freewriting** — the course's sharpest near-neighbour pair. Brainstorming is divergent but still *topic-directed*: generating and organising a list or web of ideas around a known subject (e.g., listing the causes, effects, and solutions of climate change). Freewriting is *continuous, unstructured* writing with no stopping to plan or edit, used to discover unexpected material (e.g., a novelist writing out a character's backstory to see what surfaces).
3. **Mind mapping vs clustering** — both are visual pre-writing techniques, but mind mapping *branches* a central topic into organised sub-themes or opposing sides (e.g., pro/con branches for a social-media essay), while clustering *associates* free-floating words or images around a theme for creative exploration (e.g., a poet generating imagery), with less built-in structure.
4. **Outlining vs listing** — outlining sequences key points with supporting evidence into a *logical flow* (e.g., a speech outline); listing simply itemises points, most often two-sided similarities/differences (e.g., comparing two historical figures), without necessarily ordering them into an argument.
5. Ten pre-writing techniques are named: **brainstorming, freewriting, research, mind mapping, outlining, identifying audience and purpose, storyboarding, drafting questions, clustering, listing.**
6. Post-writing runs through ten stages **in this order**: **revision -> editing for grammar and style -> peer review -> proofreading -> seeking feedback from instructors/editors -> checking citations and references -> polishing transitions -> formatting and presentation -> finalising conclusions -> publishing or submitting.**
7. **Revision vs editing vs proofreading** — the other sharp triad. Revision works at the *content/structure* level (rewriting an introduction, reordering paragraphs for flow). Editing works at the *sentence/style* level (grammar, sentence structure, consistency of style). Proofreading is the *final surface pass* (typos, misspellings, punctuation) right before the piece goes out — it comes after editing, not before.
8. **Peer review vs seeking feedback from instructors/editors** — both are outside opinions sought during post-writing, but peer review draws on a fellow student/colleague (clarity of ideas, strength of argument), while instructor/editor feedback comes from an authority figure and typically addresses deeper craft (plot development, character arcs, overall storytelling) — a later, higher-stakes check.

## Pre-writing techniques (as introduced)

- **Brainstorming** — divergent-thinking techniques (mind mapping, freewriting, clustering) used to generate and organise ideas around a topic before writing; e.g., listing the causes, effects, and solutions related to climate change.
- **Freewriting** — continuous, unstructured writing without stopping to plan or self-edit, used to discover unexpected details or insights; e.g., a novelist exploring a character's backstory.
- **Research** — gathering information (types, benefits, challenges) on a topic before writing, e.g., preparing a paper on renewable energy.
- **Mind mapping** — visually branching a central topic into connected sub-themes or opposing sides, e.g., pro/con branches for a social-media essay.
- **Outlining** — building a sequence of key points, supporting evidence, and a logical flow before drafting, e.g., planning a speech.
- **Identifying audience and purpose** — deciding who the readers are and what the piece must achieve (inform vs persuade), which then shapes tone, style, and content, e.g., a business report for executives.
- **Storyboarding** — visually planning a scene-by-scene sequence to organise narrative structure, e.g., a filmmaker planning a movie.
- **Drafting questions** — writing out the questions a piece needs to answer before starting, e.g., a journalist preparing for an interview.
- **Clustering** — associating words or images around a central theme to generate ideas through free association, e.g., a poet exploring imagery.
- **Listing** — itemising points (often similarities/differences) to guide structure, e.g., comparing two historical figures in a compare-and-contrast essay.

## Post-writing stages (in order)

1. **Revision** — revisiting content and structure: does the introduction grab attention, do paragraphs need reordering for flow?
2. **Editing for grammar and style** — correcting grammatical errors, improving sentence structure, ensuring consistent style.
3. **Peer review** — exchanging drafts with a fellow student/colleague for feedback on clarity and argument strength.
4. **Proofreading** — a final check for typographical errors, misspellings, and punctuation mistakes.
5. **Seeking feedback from instructors or editors** — submitting to an authority figure for deeper craft feedback (plot, character, storytelling).
6. **Checking citations and references** — verifying accuracy and correct formatting of sources, crucial for academic integrity.
7. **Polishing transitions** — refining the links between paragraphs and ideas for smooth, logical flow.
8. **Formatting and presentation** — attending to font, margins, spacing, and overall visual professionalism.
9. **Finalising conclusions** — making sure the closing statement effectively summarises key points with impact.
10. **Publishing or submitting** — the final step: sending the manuscript to an agent/publisher, or submitting the essay to an instructor.

## Commonly confused

- **Brainstorming vs listing** — brainstorming generates a broad, unstructured pool of possible topics or ideas (climate-change causes/effects/solutions); listing organises already-identified points into a structured set, most often for comparison (similarities/differences between two figures).
- **Mind mapping vs clustering** — mind mapping branches outward from one central topic in an organised, often two-sided way; clustering free-associates words/images around a theme with a looser, more creative structure.
- **Outlining vs storyboarding** — outlining organises verbal key points and evidence into a logical sequence (speeches, essays); storyboarding organises a sequence *visually*, scene by scene (narrative/film structure).
- **Revision vs editing vs proofreading** — scope shrinks at each stage: revision = content and structure; editing = grammar, sentence structure, and style consistency; proofreading = surface-level typos, spelling, and punctuation, done last.
- **Peer review vs seeking feedback from instructors/editors** — both are external feedback, but the source (fellow student vs authority figure) and depth (clarity/argument vs plot/character/storytelling craft) differ, and instructor/editor feedback sits later in the sequence.

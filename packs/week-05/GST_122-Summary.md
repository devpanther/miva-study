# GST_122 — Week 5

*Communication in English II · Week 5 (5 – 11 Oct 2026)*

Topic: **Writing Activities — Pre-writing & Post-writing**

## Most likely to be examined

1. **The three stages of the writing process, in order: pre-writing → during-writing (writing/drafting) → post-writing.** The lecture stresses that impactful writing tasks need "cohesive sets of activities" across all three, with a seamless, interconnected flow.
2. **Definition of pre-writing** (learn near-verbatim): the phase of the writing process that *precedes the actual composition*; a series of strategic activities aimed at **generating and organizing ideas, establishing a purpose, and constructing a preliminary framework** for the impending written work.
3. **Definition of post-writing**: the activities undertaken *after the initial draft is completed* — a meticulous revision process ensuring **coherence, clarity, and adherence to academic conventions**; writers focus on **revising, editing and refining** to improve clarity, coherence and overall effectiveness, producing a polished final version.
4. **Naming the pre-writing technique from a described activity** — brainstorming, freewriting, research, mind mapping, outlining, identifying audience and purpose, storyboarding, drafting questions, clustering, listing. Each has a signature example in the deck (table below); exams reuse those examples.
5. **Naming the post-writing activity from a described activity** — revision, editing for grammar and style, peer review, proofreading, seeking feedback from instructors/editors, checking citations and references, polishing transitions, formatting and presentation, finalising conclusions, publishing or submitting.
6. **Revising vs editing vs proofreading** — the classic discrimination. Revising = content/structure (reorganize paragraphs, rework the introduction, flow of the argument); editing = language level (grammar, sentence structure, style consistency); proofreading = surface errors only (typographical errors, misspellings, punctuation), done last.
7. **Divergent thinking** as the named cognitive mode of pre-writing brainstorming — realised through **mind mapping, free-writing, and concept clustering** (the trio named in the learning objectives; expect this as a list question).
8. **Publishing or submitting marks the end of the writing process** and the transition to sharing the work with its intended audience — the last named post-writing step.

## Pre-writing — the stage and its techniques

**Pre-writing** = everything before composition begins. Purpose: exploration, organization and planning, so the final piece gains clarity, coherence and quality. Products: ideas, a stated purpose, an audience profile, a preliminary framework/outline.

| Technique | What doing it looks like (deck example) | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | Before an essay on climate change, list related topics — causes, effects on the environment, potential solutions. Engages **divergent thinking**. | A rich pool of possibilities; unfiltered ideas |
| **Free-writing** | A novelist writes continuously and **unstructured** to explore a character's backstory or motivations. | Unexpected details and insights discovered while writing |
| **Research** | Preparing a paper on renewable energy, gather information on different energy sources, their benefits and challenges. | Facts, data and evidence to write from |
| **Mind mapping** | For an argumentative essay on social media, draw a map with branches for positive and negative aspects. | A visual of connections between ideas |
| **Outlining** | Before a speech, set out key points, supporting evidence and a logical flow of ideas. | A **roadmap** for the piece; structure and clear direction |
| **Identifying audience and purpose** | A business professional asks who will read the report (executives, stakeholders) and why (inform, persuade). | Decisions on tone, style and content |
| **Storyboarding** | A filmmaker plans the sequence of scenes visually. | An organised narrative structure |
| **Drafting questions** | A journalist writes the interview questions before the interview. | Coverage of relevant topics; essential information secured |
| **Clustering (concept clustering)** | A poet associates words or images around a central theme. | Creative associations radiating from one core idea |
| **Listing** | For a compare-and-contrast essay on two historical figures, list similarities and differences. | An item-by-item basis for the essay's structure |

## During-writing (the drafting phase, from the case study)

Implement the pre-writing outcomes to compose the piece; give **each paragraph a clear topic sentence** focused on one specific aspect; use supporting details and evidence for persuasiveness. Deck analogy: **paragraphs are like slices of cake** — each with a distinct flavour and one central theme.

## Post-writing — the stage and its activities

**Post-writing** = after the initial draft. Purpose: refine and perfect; a critical examination of the draft addressing **content, style, grammar and presentation**.

| Activity | What doing it looks like | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| **Revision** | Revisit the introduction so it captures the reader's attention and previews the essay accurately; revise and **reorganize paragraphs**. | Overall flow and coherence of the argument (content/structure level) |
| **Editing for grammar and style** | A journalist eliminates grammatical errors, improves sentence structure, ensures consistency of writing style. | Professionalism and readability (sentence/language level) |
| **Peer review** | Exchange drafts with a peer for feedback. | Insight into clarity of ideas, strength of arguments, areas to improve |
| **Proofreading** | A novelist catches typographical errors, misspellings and punctuation mistakes. | A polished, error-free final product (surface level) |
| **Seeking feedback from instructors or editors** | Submit a manuscript to an editor for constructive criticism. | Comment on plot development, character arcs, overall storytelling |
| **Checking citations and references** | Review citations and references for accuracy and proper formatting. | Academic integrity |
| **Polishing transitions** | Attend closely to the links between paragraphs and ideas. | Smooth, logical flow; coherence of the argument |
| **Formatting and presentation** | Set font size, margins, spacing. | Professionalism and visual appeal of the final document |
| **Finalising conclusions** | Revisit the conclusion so it summarizes key points with a clear, impactful closing statement. | An effective ending |
| **Publishing or submitting** | Send the manuscript to an agent/publisher; submit the final essay to the instructor. | **Marks the end of the writing process** and the transition to sharing with the intended audience |

**Analogies to quote:** pre-writing is like an **architect planning a structure** (gathering thoughts, brainstorming, outlining); post-writing is like **polishing a gem** for clarity and shine; the whole journey is likened to a **recipe**.

**Skills the lesson claims to build:** Research Proficiency, Creative Thinking, Structural Organization, Editing Expertise, Proofreading Mastery, Professional Communication.

## Commonly confused

- **Revising vs editing vs proofreading.** Ask *what level is being changed?* Revising changes **ideas, content and paragraph order** (add, cut, reorganize, rewrite the introduction). Editing changes **sentences and words** (grammar, sentence structure, style consistency, redundancies, terminology). Proofreading changes **nothing but surface slips** — typos, misspellings, punctuation. Order: revise → edit → proofread; someone "proofreading" who reorganizes paragraphs is actually revising.
- **Brainstorming vs freewriting.** Brainstorming yields a **list of ideas/topics**; freewriting is **continuous unstructured prose** written to discover what you think. Test: is the output a list, or a flow of sentences?
- **Mind mapping vs clustering vs listing.** All generate ideas. Mind mapping = a **branching diagram** showing connections between ideas; clustering = **associating words/images around a central theme** for creative exploration; listing = a **linear enumeration** (e.g. similarities and differences). Test: branches, associations round a core, or a plain list?
- **Outlining vs mind mapping.** Outlining fixes **sequence and hierarchy** — a roadmap for the actual writing; mind mapping explores **relationships** without committing to order.
- **Pre-writing research vs post-writing checking citations.** Research **gathers** sources before writing; checking citations **verifies the accuracy and formatting** of sources already used.
- **Peer review vs seeking feedback from an instructor/editor.** Both are external feedback; peer review is an **exchange of drafts with a fellow writer**, the other is submission to an **authority or professional** (instructor, editor) for constructive criticism.
- **Polishing transitions vs revision.** Transitions target the **joins between paragraphs**; revision may **move or rewrite the paragraphs themselves**.
- **Formatting and presentation vs proofreading.** Formatting is **layout** (font size, margins, spacing); proofreading is **textual error** (typos, punctuation, spelling).
- **Identifying audience and purpose vs drafting questions.** The first decides **tone, style and content** for a known readership; the second prepares **specific questions** to gather information.
- **Publishing/submitting is not a fourth stage** — it is the final **post-writing** step that ends the process.

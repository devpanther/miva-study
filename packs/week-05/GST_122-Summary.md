# GST_122 — Week 5 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 5 (5–11 Oct 2026) · Topic: Writing Activities — Pre-writing and Post-writing*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. The writing process has **three stages in order: pre-writing → writing (drafting) → post-writing**. The lesson's whole claim is that these must form "a seamless and interconnected flow", not three unrelated jobs.
2. **Pre-writing** = the phase *before* actual composition: strategic activities to **generate and organise ideas, establish a purpose, and construct a preliminary framework** for the work to come. Learn that definition close to verbatim.
3. **Post-writing** = the phase *after the initial draft is completed*: **revising, editing and refining** to improve clarity, coherence and overall effectiveness, producing a polished final version.
4. Pre-writing is **divergent thinking** — the named divergent techniques are **mind mapping, free-writing and concept clustering**.
5. The three-word skeleton of pre-writing in the summary slide: **researching, brainstorming, outlining**. The three-word skeleton of post-writing: **editing, proofreading** (with revision named first in the slides).
6. The **outline is the roadmap**: it organises thoughts, structures the piece and gives clear direction. Pre-writing ends at an outline; drafting begins after it.
7. The two analogies the lesson uses: pre-writing is like an **architect planning a structure**; post-writing is like **polishing a gem**. (Paragraphs are "slices of cake", each with one flavour and a clear topic sentence — carried over from Week 4.)
8. Named skills for the lesson: **research proficiency, creative thinking, structural organisation, editing expertise, proofreading mastery, professional communication**.

## Pre-writing techniques — what each is, what it produces, when to use it

- **Brainstorming** — rapid, uncensored generation of every idea related to the topic, judgement suspended. *Produces:* a raw pool of possibilities. *Use when:* you have a topic but no angle. *Source example:* before an essay on climate change, listing causes, effects on the environment, and potential solutions.
- **Freewriting** — writing continuously for a set time without stopping, correcting or planning; unstructured prose, not notes. *Produces:* unexpected details and insights you did not know you had. *Use when:* you are blocked, or exploring something you cannot yet state — the source's case is a novelist exploring a character's backstory and motivations.
- **Clustering / concept clustering** — write the central theme in the middle, then associate words and images around it in loose groups. *Produces:* clusters of associated ideas. *Use when:* the material is associative or creative rather than linear — the source's case is a poet generating ideas for a poem.
- **Mind mapping** — a visual map: central topic at the centre, branches for sub-themes, twigs for details, lines showing connections. *Produces:* a picture of how ideas relate. *Use when:* you must see relationships or a two-sided structure — the source's case is an argumentative essay on social media with branches for positive and negative aspects.
- **Listing** — plain enumeration under headings. *Produces:* an ordered set of items ready to become sections. *Use when:* the structure is already parallel — the source's case is a compare-and-contrast essay on two historical figures, listing similarities and differences.
- **Drafting questions (the 5Ws and H)** — write out the questions the finished piece must answer: *who, what, where, when, why, how*. *Produces:* a checklist that doubles as a coverage test. *Use when:* you must not miss anything — the source's case is a journalist drafting interview questions before a magazine article.
- **Research** — gathering data, sources and evidence before drafting. *Produces:* the factual base and citations. *Use when:* the topic requires evidence — the source's case is gathering types of renewable energy, their benefits and challenges.
- **Identifying audience and purpose** — deciding *who reads this* and *what it must do* (inform, persuade, instruct). *Produces:* the tone, style and content decisions. *Use when:* always, and first — the source's case is a business report written for executives and stakeholders.
- **Storyboarding** — sequencing the piece visually, panel by panel. *Produces:* narrative order. *Use when:* the work is sequential or visual — the source's case is a filmmaker planning scenes.
- **Outlining** — converting the chosen ideas into a hierarchy of key points with supporting evidence in logical order. *Produces:* the roadmap you actually draft from. *Use when:* last in pre-writing, after generating and before drafting — the source's case is a speaker outlining key points, evidence and flow before writing a speech.

## Post-writing stages — what each looks at, in order

1. **Revision** (global, first) — looks at **content, structure and argument**: does the introduction capture attention and preview the essay accurately, do paragraphs sit in the right order, is the argument coherent? You may add, cut, reorder or rewrite whole paragraphs. Related activities: **polishing transitions** between paragraphs, **finalising the conclusion** so it summarises key points and closes impactfully.
2. **Editing** (sentence-level, second) — looks at **grammar, sentence structure, word choice, style and consistency**, eliminating redundancies and refining terminology. The source's case is a journalist editing a news article for professionalism and readability. Nothing structural changes here.
3. **Proofreading** (surface, last) — looks only at **typographical errors, misspellings and punctuation**, plus **formatting** (font size, margins, spacing) and **citations and references**. The source's case is a novelist catching typos that would distract readers.
4. **Peer review / seeking feedback** — exchanging the draft with a peer, instructor or editor for comment on clarity of ideas, strength of argument, plot and character, and areas for improvement. Feeds back into revision.
5. **Publishing or submitting** — sending the finished piece to the agent, publisher or instructor. This step **marks the end of the writing process** and the transition to sharing the work with its audience.

The order matters and is examinable: revise before you edit, edit before you proofread. Correcting commas in a paragraph you are about to delete is wasted work.

## Worked illustration: one topic, one technique, one outline

**Topic:** "Social media does more harm than good to Nigerian undergraduates."
**Technique — mind mapping.** Centre: *social media and undergraduates*. Branch 1 (positive): access to learning resources, networking, cheap communication, entrepreneurship. Branch 2 (negative): distraction from study, sleep loss, misinformation, comparison and anxiety. A line drawn between "networking" and "distraction" shows they are the same behaviour judged two ways — the kind of connection only a map reveals.
**Outline produced:**
I. Introduction — thesis: the harms outweigh the benefits for undergraduates specifically.
II. Benefits acknowledged — learning resources; networking and entrepreneurship.
III. Harm 1 — distraction and lost study time (evidence).
IV. Harm 2 — mental health: comparison, anxiety, sleep loss (evidence).
V. Harm 3 — misinformation and poor source judgement (evidence).
VI. Counter-argument and rebuttal — "it is a tool, not a cause".
VII. Conclusion — restate, recommend managed use.
Only now does drafting begin.

## Commonly confused

- **Revising vs editing vs proofreading** — revising is **global** (ideas, structure, order, argument, transitions); editing is **sentence-level** (grammar, style, word choice, redundancy); proofreading is **surface** (typos, spelling, punctuation, formatting). Big to small, in that order. Proofreading is not "checking the essay"; it is the last, narrowest sweep.
- **Brainstorming vs freewriting** — both generate, but brainstorming produces **fragments, lists and branches** and can be done in a group; freewriting produces **continuous unedited prose** written alone against a clock. Brainstorming asks "what ideas exist?"; freewriting asks "what happens if I keep writing?"
- **Clustering vs mind mapping** — clustering groups **associated words and images** around a theme with no fixed hierarchy (creative work); mind mapping builds a **branching hierarchy** that shows relationships (analytical and argumentative work). The lesson lists both as divergent thinking.
- **Outline vs draft** — an outline is the **skeleton**: headings, key points and supporting evidence in order, produced in pre-writing. A draft is **continuous prose in full sentences and paragraphs**, produced in the writing stage from that outline. An outline is never submitted; a draft is never final.
- **Pre-writing vs post-writing** — pre-writing is **before any prose exists** (generating, planning, purpose, audience, outline); post-writing is **after a complete first draft exists** (revising, editing, proofreading, feedback, submission). The middle stage, drafting, belongs to neither.
- **Editing vs peer review** — editing is what **you** do to the sentences; peer review is what **someone else** tells you about the ideas. Peer-review comments normally send you back to revision, not to proofreading.

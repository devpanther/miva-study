# Thursday — MTH_102 nightly check

*Differentiation problems worked end to end: the product rule, the quotient rule, a chain nested inside a product, a chain nested inside a quotient, rewriting before differentiating, and simplifying the answer to factored form.*
*Sit cold, notes closed, 8 minutes. Score out of 6.*

**1.** Differentiate y = (2x³ − 4x)(x² + 5).
a) 10x⁴ + 18x² − 20
b) 12x³ − 8x
c) 6x⁴ + 26x² − 20
d) 4x⁴ − 8x²

**2.** Differentiate y = (3x + 2)/(x² + 1).
a) (3x² + 4x − 3)/(x² + 1)²
b) (−3x² − 4x + 3)/(x² + 1)²
c) (−3x² − 4x + 3)/(x² + 1)
d) 3/(2x)

**3.** Differentiate y = x²(2x + 1)⁵, fully factored.
a) x(2x + 1)⁴(9x + 2)
b) 20x(2x + 1)⁴
c) 2x(2x + 1)⁴(7x + 1)
d) 2x(2x + 1)⁴(5x + 1)

**4.** Differentiate y = (2x + 3)⁴/(x − 1), fully factored.
a) (2x + 3)³(6x − 11)/(x − 1)²
b) (2x + 3)³(2x − 7)/(x − 1)²
c) (2x + 3)³(11 − 6x)/(x − 1)²
d) 8(2x + 3)³

**5. (explain why)** Differentiate each of the following, showing every step and simplifying fully. **(i)** y = (2x³ − 5)/x², **twice over** — once by rewriting it as a sum of powers, and once by the quotient rule — and confirm that the two answers agree. **(ii)** y = (3x − 1)√(x² + 4), stating which rule is outermost and which is nested inside it, and leaving the answer over a single denominator.

**6. (explain why)** Take u(x) = x³ and v(x) = x⁵. Differentiate the product uv in two independent ways — by expanding first, and by the product rule — show that they agree, then compute u′v′ and say exactly what it fails to be. Next differentiate y = (3x² + 5)² correctly by the chain rule, also expand the bracket and differentiate the polynomial directly to check, and finally write down the answer a student gets by forgetting the inner derivative, saying how the check exposes it.

## Answers

**1. a** — *The product rule contributes two terms, one per factor, and the derivative of a product is never the product of the derivatives.* Take u = 2x³ − 4x, u′ = 6x² − 4, v = x² + 5, v′ = 2x. Then y′ = u′v + uv′ = (6x² − 4)(x² + 5) + (2x³ − 4x)(2x) = (6x⁴ + 26x² − 20) + (4x⁴ − 8x²) = **10x⁴ + 18x² − 20**. The independent check: expanding first gives y = 2x⁵ + 6x³ − 20x, whose derivative is 10x⁴ + 18x² − 20 — the same function. (b) is u′v′ = (6x² − 4)(2x) = 12x³ − 8x, the product-of-derivatives error; note it is degree 3 where the answer must be degree 4, since y itself is degree 5. (c) is the first term alone, u′v — the slip of differentiating the first factor and copying the second unchanged. (d) is the second term alone, uv′ — the mirror-image slip. Because (c) and (d) sum exactly to (a), a student who reaches either has done half the correct work and stopped, which is why neither can be eliminated by inspection.

**2. b** — *The quotient rule is (u′v − uv′)/v²: the numerator's derivative comes first, the subtraction is fixed, and the denominator is squared.* With u = 3x + 2, u′ = 3, v = x² + 1, v′ = 2x: y′ = [3(x² + 1) − (3x + 2)(2x)]/(x² + 1)² = [3x² + 3 − 6x² − 4x]/(x² + 1)² = **(−3x² − 4x + 3)/(x² + 1)²**. A sanity check at x = 0 gives y′(0) = 3, matching the slope of (3x + 2)/(x² + 1) near the origin, where the denominator is momentarily flat and y behaves like 3x + 2. (a) has the two numerator products in the reversed order, (uv′ − u′v), and is the **exact negative** of the truth — it would report y decreasing at 0 where it is in fact rising steeply. (c) has the numerator right and the denominator unsquared; it agrees with the truth only where x² + 1 = 1, i.e. at x = 0 alone, and misbehaves badly for large x. (d) is the quotient of the derivatives, u′/v′ = 3/(2x), the direct analogue of the product-of-derivatives error, and it is not even defined at x = 0 although y plainly has a slope there.

**3. c** — *The outermost structure is a product; the second factor then needs the chain rule, and the inner derivative 2 must survive into the final answer.* u = x², u′ = 2x. v = (2x + 1)⁵, so by the chain rule v′ = 5(2x + 1)⁴ · **2** = 10(2x + 1)⁴. Then y′ = 2x(2x + 1)⁵ + 10x²(2x + 1)⁴. Factor out the lowest power of the repeated bracket together with the common 2x: y′ = 2x(2x + 1)⁴[(2x + 1) + 5x] = **2x(2x + 1)⁴(7x + 1)**. Check at x = 1: y = 1·3⁵ = 243, and y′ = 2·3⁴·8 = 1296; differentiating the expanded polynomial 32x⁷ + 80x⁶ + 80x⁵ + 40x⁴ + 10x³ + x² at x = 1 gives 224 + 480 + 400 + 160 + 30 + 2 = 1296 ✓. (a) is what you get from v′ = 5(2x + 1)⁴ with the inner derivative dropped: 2x(2x+1)⁵ + 5x²(2x+1)⁴ = x(2x + 1)⁴(9x + 2). It is the closest miss on the page and the most commonly chosen wrong answer. (b) is u′v′ = 2x·10(2x + 1)⁴, the product-of-derivatives error, carrying the chain rule correctly but discarding the product structure. (d) is the correct pair of terms spoiled at the last step: factoring 2x(2x + 1)⁴ out of 2x(2x+1)⁵ + 10x²(2x+1)⁴ leaves [(2x + 1) + 5x] = 7x + 1, and reading that bracket as 5x + 1 means losing the 2x that came out of the first term — a simplification slip, not a rule slip, and it is why the answer must be checked numerically rather than admired.

**4. a** — *The outermost structure is a quotient; the numerator then needs the chain rule, so u′ carries the factor 2 from the inner function 2x + 3.* u = (2x + 3)⁴ ⟹ u′ = 4(2x + 3)³ · **2** = 8(2x + 3)³; v = x − 1, v′ = 1. Then y′ = [8(2x + 3)³(x − 1) − (2x + 3)⁴]/(x − 1)² = (2x + 3)³[8(x − 1) − (2x + 3)]/(x − 1)² = (2x + 3)³[8x − 8 − 2x − 3]/(x − 1)² = **(2x + 3)³(6x − 11)/(x − 1)²**. Check at x = 0: y = 81/(−1) = −81, and the formula gives 27·(−11)/1 = −297; differentiating numerically, y(0.01) ≈ (3.02)⁴/(−0.99) ≈ −84.0, so the slope near 0 is around −300 ✓. (b) uses u′ = 4(2x + 3)³ with the inner derivative forgotten, and the same factoring then yields (2x + 3)³(2x − 7)/(x − 1)² — structurally identical to the right answer and distinguishable only by having done the chain rule properly. (c) is the numerator's two products taken in the reversed order, giving (2x + 3)³(11 − 6x)/(x − 1)², the **exact negative** of the truth. (d) is u′/v′ = 8(2x + 3)³/1, the quotient-of-derivatives error; it is the only option that is not a rational function with (x − 1)² underneath, but it is also the option that looks cleanest, which is exactly why it attracts.

**5.** *Concept: an expression can often be differentiated by more than one legitimate route, and agreement between two routes is the cheapest available check; where nesting is unavoidable, name the outermost rule first.* **(i) y = (2x³ − 5)/x².** *Route 1 — rewrite as powers.* Divide term by term: y = 2x³/x² − 5/x² = **2x − 5x⁻²**. Now the sum and power rules: y′ = 2 − 5(−2)x⁻³ = 2 + 10x⁻³ = **2 + 10/x³**. Note the two negatives combining to a plus — the standard sign trap on a negative exponent. *Route 2 — quotient rule.* u = 2x³ − 5, u′ = 6x²; v = x², v′ = 2x. y′ = [6x²·x² − (2x³ − 5)(2x)]/(x²)² = [6x⁴ − (4x⁴ − 10x)]/x⁴ = (2x⁴ + 10x)/x⁴ = 2x⁴/x⁴ + 10x/x⁴ = **2 + 10/x³**. The two routes agree, as they must. The moral is practical: where the denominator is a single power, rewriting is faster and far less error-prone than the quotient rule, and the quotient rule should be reserved for denominators that are genuine sums. **(ii) y = (3x − 1)√(x² + 4).** The outermost structure is a **product** of 3x − 1 with (x² + 4)^(1/2); the **chain rule** is nested inside, in differentiating the square root. u = 3x − 1, u′ = 3. v = (x² + 4)^(1/2) ⟹ v′ = (1/2)(x² + 4)^(−1/2)·**2x** = x/√(x² + 4) — the inner derivative 2x cancels the ½, which is why this shape appears so often. Then y′ = 3√(x² + 4) + (3x − 1)·x/√(x² + 4). Put over the single denominator √(x² + 4), writing the first term as 3(x² + 4)/√(x² + 4): y′ = [3(x² + 4) + x(3x − 1)]/√(x² + 4) = [3x² + 12 + 3x² − x]/√(x² + 4) = **(6x² − x + 12)/√(x² + 4)**. Check at x = 0: y′(0) = 12/2 = 6, and directly, u′v + uv′ = 3·2 + (−1)·0 = 6 ✓.

**6.** *Concept: the product rule and the chain rule can both be verified against an independent elementary computation, and doing so once is what makes the two headline errors impossible to believe again.* **The product.** u = x³, v = x⁵, so uv = x⁸. *By expanding first:* d/dx(x⁸) = **8x⁷**, by the power rule alone, with no product rule involved and therefore no possible dependence on it. *By the product rule:* u′ = 3x², v′ = 5x⁴, so (uv)′ = u′v + uv′ = 3x²·x⁵ + x³·5x⁴ = 3x⁷ + 5x⁷ = **8x⁷** ✓. The two terms are the two contributions, and it is worth noticing that they combine here as 3 + 5 = 8 — precisely the exponents adding, which is what the product rule reduces to when both factors are powers. *The error:* u′v′ = 3x²·5x⁴ = **15x⁶**. This fails to be the derivative in two independent ways. Its **degree** is wrong: uv is degree 8, so its derivative must be degree 7, and 15x⁶ could not be the derivative of a degree-8 polynomial at all. Its **coefficient** is wrong: 15 = 3·5, a product, where the correct answer needs 3 + 5, a sum — the two contributions are to be *added*, since over a small step both factors change and each change contributes separately. At x = 1 the truth is 8 and the error gives 15, so no numerical accident hides it. **The composite.** y = (3x² + 5)². *By the chain rule:* outer u², inner u = 3x² + 5, so y′ = 2(3x² + 5)·**6x** = 12x(3x² + 5) = **36x³ + 60x**. *Independent check by expanding:* (3x² + 5)² = 9x⁴ + 30x² + 25, and differentiating the polynomial term by term gives 36x³ + 60x ✓ — computed with no chain rule anywhere, so it is a genuine verification, not a restatement. *The error:* forgetting the inner derivative gives 2(3x² + 5) = **6x² + 10**. The check destroys it instantly: 6x² + 10 is a quadratic, but the derivative of the quartic 9x⁴ + 30x² + 25 must be a cubic, and at x = 1 the truth is 96 while the error gives 16 — out by exactly the factor 6x = 6 that was dropped. This is the general shape of the mistake: omitting g′(x) scales the answer by the wrong factor everywhere, and when g is linear that factor is just g's slope, which is the quickest way to spot the omission on sight.

# Tuesday — PHY_102 nightly check

*Core theory: Kirchhoff's rules — the definitions of junction and loop; the junction (current) rule as conservation of charge and the loop (voltage) rule as conservation of energy; the sign conventions for emfs and for IR terms when travelling round a loop; and how to set up an independent system of equations (how many junction equations, how many loop equations, and what a negative current means).*
*Sit cold, notes closed, 8 minutes. Score out of 6.*

**1.** You are travelling **clockwise** round a loop. In one branch of that loop you pass, in order, a 6 Ω resistor whose **assumed current runs anticlockwise** (that is, opposite to your direction of travel), and then a 9 V battery which you enter at its **+** terminal and leave at its **−** terminal. Writing I for the assumed current in that branch, the contribution of these two elements to the loop equation is:
a) +6I − 9
b) −6I − 9
c) +6I + 9
d) −6I + 9

**2.** Which statement about the junction rule is correct?
a) A junction is any point where two or more conductors meet; the rule expresses conservation of energy, since a charge arriving at the point must carry away the energy it brought
b) A junction is a point where three or more conductors meet; the rule expresses conservation of charge — the algebraic sum of the currents at the junction is zero, because in a steady current charge cannot accumulate at a point
c) A junction is a point where three or more conductors meet; the rule expresses conservation of energy, and it is the reason the sum of the emfs round a closed loop equals the sum of the IR drops
d) A junction is a point where three or more conductors meet; the rule states that the currents leaving the junction must add up to the current supplied by the battery, which is what fixes the scale of all the branch currents

**3.** A network consists of **three branches connected in parallel between the same two junctions a and b**, each branch containing at least one resistor and two of them containing a battery as well. To find the three unknown branch currents, the correct **independent** set of Kirchhoff equations is:
a) two junction equations, one written at a and one written at b, together with one loop equation
b) one junction equation together with two loop equations
c) three loop equations, one for each of the three closed conducting paths that can be traced through the network
d) two junction equations and two loop equations, since each of the two junctions and each of the two adjacent loops contributes one equation

**4.** A student assumes the current in one branch flows from b to a, applies the sign rules consistently throughout, solves the system and obtains **I₂ = −0.40 A** for that branch. The correct interpretation is:
a) an arithmetic slip has been made, since a current cannot be negative; the assumed direction must be reversed and the whole system rewritten and re-solved
b) the current has magnitude 0.40 A and actually flows from a to b; nothing needs rewriting, because the sign convention was applied consistently to the assumed direction
c) the current is 0.40 A from b to a as assumed, and the minus sign records instead that the battery in that branch is absorbing rather than delivering energy
d) that loop was traversed in the wrong sense; traversing the same loop the opposite way will restore the answer to +0.40 A

**5. (explain why)** The loop rule is described as an expression of **conservation of energy**. Explain what physical quantity is actually being conserved as you travel round the loop, and why that forces ΣV = 0 for **any** closed path. Then explain precisely why the sign given to an **emf** term depends on your direction of travel but **not** on the assumed current direction, while the sign given to an **IR** term depends on both — and say what happens to a loop equation if you traverse the same loop in the opposite sense.

**6. (explain why)** A network has **b** branches and **n** junctions. Explain why only **n − 1** of the junction equations are independent, and why only **b − n + 1** loops give independent equations. Say what specifically goes wrong when a student writes one junction equation too many, and what goes wrong when a student writes one loop equation too few.

## Answers

**1. a** — *Two different tests: the IR sign compares your travel direction with the assumed current; the emf sign compares your travel direction with the battery's polarity.* You traverse the resistor **against** the assumed current, so you are climbing to a higher potential and the term is a **rise**: **+6I**. You traverse the battery from **+ to −**, which is a **fall** through the source, so the emf term is **−9**. Total **+6I − 9**. (b) applies the reflex "IR is always negative": that rule holds only when you travel *with* the assumed current, and a student who writes it every time gets the outer-loop check wrong every time. (c) has the resistor right but ties the emf's sign to the current instead of to the terminals you pass through — the emf sign is fixed by polarity and travel alone, and is the same whether the battery is charging or discharging. (d) flips both tests, which is what happens when the loop is traversed in one sense but the terms are signed as though it had been traversed in the other.

**2. b** — *Junction = three or more conductors; junction rule = conservation of charge.* The lesson defines a junction as a point where **three or more** conductors meet (which is why the lesson's points c, d, e and f, where only two wires meet, are *not* junctions), and the rule states that the algebraic sum of the currents there is zero — currents in counted positive, currents out negative — because in a steady current charge cannot pile up at a point: whatever arrives per second must leave per second. (a) drops the "three or more" to "two or more", which makes every bend in a wire a junction and yields the empty equation I = I, and it attaches the wrong conservation law. (c) has the definition right but swaps the two conservation principles: **energy** conservation is the **loop** rule, and the statement "sum of emfs = sum of IR drops" is a rearrangement of ΣV = 0, nothing to do with junctions. (d) replaces the local statement with a global one about "the battery"; the junction rule is a statement about *that junction only*, it says nothing about which source the current came from, and in a network with several sources there is no single "current supplied by the battery" to equate it to.

**3. b** — *With n junctions only n − 1 junction equations are independent, and the number of independent loops is b − n + 1.* Here b = 3 branches and n = 2 junctions, so **1** junction equation (I₁ + I₂ + I₃ = 0 with signs) and **3 − 2 + 1 = 2** loop equations — three equations for three unknown currents. (a) counts both junctions: the equation at b is exactly the equation at a multiplied by −1, so it adds no information and leaves the system with only two usable equations for three unknowns. (c) confuses *traceable closed paths* with *independent loops*: three paths can indeed be traced here, but the third is the sum (or difference) of the other two and collapses to 0 = 0 when you try to solve — useful as a check, useless as an equation. (d) is the "one of everything" answer, four equations for three unknowns, and it is dependent twice over — once in the junctions and once in the loops.

**4. b** — *The sign convention is applied to the direction you assumed, so the algebra corrects a wrong guess by handing back a minus sign.* Every term in the system was signed relative to the assumed direction, so the solution is the value of the current *measured in that direction*. A value of −0.40 A therefore says: magnitude 0.40 A, direction opposite to the assumption, i.e. from a to b. The lesson's own instruction — assume a direction and mark it, any guess will do — is exactly what makes this safe. (a) is the standard panic; reversing the assumption and re-solving simply flips the sign again to +0.40 A in the new direction, which is the same physical current, so the work is wasted rather than wrong. (c) confuses the sign of a **current** with the direction of **energy flow**; whether a battery charges or discharges is read off from which of its terminals the (correctly directed) current enters, and is a separate question from the sign of I₂. (d) misunderstands what reversing the traversal does: traversing a loop the other way multiplies **every** term in that equation by −1, so it is the same equation and yields the identical solution — the sense of traversal can never change a physical answer.

**5.** *Concept: the loop rule says that electric potential is single-valued, so the energy per unit charge gained from emfs round a closed path exactly equals the energy per unit charge lost in resistive drops; the emf sign is a polarity test, the IR sign is a current-direction test.* Take a unit positive charge round the closed path. Every element it passes changes its potential energy: a source drives it from low to high potential (energy supplied by chemistry) or from high to low (energy stored); a resistor always converts energy to heat when the charge moves through it in the direction of the current. When the charge returns to its **starting point** it is at the **same potential** it started from — potential is a property of the point, not of the route taken to it — so the **net** change must be zero: **ΣV = 0**, i.e. Σε = ΣIR. That argument used nothing about the particular loop, so it holds for **any** closed conducting path in the network. The two sign tests are different because they answer different physical questions. An **emf** term asks: *did I climb or fall through this source?* — and that is settled entirely by which terminal I entered, − to + (a rise, **+ε**) or + to − (a fall, **−ε**). The current has no say: a battery maintains its polarity whether it is delivering or being charged, so the emf sign is the same either way. An **IR** term asks: *is the potential dropping along my direction of travel?* — and current flows from high to low potential through a resistor, so if I travel **with** the assumed current I am going downhill (**−IR**), and if I travel **against** it I am going uphill (**+IR**). That test needs both the travel direction and the current direction, hence two inputs rather than one. Finally, traversing the same loop in the **opposite** sense reverses **every** test at once: each +ε becomes −ε and each −IR becomes +IR, so the whole equation is multiplied by **−1**. It is the same equation, with the same solution — which is why the lesson insists only on **consistency**, not on any particular choice.

**6.** *Concept: the junction equations are constrained by the fact that every branch current leaves one junction and enters another, so their sum is identically zero; and the independent loops are exactly the meshes left once a spanning tree has been chosen.* **Why n − 1 junction equations.** Write the junction equation at every one of the n junctions, taking currents in as positive. Each branch current appears in exactly **two** of those equations — once with a + sign (at the junction it enters) and once with a − sign (at the junction it leaves). Add all n equations and every term cancels in pairs, giving the identity **0 = 0**. So the n equations satisfy one exact linear relation: any one of them is the negative of the sum of the others, and only **n − 1** carry information. In the two-junction case this is easy to see directly — the equation at b is the equation at a with every sign reversed. **Why b − n + 1 loops.** After the n − 1 junction equations, b − (n − 1) = **b − n + 1** more independent equations are needed to pin down the b branch currents, and that is exactly the number of independent loops the network possesses (for a planar circuit, its meshes — the "windows" of the diagram). Every other closed path you can trace is an algebraic **sum or difference** of those, so its loop equation is a combination of equations you already have. **What goes wrong.** One junction equation too many: you have not gained anything, but you have not lost anything either — the extra equation is consistent and redundant, and the danger is only that you *believe* you now have enough equations and drop a loop equation to compensate, leaving the system **underdetermined** (fewer independent equations than unknowns, so the currents come out with a free parameter, or the elimination collapses to 0 = 0). One loop equation too few is the same disease directly: the system is underdetermined and no unique set of currents exists. The safe drill is to **count first** — b unknowns, n − 1 junction equations, b − n + 1 loop equations — and then use any spare loop, such as the outer loop, purely as a **check** on the solved values.

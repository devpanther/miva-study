# Wednesday — COS_102 nightly check

*Flowchart concepts only: which symbol means what, how many flowlines each may carry, the rules of flow and the course's principles and types, and how a flowchart relates to an algorithm and to pseudocode. No tracing, no fault-finding, no conversion — those are Saturday.*
*Sit cold, notes closed, about 15 minutes. Score out of 12.*

**1.** A symbol in a flowchart has exactly one flowline arriving at it and two flowlines leaving it, and each of the two departing lines carries a written label. Which symbol is it, and what are the labels for?
a) The merge symbol; the labels name the various paths that are being combined into a single path
b) The summing junction; the labels name the inputs from various paths that are being added together
c) The decision or control symbol; each label states the answer, yes or no, that selects that path
d) The preparation symbol; the labels separate the steps that prepare for work from those that do the work

**2.** In a correctly drawn flowchart, exactly one kind of symbol has no flowline arriving at it and none leaving it. Which one, and why?
a) The on-page connector, because the flow jumps over it and resumes at the twin circle with the same label
b) The comment or note symbol, because it only annotates a part of the chart and hangs off it on a dashed line
c) The predefined process symbol, because its internal steps are defined on another chart and so are not run here
d) The preparation symbol, because preparing for work is not itself one of the steps of the work being charted

**3.** A rectangle labelled "Compute payroll tax" is drawn with a vertical bar down each of its two sides. What does that drawing tell a reader that a plain rectangle would not?
a) That the step is a comment on the payroll calculation rather than a part of the calculation itself
b) That the step is setting variables up in readiness, before the payroll calculation proper begins
c) That the step is a complex or well-known operation whose internal steps are defined elsewhere, not drawn here
d) That the step produces a payroll document as its output rather than leaving a value inside the program

**4.** Why does the course provide connector symbols at all, when a flowline could join the same two points?
a) Because a flowline may only join two symbols that are drawn next to each other, so distant symbols need a connector
b) Because a long or awkward line would make flow lines cross, which the principles say to avoid; the on-page connector stands in for it on one page, the link connector across pages
c) Because the flow has to be halted at a connector so that the reader has time to turn to the next page before continuing
d) Because connectors mark the boundaries between the independent elements of a chart, and a flowline is not permitted to cross such a boundary

**5.** A student states a method as an algorithm, draws it as a flowchart, and writes it as pseudocode. Which statement is correct?
a) The flowchart and the pseudocode are two representations of the one algorithm, and a compiler accepts none of the three
b) The pseudocode is the algorithm, the flowchart is a picture of the pseudocode, and only the pseudocode can be compiled
c) The flowchart is the algorithm, since the course defines it as the sequence of operations in a computer program
d) The algorithm is obtained by tracing the flowchart, so a flowchart has to exist before any algorithm can be stated

**6.** Two separate flowlines both arrive at the top of the same process rectangle, and one flowline leaves it. A classmate says this breaks the "one entry, one exit" rule and the chart must be redrawn. Who is right?
a) The classmate is right: no symbol may ever have more than one flowline arriving at it, whatever the symbol is
b) The classmate is right, and the fix is to draw a second copy of the rectangle so that each arriving path has its own
c) The classmate is wrong: paths may rejoin, and the rule bites on exits, since two lines leaving a non-decision box leave it unsaid which is taken
d) The classmate is wrong: the only rule about flow lines is that they should generally not cross, and these two do not cross

**7.** A team must document how a purchase request moves through an organisation, so that new employees can be trained on it and problem areas identified. On the course's stated uses, which type of flowchart is being asked for?
a) A process flowchart, which can be applied to almost anything and describes how a process is completed in an organisation
b) A workflow chart, which describes the manner in which businesses function and is used for employee training and for identifying possible problematic areas
c) A swimlane flowchart, which is useful where multiple information flows side by side and allows multiple categories of activities
d) A data flowchart, which shows the flow of information and reduces the communication gap between users and developers

**8.** The course lists as a disadvantage that adjustments to a flowchart "might be complicated and might even require that a new flowchart is drawn". What is the mechanism behind that?
a) Because a flowchart is not a program, so every change has to be made twice, once in the chart and once in the code
b) Because the layout carries meaning: a symbol's position fixes where its flowlines run, so inserting a step can force lines to cross or run out of room, and the chart must be redrawn to stay neat and clear
c) Because the flowchart logic is hardly the most efficient, and can make the program unnecessarily longer than normal
d) Because a complex chart may span multiple pages, and a page can no longer be edited once its link connectors have been labelled

**9. (explain why)** The course defines a flowchart as "the graphical representation of an algorithm". Explain what work the word **representation** is doing in that definition. Then use it to explain how two students can draw two visibly different flowcharts of the same algorithm and both be correct, and state exactly what would have to be true of the two charts for that to hold.

**10. (explain why)** Name the six symbols you would need in order to draw any of sequence, selection and iteration, say in one line what each does, and give the number of flowlines entering and leaving each. Then explain why the decision is the only one of the six permitted more than one exit.

**11. (explain why)** Explain what a matched pair of connectors does to the flow of a chart. Then explain what a reader who mistakes the first connector for a STOP terminator will wrongly conclude, and name the principle that connectors exist to protect.

**12. (explain why)** Take two rules of flow: *every path must terminate*, and *a decision has exactly two outgoing branches, both labelled*. Explain how a chart can satisfy the second and still break the first, and how a chart can satisfy both and still be a bad flowchart by the course's principles. Name the principle each failure offends.

## Answers

**1. c — The decision or control symbol.** *Concept: fan-out is unique to the decision; the branch label carries the condition's answer.* The course defines the decision symbol as the one **used when questions such as yes or no and true or false need an answer**, and says **the answer determines the path the algorithm flow will take** — so the two exits are the two answers, and the label is what tells the reader which answer sends the flow that way. Count the arrows: one in, two out, labelled. (a) and (b) come from the same single misunderstanding, the reversal of **fan-in** and **fan-out**: both **merge** (*combines various paths into a single path*) and the **summing junction** (*shows the addition of inputs from various paths*) take **several lines in and give one out*. Neither can produce a branch, and neither carries branch labels because there is no choice to record. (d) is the shape-confusion answer — the hexagonal preparation symbol is the other non-rectangular box on the sheet — but preparation is a plain 1-in, 1-out step, and its job is to distinguish setup from work, not to split the flow.

**2. b — The comment or note symbol.** *Concept: what is on the flow path and what merely annotates it.* A comment is joined to the symbol it comments on by a **dashed** line, which is not a flowline: the flow never enters it and never leaves it, so nothing in the comment is executed. (a) is the widespread error that a connector is somehow "skipped": a connector is very much on the path — the flow **arrives** at one circle and **departs** from its twin, so between them the pair carries one line in and one line out, just drawn in two pieces. (c) confuses "defined elsewhere" with "not run here": a predefined process is entered and left exactly like any other step; only its internals are hidden. (d) mistakes the *description* of the preparation symbol for a claim about execution — preparation steps really are performed, they are just distinguished from the work proper.

**3. c — A predefined process (declaration) symbol.** *Concept: predefined process vs process, preparation, comment and document.* The course's wording is that it is **used to show a complex process or a well-known operation**. Drawing it says: this step happens, it may be large, and its steps live somewhere other than this chart. (a) is the comment symbol, which is attached on a dashed line and is not on the flow path at all. (b) is the preparation symbol, drawn as a hexagon, whose job is **showing the difference between steps preparing for work and those that actually work**. (d) is the document symbol, drawn with a wavy bottom edge, which **shows a document's input or output**. All four distractors are real symbols; only one of them is the one with bars down its sides.

**4. b.** *Concept: why the connector exists — the no-crossing principle, and the on-page/off-page split.* Two of the course's own statements meet here: the principle that **flow lines should generally not cross, if possible**, and the disadvantage that a complex chart **might require that the flowchart spans multiple pages**. The connector is the answer to both — the **on-page connector connects independent elements across a single page**, the **link connector connects independent elements across multiple pages**. (a) invents a restriction the course never states; flowlines routinely run the length of a chart, which is exactly the case where a connector is *preferable*, not *required*. (c) reads the connector as an instruction to the human reader rather than as part of the flow — the flow does not pause, it resumes at the twin. (d) invents the notion that "independent elements" are fenced regions that lines may not cross; "independent elements" in the course's phrasing simply means parts of the chart not drawn adjacent to each other.

**5. a.** *Concept: algorithm vs flowchart vs pseudocode — one method, two notations, no program.* The **algorithm** is the method itself; the **flowchart** is the course's **graphical representation** of it; **pseudocode** is the text representation of the same thing, using English-like statements and keywords where the chart uses shapes and arrows. Neither notation is a programming language, so no compiler accepts either — nor the algorithm, which is not written down in any fixed syntax at all. This is why the chart belongs in the **program specification documents**. (b) is the "pseudocode is nearly code" error — it looks compilable because it borrows keywords, but the keywords are not those of any one language and the statements are not defined by any grammar. (c) misreads the definition: the definition says a flowchart is a representation *of* the operations, so the flowchart cannot be the thing it represents. (d) reverses the dependency — the algorithm is what the chart is drawn *from*; you can certainly recover it by tracing a chart, but the method exists before any drawing of it does.

**6. c.** *Concept: what "one entry, one exit" actually constrains.* The rule exists to make the flow **unambiguous**, and ambiguity can only arise where the flow has a *choice* — that is, at exits. Two lines arriving at one box is not a choice: whichever path the flow was on, it now continues into that box, and the course supplies the **merge** symbol precisely to **combine various paths into a single path**. Two lines *leaving* a non-decision box would be a choice with nothing to decide it, which is why only the decision, whose branches are labelled with the answers, is allowed two exits. (a) is the over-literal reading of the rule, which would outlaw the merge symbol the course teaches and make every loop's back-arrow illegal, since it rejoins the flow at an earlier box. (b) accepts the same wrong reading and then "fixes" it by duplication, which the course's own advantage — that a chart shows whether the logic works — argues against, since the duplicate must now be kept in step with its twin by hand. (d) quotes a real principle at a question it does not answer; crossing is about legibility, entries and exits are about meaning.

**7. b — A workflow chart.** *Concept: matching a chart type to the course's stated uses, not to its topic.* The two things asked for — **employee training** and **identifying possible problematic areas** — are listed by the course under the **workflow** flowchart, together with simplifying the operations of a business and producing stable, high-quality output from standard procedures; and a workflow chart is defined as describing **the manner in which businesses function**. (a) is the strongest distractor because everything in the question also fits the very broad description of a process flowchart; the discriminator is that the course attaches *these particular uses* to workflow, and attaches to the process flowchart a different list — roles and responsibilities, the production chain, information communication. (c) would only be right if the question had asked to show the request moving **between departments in parallel lanes**; nothing here calls for multiple categories of activities side by side. (d) picks the type up by the word "information", but the data flowchart is about the **data processing** aspect of a system's design and analysis, not about how staff handle a request.

**8. b.** *Concept: why the graphical form makes a chart expensive to change.* In pseudocode you insert a line and everything below shifts down by itself. In a flowchart, position is meaning: every symbol occupies a place on the page and every flowline is a drawn route between two places. Insert a step and the routes must be re-drawn; do it a few times and lines that had been kept apart now cross, which offends the principle that **flow lines should generally not cross** and the requirement that the chart be **easy to follow, neat and clear such that the visual impact is very good** — so the honest remedy is a new drawing. (c) is the "quote the nearest listed disadvantage" trap: it is a genuine course disadvantage, but it is the one about the **efficiency of the resulting program**, a different complaint entirely. (d) welds together two separate disadvantages and adds an invented rule; nothing stops a page being edited, and the labels on link connectors are the very thing that survives redrawing. (a) is true of any design document and so explains nothing about flowcharts in particular — and the course's complaint is about changing the **chart**, not about keeping chart and code in step.

**9.** *Concept: a flowchart represents an algorithm, so identity of algorithm, not identity of drawing, is the test.* "Representation" says that the flowchart is a *notation for* something that exists independently of it. The algorithm — the finite, ordered, unambiguous sequence of steps that solves the problem — is the thing; the chart is one way of writing that thing down, and pseudocode is another. Because the algorithm is what is being represented, correctness of a chart is judged against **the algorithm**, not against another chart. So two students may legitimately produce charts that look nothing alike: one may run down the page and the other across it; one may use a matched pair of on-page connectors where the other draws a long flowline; one may put the loop's test at the foot of the body and the other draw the body inside a preparation-and-test arrangement; one may write "Is count < 5?" where the other writes "Is count >= 5?" and swaps the Yes and No labels. For both to be right, the charts must agree on everything the algorithm consists of and may differ only in how it is drawn: the **same steps in the same order**, the **same conditions** with each branch leading to the **same consequence** (so an inverted condition must have its labels inverted too), the **same number of repetitions** of any loop, the **same inputs read and outputs produced in the same order**, and **every path terminating**. Trace both charts on the same data and they must produce identical output; if they do not, they are not two representations of one algorithm but representations of two different algorithms, and at most one of them answers the question.

**10.** *Concept: the minimal symbol set for the three control structures, with arrow counts.* **Terminator** (oval) — shows the start and end points of the flowchart; START has **0 in, 1 out**, STOP has **1 or more in, 0 out**. **Process / action** (rectangle) — shows an action, process or function, such as an assignment; **1 in, 1 out**. **Input/output, the data symbol** (parallelogram) — shows data used either at the input or at the output; **1 in, 1 out**. **Decision / control** (diamond) — used when a yes/no or true/false question needs an answer, the answer determining the path the flow takes; **1 in, exactly 2 out**, both labelled. **Flowline** (arrow) — carries the flow from one symbol's exit to the next symbol's entry and fixes the direction, normally top to bottom and left to right; an upward flowline is the **back-arrow** that makes a loop. **Merge** (or a plain rejoining of lines) — combines various paths into a single path, so that the two branches of a selection can continue as one; **several in, 1 out**. With these: **sequence** is a chain of 1-in-1-out boxes; **selection** is a decision whose two labelled branches either both reach STOP or rejoin at the merge; **iteration** is a decision one of whose branches is a back-arrow to a box already visited. The decision is the only symbol allowed more than one exit because it is the only symbol that **produces an answer**. A flowchart must be unambiguous — a reader tracing it must know at every point where the flow goes next — so a symbol may only have two exits if something in the chart states which of them is taken, and the only thing that can state that is the decision's condition together with the Yes/No labels on its branches. Give a rectangle two exits and the chart no longer says what happens; give the decision two, and the condition says it.

**11.** *Concept: the connector as a two-piece flowline, and why misreading it as a terminator corrupts the trace.* A matched pair of connectors is a single flowline drawn in two pieces. The flow arrives at the first circle, which carries the label, and **resumes** at the second circle bearing the same label — on the same page for the **on-page connector**, on a different page for the **link (off-page) connector**. Between them nothing is executed and nothing is decided; the pair is a jump, and the label is the only thing joining its two ends. A reader who takes the first circle for a **STOP terminator** — an easy slip, since both have a line arriving and none leaving — will conclude that the path ends there. Two wrong conclusions follow. First, the trace stops early: everything downstream of the twin connector is never reached, so the reader reports the wrong output, and in a loop drawn with connectors reports no repetition at all. Second, the reader will judge the chart's structure wrongly, believing the path terminates when in fact it may run on for ever, or believing the chart has several stop points and, worse, that the part of the drawing hanging off the twin connector is unreachable dead material. The principle connectors exist to protect is that **flow lines should generally not cross, if possible**, together with the requirement that the chart be **easy to follow, neat and clear**: a connector replaces exactly the long, awkward line that would have had to cross others. The second, related motive is the course's own disadvantage that a complex chart **might span multiple pages** — which is what the link connector answers.

**12.** *Concept: the rules of flow are independent, and satisfying them is not the same as being a good chart.* **Second satisfied, first broken.** Every decision may carry two properly labelled branches and the chart may still contain a path that never reaches STOP. The plain case is the loop whose condition can never turn: a chart reading START, then `count = 0`, then `Enter n`, then `sum = sum + n`, then a decision "Is count < 5?" with a Yes back-arrow to `Enter n` and a labelled No branch to `Print sum` and STOP, has a perfectly formed decision — one in, two out, both labelled — but nothing inside the loop ever changes `count`, so the answer is Yes on every pass, the No branch is never taken and the STOP is unreachable. The second case is a branch that simply runs into nothing: both branches labelled, but one of them ends at a process box with no outgoing flowline, so that path has no end point. Both offend the principle that **the flowchart should have a start point and a stop point** — meaning every path must arrive at one, not merely that a STOP is drawn somewhere on the page. **Both satisfied, still a bad chart.** A chart can be formally impeccable — every path terminating, every decision two-branched and labelled — and still fail the course's remaining principles. Flow lines may wander and **cross** one another repeatedly where a pair of connectors would have kept them apart. The conditions in the diamonds may be compound tangles such as "Is (mark >= 50 and attendance >= 75) or lecturer override?" when the principle is that **instructions for comparisons should be made as simple as possible**, and the fix is to split it into two or three plain decisions. The layout may run in every direction instead of the usual **top to bottom and left to right**, so the reader loses the thread. All of these offend the principle that the chart be **easy to follow, neat and clear such that the visual impact is very good** — which matters precisely because the advantages the course claims for flowcharts are advantages *for a reader*: simplifying the logic, letting you ascertain whether the logic works, and letting other programmers understand it from the specification documents. A chart nobody can follow delivers none of them, however legal its arrows.

# Monday — MTH_102 nightly check

*Core differentiation theory: the derivative as a limit and what the limit is doing, the tangent/secant picture and the notations, and the constant, constant multiple, sum, power, product, quotient and chain rules stated with their exact forms and conditions.*
*Sit cold, notes closed, 8 minutes. Score out of 6.*

**1.** In f′(x) = lim_{h→0} [f(x + h) − f(x)]/h, the quotient is 0/0 when h = 0. Which statement correctly describes what is going on?
a) f′(x) is the limit of the quotient as h → 0, and the limit never evaluates the quotient at h = 0 at all; cancelling the common factor h is legitimate precisely because h ≠ 0 throughout the approach
b) f′(x) is the value the quotient takes once h is set to 0, which is legitimate because after the h has been cancelled the resulting expression is defined at h = 0
c) f′(x) is the value of the quotient for a very small but non-zero h, so the derivative is an approximation to the true rate of change whose accuracy improves as h is made smaller
d) f′(x) is the average rate of change of f over the interval [x, x + h], with h chosen small enough that the average rate and the instantaneous rate become equal

**2.** Which statement about the power rule is correct?
a) d/dx(xⁿ) = n x^(n−1) holds only for positive integer n; negative and fractional exponents fall outside it and must be handled by the quotient rule and the surd rules respectively
b) d/dx(xⁿ) = n x^(n−1) holds for every real constant n, so d/dx(x⁻²) = −2x⁻³ and d/dx(x^(1/2)) = (1/2)x^(−1/2); but it does not apply to 2ˣ, where the exponent varies and the base is fixed, and whose derivative is 2ˣ ln 2
c) d/dx(xⁿ) = n x^(n−1) holds for every real n, and by the same rule d/dx(2ˣ) = x·2^(x−1), the base being carried down unchanged
d) d/dx(xⁿ) = n x^(n−1) holds for every real n except n = 0 and n = 1, which must be handled separately by the constant rule and the identity rule since n − 1 would otherwise be negative or zero

**3.** For y = u/v with u, v differentiable and v ≠ 0, which is the quotient rule, and why does the order in the numerator matter?
a) dy/dx = (u′v − uv′)/v², and the order matters because subtraction is not commutative: writing (uv′ − u′v)/v² produces exactly the negative of the correct derivative at every point
b) dy/dx = (uv′ − u′v)/v², the numerator being read left to right in the same order as the product rule's u′v + uv′, so that the two rules stay consistent
c) dy/dx = (u′v − uv′)/v, with a single v underneath, since the original expression contained only one division and squaring it would change the degree of the answer
d) dy/dx = u′/v′, which follows by writing u = y·v and differentiating both sides, the v's then cancelling

**4.** Which statement of the chain rule is correct, together with its condition?
a) If y = f(g(x)) then dy/dx = f′(x)·g′(x): the derivative of a composite is the product of the two functions' derivatives with respect to x
b) If y = f(g(x)) then dy/dx = f′(g(x)): the outer derivative evaluated at the inner function; no extra factor is needed because u = g(x) is already a function of x and the dependence is therefore built in
c) If y = f(u) and u = g(x), with g differentiable at x and f differentiable at g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx) = f′(g(x))·g′(x): the outer derivative evaluated at the inner function, multiplied by the inner derivative
d) If y = f(g(x)) then dy/dx = f′(g′(x)): differentiate the inner function first and feed the result into the outer derivative, working from the inside out

**5. (explain why)** Write down the definition of f′(x) as a limit. Use it to prove (i) the constant rule and (ii) the constant multiple rule, showing at each step where the properties of limits are being used. Then explain why the same one-line argument does **not** extend to a product of two functions of x, and state the product rule in its exact form together with its condition on u and v.

**6. (explain why)** State the product, quotient and chain rules exactly, with conditions. For each, explain what its **structure** records — why the product rule has two terms *added*, why the quotient rule has two terms *subtracted in a fixed order* over v², and why the chain rule ends in a *multiplication* by the inner derivative. Then, for each rule, give on a named example the specific wrong answer produced by ignoring that structure.

## Answers

**1. a** — *A limit is a statement about the approach, never about the point itself; that is exactly why an expression which is 0/0 at h = 0 can still have a perfectly definite limit.* Through the whole approach h is a non-zero number, so [f(x+h) − f(x)]/h is a genuine quotient and dividing the numerator's common factor h out of it is ordinary algebra, valid for every h ≠ 0. The simplified expression agrees with the quotient at every h ≠ 0, so it has the same limit — and *its* limit is what f′(x) is defined to be. This is the same manoeuvre as Week 3's cancellation in (x² − 1)/(x − 1); the derivative is that technique promoted to a definition. (b) is the near-miss and the discriminating distractor: it gets the right number by the right algebra but describes it as *substituting* h = 0, which would require the original quotient to be defined there — it is not, and the simplified expression is a different function that merely shares the limit. (c) turns an exact object into an approximation: no non-zero h gives f′(x); the derivative is the limit, which is a single exact number, not a small-h estimate. (d) says the average rate "becomes equal" to the instantaneous rate for small h, which is false for every h ≠ 0 — for f(x) = x² the average over [x, x+h] is 2x + h, never 2x; the limit is what closes the gap, not smallness.

**2. b** — *The power rule is a statement about a varying base raised to a constant exponent, and it is valid for every real exponent; an exponential reverses those roles and needs a different rule entirely.* n x^(n−1) holds for n negative (1/x² = x⁻² gives −2x⁻³ = −2/x³), fractional (√x = x^(1/2) gives (1/2)x^(−1/2) = 1/(2√x)) and irrational alike, which is precisely why the standard first move is to **rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers**. In 2ˣ the exponent is the variable, so no power rule applies: d/dx(aˣ) = aˣ ln a. (a) is the commonest false restriction and would leave you unable to differentiate 3/x² or √x at all, forcing the quotient rule where none is needed. (c) is the headline exponential error: it treats x in the exponent as though it were the base, producing x·2^(x−1), which is not even correct at a single point of interest. (d) invents exceptions from a misreading of the arithmetic: n = 0 gives 0·x⁻¹ = 0, which *is* the constant rule, and n = 1 gives 1·x⁰ = 1 — the rule reproduces both special cases rather than breaking on them.

**3. a** — *The quotient rule is an ordered, signed statement; both the order of the two products and the square on the denominator are load-bearing.* With u′v first, y = x/(x + 1) gives [1·(x + 1) − x·1]/(x + 1)² = 1/(x + 1)², positive everywhere, matching a curve that increases on each branch. Reversing the numerator returns −1/(x + 1)², the exact negative, so a student who mis-remembers the order gets every sign wrong and, on a maximum/minimum question, every conclusion reversed. (b) is the plausible-sounding one: it appeals to consistency with the product rule, but the product rule is a *sum*, where order is irrelevant, and importing that ordering into a difference is exactly the mistake. (c) drops the square, which fails dimensionally and numerically — for x/(x + 1) it would give 1/(x + 1), a function with the wrong behaviour far out. (d) is the quotient analogue of "the derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives": for x/(x + 1) it gives 1/1 = 1, claiming a constant slope for a curve that flattens toward y = 1.

**4. c** — *The chain rule computes a rate of change through an intermediate variable, so the two rates are multiplied, and the outer rate must be measured where the inner function actually is.* If u changes du/dx times as fast as x, and y changes dy/du times as fast as u, then y changes (dy/du)(du/dx) times as fast as x — a multiplication of rates, with the differentiability of g at x and of f at g(x) as the conditions. On y = (4x − 7)⁶ this gives 6(4x − 7)⁵·4 = 24(4x − 7)⁵. (b) is the single commonest error in the entire topic: it evaluates the outer derivative correctly but drops the factor g′(x), giving 6(4x − 7)⁵ — wrong by a factor of 4 everywhere. Its stated reason is precisely the misunderstanding, since "u depends on x" is *why* the extra factor is needed, not why it can be omitted. (d) composes the derivatives instead of multiplying them, f′(g′(x)), which for the same example plugs the constant 4 into 6(·)⁵ and returns the constant 6·4⁵ — a function of nothing. (a) differentiates the outer function with respect to x as though f were a function of x directly; f′(x) is not even defined here, since f's argument is u.

**5.** *Concept: every differentiation rule is a theorem about the limit of a difference quotient, and each rule is exactly as general as the limit manipulation that proves it.* **The definition:** f′(x) = lim_{h→0} [f(x + h) − f(x)]/h, where f is differentiable at x precisely when this limit exists and is finite. **(i) Constant rule.** Let f(x) = k for all x. Then f(x + h) − f(x) = k − k = 0, so for every h ≠ 0 the quotient is 0/h = **0** — not an indeterminate form, since the numerator is exactly zero while h is not. The limit of the constant function 0 is 0, so **d/dx(k) = 0**. Geometrically the graph is a horizontal line and every secant slope is 0 before any limit is taken. **(ii) Constant multiple rule.** Let g(x) = k·f(x). Then [g(x + h) − g(x)]/h = [k f(x + h) − k f(x)]/h = k·[f(x + h) − f(x)]/h, by factoring the constant out of the numerator — pure algebra, valid for each h ≠ 0. Now the **constant multiple law for limits** (Week 3) lets the k pass through: lim_{h→0} k·Q(h) = k·lim_{h→0} Q(h), provided the inner limit exists. Hence **g′(x) = k f′(x)**. The step that does the work is that k is *the same number* for every h, so it is untouched by the approach. **Why this does not extend to a product.** Write P(x) = u(x)v(x). Then P(x + h) − P(x) = u(x+h)v(x+h) − u(x)v(x), and there is no factor to pull out: v is **not** a constant, so the "same number for every h" argument collapses — v(x + h) differs from v(x), and the difference is itself of order h. The honest computation adds and subtracts u(x+h)v(x): the numerator becomes u(x+h)[v(x+h) − v(x)] + v(x)[u(x+h) − u(x)], which on dividing by h and letting h → 0 gives u(x)v′(x) + v(x)u′(x) — **two** terms, one for each factor's contribution to the change. That is why the product of the derivatives is not the derivative of the product: u′v′ accounts for neither contribution, and on u = x³, v = x⁵ gives 15x⁶ instead of 8x⁷. **The rule:** if u and v are both differentiable at x, then **d/dx(uv) = u′v + uv′**.

**6.** *Concept: each rule's algebraic shape is a record of how the change in the answer is assembled from changes in the parts, so the standard errors are all failures to respect that shape.* **Product rule** — u, v differentiable at x ⟹ **(uv)′ = u′v + uv′**. Two terms *added*, because over a small step both factors move, and to first order the change in the product is (change in u)·v plus u·(change in v); each term holds one factor still while the other varies, and the sum collects both contributions. *Ignoring the structure:* on y = (x² + 3)(2x − 1) a student writes u′v′ = 2x·2 = **4x**, whereas the truth is 2x(2x − 1) + (x² + 3)(2) = **6x² − 2x + 6** — the wrong degree, not merely the wrong coefficient. **Quotient rule** — v ≠ 0 ⟹ **(u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v²**. Two terms *subtracted in a fixed order*, because a rise in the denominator *lowers* the quotient: growth in u helps (+u′v), growth in v hurts (−uv′). The v² arises because the two competing effects, each already carrying one v, are compared over a common denominator. *Ignoring the structure:* on y = (3x + 2)/(x² + 1) the correct derivative is (−3x² − 4x + 3)/(x² + 1)²; reversing the numerator gives (3x² + 4x − 3)/(x² + 1)², the **exact negative**, and dropping the square gives a function with entirely different far-field behaviour. **Chain rule** — g differentiable at x, f differentiable at g(x) ⟹ **(f∘g)′(x) = f′(g(x))·g′(x)**, equivalently dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx). It ends in a *multiplication* because rates through an intermediate variable compose multiplicatively: if u moves g′(x) times as fast as x, then whatever rate f has *per unit of u* must be scaled by that factor to become a rate per unit of x. The outer derivative must additionally be evaluated at g(x), not at x, because that is where the inner function has actually put us. *Ignoring the structure:* on y = (3x² + 5)⁴ the correct answer is 4(3x² + 5)³·6x = **24x(3x² + 5)³**; forgetting the inner derivative gives 4(3x² + 5)³, which coincides with the truth only at the single point x = 1/6; multiplying by the inner *function* instead of its derivative gives 4(3x² + 5)⁴, a fourth power where a third belongs. The unifying moral is that the rules are not mnemonics to be recalled in outline — the number of terms, their signs, their order and the point of evaluation each carry information, and losing any one of them produces a wrong function, not a wrong constant.

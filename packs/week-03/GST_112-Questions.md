## Multiple choice (12)

**1.** In the triangular-trade structure this week describes, Europeans were the:
A) Source  B) Destination  C) Organisers  D) Middlemen only in the Americas

**2.** Philip Curtin described the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade as:

**3.** The Asiento refers to:
A) The Portuguese route connecting Europe, Africa and the Americas
B) The Spanish licence system granting the right to trade slaves in the New World
C) The British bill of 1833 to abolish the slave trade

**4.** A swollen slave population under the Asiento system led to an uprising in:

**5.** By the 18th century, which nation was carrying more than half of all enslaved Africans across the Atlantic?

## Short answer (3)

**13.** Trace the company's name changes from Macgregor Laird's 1858 joint-stock company through to the Royal Niger Company, naming the two charter-related dates (refusal and grant).

**14.** Using the named actors, explain how the 1851 Kosoko–Akitoye crisis led to the 1861 annexation of Lagos.

**15.** Name the Oil River Protectorate's seven districts, and state the year it was renamed the Niger Coast Protectorate.

## Answers

**1. C** — the source describes the trade as **triangular**: **Africa** (source), the **Americas** (destination), **Europeans** (organisers).
**2. A** — **Philip Curtin**'s description: "the largest intercontinental migration in western History," spanning **300 years**.
**3. B** — the **Asiento** was the **Spanish** licence system granting the right to trade slaves in the New World.
**4. B** — the uprising was in **Puerto Rico in 1527**, as the slave population under the Asiento swelled enough to mobilise and rebel.
**5. C** — by the **18th century**, **Britain** was the biggest slave-trading nation, carrying more than half of all slaves from Africa. (The Dutch led in the **17th century**; Portugal started the trade in the **15th century**; Spain's role by the 19th century was through Europeans already settled in the New World, not direct trading.)
**6. A** — **Paul Lovejoy** notes **over 7 million** died in transit, on top of the roughly **15 million** who reached the Americas — two separate figures, not alternatives.
**7. B** — Buxton's **1833** bill argued the trade had to be stopped **"from Africa"** itself; the source names this reasoning as the chain leading to the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**.
**8. B** — **Kosoko** signed the 1851 treaty, then refused to honour it, triggering the British bombardment. **Akitoye** is the ruler Britain restored afterward.
**9. B** — Beecroft's 1849 appointment marked **the start of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria**, well before the 1861 annexation or the 1886 Royal Charter.
**10. C** — NAC's charter was refused in **1881**, its capital reached £1m by **1882** (still blocked by French competition), and the Royal Charter was finally granted in **1886**, creating the Royal Niger Company.
**11. C** — renamed the **Niger Coast Protectorate** in **1893**, nine years after the ORP's 1884 founding.
**12. A** — the **raid on Akassa by the Brass people** (1895), combined with Chamberlain's appointment, weakened the RNC and led to the **Kirk Commission of Enquiry**. (Ralph Moor's appointment was **1891**, four years earlier — a separate stage in the decline.)
**13.** Laird's **1858** joint-stock company was the **Central African Company**. Amalgamation under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman** produced the **United African Company**, renamed the **National African Company (NAC)** after it bought two rival French firms. NAC's charter application was **refused in 1881** (lack of capacity); by **1882** its capital reached £1 million, but French competition still blocked a British charter. NAC finally received its **Royal Charter in 1886** and became the **Royal Niger Company (RNC)**, which by 1887 controlled the whole Niger basin.
**14.** In **1851**, Britain signed a treaty with **Kosoko**, ruler of Lagos, to end the slave trade in favour of "legitimate" trade. Kosoko refused to honour it, so the British **bombarded** Lagos. On **29 December 1851**, **Akitoye** — Kosoko's rival — regained the Lagos throne, and Kosoko **fled to Epe**. Britain then consolidated its position with a further trading treaty in **1852**, and finally moved from influence to control by formally **annexing Lagos in 1861**.
**15.** The Oil River Protectorate's seven districts: **Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, and Opobo**. It was established in **1884** and renamed the **Niger Coast Protectorate** in **1893**.

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Set out, in order with years, the chain of companies that runs from Macgregor Laird's 1858 firm to the Royal Niger Company, naming each company, the man behind the amalgamation, and the three charter dates.

**14.** State the conditions of the Middle Passage as given this week (duration, space, ship capacity, diseases), and the two figures for the human cost of the trade, naming the scholar attached to the second.

**15.** Explain Buxton's change of strategy in 1833 and its stated implication for Lagos, then state the economic reason the lesson gives for Europe's turn against the slave trade.

## Answers

**1. B** — the **Bight of Benin**, with **Lagos** the leading slave port. *The persistence of the trade after abolition*: legitimate commodities (palm oil, ivory) were expanding at the same time.
**2. B** — **1849**, the appointment of **Beecroft** "for the purpose of control and supervision", marking the **beginning of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria**.
**3. C** — **Epe**. *The Kosoko–Akitoye feud*: Kosoko signed an 1851 treaty to end the slave trade, refused it, was bombarded, and fled; **Akitoye** was restored on **29 December 1851**.
**4. B** — **Ado, Onitsha and Gbebe**. Laird was also **granted admiralty in the Delta**. (Option A lists Oil Rivers Protectorate districts, not Laird's ports.)
**5. C** — **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**. *The company chain*: Laird's **Central African Company (1858)** → **United African Company** → **National African Company** (after buying **two rival French firms**) → **Royal Niger Company**.
**6. C** — **1886**. Applied **1881**, refused for **lack of capacity**; by **1882** capital was raised to **£1 million** but the presence of **French firms** made a British charter impossible; granted **1886**.
**7. B** — **seven districts** (**Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo**), renamed the **Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893**. *Same territory, new name.*
**8. B** — **Joseph Chamberlain's** appointment as Colonial Secretary and the **Akassa raid** by the **Brass** people, which prompted the **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** and led to the **revocation of the RNC's charter**. (Earlier causes of decline: opponents' alliances, **Sir Ralph Moor** as ORP Consul from **1891**, a price war, and smuggling.)
**9. B** — *the Asiento*: the **Spanish** system granting the **right to trade in slaves in the New World**, later made **transferable** to other Europeans. Before 1600 the **Portuguese** were the chief West African slave traders, taking people to **Brazil** or selling them to **Spanish settlers**.
**10. B** — **Portuguese** from the **15th century**; the **Dutch** joined them as a leading slave-trading nation in the **17th century**; the **British** were the biggest in the **18th century**, carrying **more than half** the total. By the **19th century** European nations were no longer active and **settlers in the New World** organised much of the trade.
**11. B** — over **15 million** reached the Americas; **Paul Lovejoy** notes over **7 million died in transit**. *Arrivals is the larger figure.*
**12. C** — **Thomas Fowell Buxton**, a **British parliamentarian**. (Johnston, Crowther and Hoenigsberg are the **critics of the RNC**; Chamberlain is the 1895 Colonial Secretary.)
**13.** *Company rule*: **1858 — Central African Company**, the joint stock company formed by **Macgregor Laird** after his **1857** expedition (Laird died **1861**). **1862** — the **River Niger Navigation and Trading Company** is floated, alongside the **Company of African Merchants**, and big firms (**West African Company, Alexander Miller Bros, Mr James Pinnock, the Central African Company of London**) take charge of the Delta trade. Amalgamation then produces the **United African Company** under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**, renamed the **National African Company (NAC)** after it bought **two rival French firms**. By **1882** it controlled large areas of the Niger Delta but could not monopolise the trade. The three charter dates: **applied 1881** (refused, lack of capacity); **1882** capital raised to **£1 million** but blocked by French firms; **1886** the **Royal Charter** granted, and it becomes the **Royal Niger Company**, controlling **all the territories in the basin of the Niger by 1887**.
**14.** *The Middle Passage*: the crossing took **5 to 8 weeks** in terrible conditions; each slave was allotted a space of **6ft by 1ft by 4 inches**, so that a ship carried between **451 and 600 slaves**; they suffered diseases including **smallpox, dysentery and ophthalmia**. Human cost: **over 15 million** Africans reached the Americas, and **Paul Lovejoy** notes that **over 7 million died in transit**, with a high percentage dying on the middle passage itself and many killed in the brutal process of capture. **Phillip Curtin** called it "the largest intercontinental migration in western History", spanning **300 years**.
**15.** *Abolition from the African end*: existing strategy (Britain had been **seizing slave ships since 1792** and repatriating them to **Sierra Leone**) had achieved little, so **Buxton's 1833 bill** argued that for the slave trade to stop, **it must be stopped from Africa** — the implication of which was the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**. The economic reason: **rapid economic and technological development in the metropole** meant slave labour was no longer needed; Europe now wanted Africans **to remain in Africa** to grow **cash crops** for its industries and to serve as a **ready market for finished goods**. Hence the end of the trade "ushered in a new form of exploitation known as **colonialism**".

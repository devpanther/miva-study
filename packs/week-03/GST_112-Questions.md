## Answers

**1. c) 1886** — 1881 the application was refused for lack of capacity; 1882 capital reached £1 million but French firms blocked it; the charter came in 1886.
**2. a) 29 December 1851** — the date Akitoye regained the seat of Lagos after the bombardment; the annexation itself was ten years later, in 1861.
**3. d) The beginning of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria** — Beecroft's 1849 appointment was explicitly "for the purpose of control and supervision."
**4. b) Ado, Onitsha and Gbebe** — the three Delta trading ports from Laird's 1857 expedition; Bonny, Brass, Opobo and Forcados are ORP districts instead.
**5. a) Thomas Fowell Buxton** — a British parliamentarian; his change of strategy (stop the trade from Africa) implied the annexation of Lagos.
**6. d) Chamberlain's appointment and the Akassa raid** — described as "two significant but unrelated events" by 1895; the raid triggered the Kirk Commission.
**7. b) The Niger Coast Protectorate, in 1893** — the ORP was founded in 1884 with 7 districts and renamed nine years later.
**8. c) Portuguese (15th) → Dutch joined (17th) → British biggest (18th)** — British ships then carried more than half of all slaves from Africa.
**9. d) The early 15th century; for nearly 200 years** — Portuguese sailors sought new markets and improved seafaring and ship design (the caravel); dominance rested on that plus fabrics, spices and gold. Do not confuse the ~200 years with Curtin's 300-year span of the slave trade.
**10. a) Opening up the area to the rest of the world, which eventually led to colonialism and exploitation** — one of four named impacts, with the wars fought to keep up the slave supply, the abandonment of African cultures, and the major role in the trans-Atlantic slave trade. Brass casting in Benin *grew*, and the Asiento was Spanish.
**11. b) Over 7 million died in transit** — the 15 million figure is the estimate of those who reached the Americas; the 300 years is Curtin's.
**12. c) The Kirk Commission's rejection of its charter application in 1881** — invented: the Kirk Commission of Enquiry investigated the Akassa raid; the 1881 refusal was on grounds of lack of capacity.

**13.** 1851: the British sign a treaty with **Kosoko** to end the slave trade and embrace "legitimate" trade → Kosoko refuses → the consequent **bombardment** of Lagos → **29 December 1851**, **Akitoye** regains the seat of Lagos while **Kosoko flees to Epe** → trade in palm oil expands and the **1852** treaty secures British traders, the right to trade and storage facilities against import/export duties → **1861, the annexation of Lagos**.

**14.** Laird's **Central African Company** (1858) and the later proliferation of firms amalgamated into the **United African Company** under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**, renamed the **National African Company** after it bought **two rival French firms**. **1881**: charter applied for, refused for lack of capacity. **1882**: capital raised to **£1 million**, but French firms still made a British charter impossible; the company controlled large areas of the Delta yet could not monopolise trade. **1886**: Royal Charter granted; it became the **Royal Niger Company**, and by **1887** controlled all territories in the basin of the Niger. Decline: opponents' alliances, **Sir Ralph Moor** as Consul of the ORP (1891), price war, smuggling; then by **1895** **Joseph Chamberlain**'s appointment as Colonial Secretary and the **Brass people's raid on Akassa**, investigated by the **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** → **revocation of the charter**.

**15.** Rapid economic and technological development in the **metropole** removed the need for slave labour: Europeans now wanted Africans to **stay in Africa** growing **cash crops** for European industries and forming a **ready market for finished goods**, which is why "legitimate" trade in palm oil and ivory expanded. From **1792** the British began **seizing slave ships and repatriating them to Sierra Leone**; **Buxton's 1833 bill** argued the existing strategy had failed and that the trade **must be stopped from Africa** — a base on the African coast was therefore needed, and since Lagos was the leading slave port in the Bight of Benin (the last centre of the trade by 1840), the implication was the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**.

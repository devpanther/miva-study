## Short answer

**13.** State the test that tells a **compound** sentence from a **complex** one, and give one original example of each, naming the joining word in both.

**14.** Define **function words** as the lesson defines them, list the five parts of speech that fall under them, and state the one property that separates them from content words in **speech**.

**15.** Give **both** labels — structure and function — for: "Close the window before the rain starts." Then state precisely how you tell an imperative sentence from a declarative one.

## Answers

**1. C** — Two independent clauses ("the students continued their exams" / "the invigilator lit a lantern") plus one dependent clause ("Although the generator failed") = **compound-complex**. **D** would need only one independent clause; **B** would need no dependent clause at all; **A** would need a single clause.

**2. A** — **Function** describes a sentence by its purpose — declarative, imperative, interrogative, exclamatory, plus the conditional category. **B**, structure, describes how it is built out of clauses. **C** and **D** are levels of analysis the lesson does not use for this contrast.

**3. D** — **Adverbs** are content (lexical) words. The function-word parts of speech the lesson's body slide lists are **conjunctions, auxiliary/helping verbs, prepositions, articles and pronouns** — so **A**, **B** and **C** are all on the list. (Be aware the deck's summary slide slips and adds "certain adverbs"; the body slide is the one to answer from.)

**4. B** — It gives a command or request with an understood subject "you", which is the **imperative**. **A** would state a fact or opinion, **C** would ask, **D** would exclaim.

**5. A** — Two independent clauses joined by a **semicolon**, and a semicolon makes a sentence compound just as a coordinating conjunction would. **B** and **D** both require a dependent clause, and there is none; **C** requires one clause.

**6. D** — Proximity agreement makes the verb (or noun) agree with the **nearest** subject where several subject elements are present. **A**, **B** and **C** each name a different candidate — first, plural, or "main" — and none of them is the rule.

**7. B** — **The number of independent clauses**: complex has exactly one, compound-complex has two or more. **A** fails because both types contain at least one dependent clause and so both may carry a subordinating conjunction. **C** is irrelevant — a long sentence can be simple. **D** points at compounding, not at the complex/compound-complex line.

**8. C** — **"Sitting"** is a main (lexical) verb, and main verbs are content words. **A** is a pronoun, **B** an auxiliary verb, **D** a preposition — all three are function words.

**9. A** — **Conditional** sentences express a condition and its likely result, and the lesson adds them as a category beyond declarative, imperative, interrogative and exclamatory. **B**, **C** and **D** are three of those four main types, not the additional one.

**10. D** — With "or"/"nor" the **Rule of Proximity** applies: the nearest subject is the singular *designer*, so the verb is singular — **has**. **A**, **B** and **C** are all plural forms, chosen by looking at *developers* instead of at the closest subject.

**11. B** — **Content words** are accentuated in speech; function words remain non-stressed, which is why the distinction refines pronunciation. **A** inverts the rule; **C** and **D** narrow it to a single function-word class each.

**12. C** — One independent clause ("Nneoma cooked dinner") plus one dependent clause introduced by the subordinating conjunction *while* = **complex**. **B** would require both clauses to stand alone; **D** would require a second independent clause; **A** would require a single clause.

**13.** *Compound vs complex.* Count the independent clauses and check the joining word. **Compound** = two or more clauses that could each stand alone, joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so) or by a semicolon — e.g. *The lecturer arrived late, but the class had already started* (joiner: **but**). **Complex** = one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause that cannot stand alone, usually introduced by a subordinating conjunction (although, because, while, since) — e.g. *Because the lecturer arrived late, the class started without him* (joiner: **because**).

**14.** *Function words.* Function words — also called grammatical or structure words — have a primarily grammatical function and convey little or no meaning on their own; they connect content words and build the relationships between them, and they are crucial to a sentence's grammatical structure. The five parts of speech the lesson lists under them are **conjunctions, auxiliary/helping verbs, prepositions, articles and pronouns**. In **speech**, function words are **non-stressed**, whereas content words are stressed or accentuated — that is the separating property the lesson gives.

**15.** *"Close the window before the rain starts."* Structure: **complex** — one independent clause ("Close the window") plus one dependent clause ("before the rain starts"), introduced by the subordinating conjunction *before*. Function: **imperative** — it issues a command or instruction. The test: an imperative's subject ("you") is **understood rather than stated**, and the sentence tells someone to do something; a declarative **states** a fact, opinion or idea and carries an explicit subject (*You closed the window before the rain started.*).

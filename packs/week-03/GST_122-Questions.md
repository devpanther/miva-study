## Short answer (3)

**13.** Explain the difference between a compound sentence and a complex sentence, and name the type of connector each one uses.

**14.** State the Rule of Proximity Agreement in your own words, and give one example sentence where swapping the order of two "either/or" (or "neither/nor") subjects changes which verb is correct.

**15.** Define function words and content words, and explain the closed-class/open-class distinction that separates them.

## Answers

**1. c** — *complex*: "Although the exam was difficult" is a dependent clause attached to one independent clause via the subordinating conjunction "although."
**2. a** — *simple*: one subject ("Ngozi") shared by a compound verb ("cooked... and cleaned..."). One independent clause = simple, even though it names two actions — the classic simple-vs-compound trap.
**3. c** — *compound-complex*: "The lecturer apologised to the class" and "the students had already left" are two independent clauses joined by "but," and "who arrived late" is a dependent (relative) clause embedded in the first.
**4. b** — *imperative*: it gives an instruction with an understood "you" subject, regardless of naming two actions.
**5. b** — *interrogative*: it opens with the question word "what" and ends in a question mark.
**6. c** — *imperative*: despite the polite "please" and no exclamation mark, it is still a command/request with an understood "you" — function is about purpose, not tone.
**7. b** — *content word*: "quickly" is an adverb describing how the approval happened; it carries real information, unlike a function word.
**8. c** — *function word*: "between" is a preposition (closed class); the other three are open-class content words (noun, adjective, verb).
**9. b** — *subject-verb agreement*: the true subject is "list" (singular); "of items on the shelf" is a prepositional phrase, and its object is never the subject. The verb must agree with "list," so "is" is correct as written.
**10. a** — *Rule of Proximity Agreement*: with "neither...nor," the verb agrees with the nearer subject — here, "the teacher" (singular) — so "is" is correct.
**11. c** — *"and" joins subjects*: "the manager and the clerk" is a compound subject joined by "and," treated as a plural unit, so it takes the plural verb "were."
**12. b** — *Rule of Proximity Agreement*: with "either...or," the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "manager" (singular), not with the total number of people named — so "is" is correct.
**13.** A compound sentence joins two or more **independent** clauses (each able to stand alone) using a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so...) or a semicolon. A complex sentence joins one independent clause with at least one **dependent** clause (cannot stand alone) using a subordinating conjunction (although, because, while, since...). The test is whether both halves can stand alone (compound) or only one can (complex).
**14.** The Rule of Proximity Agreement says that when a subject has more than one element (typically linked by or/nor, as in either...or or neither...nor), the verb agrees in number with whichever element is closest to it, not with the subject as a whole. Example: "Neither the teacher nor the students **are** ready" (verb agrees with the nearer, plural "students") versus "Neither the students nor the teacher **is** ready" (same two subjects, reordered — verb now agrees with the nearer, singular "teacher").
**15.** Function words (grammatical/structure words: conjunctions, auxiliary verbs, prepositions, articles, pronouns) carry little meaning of their own and exist to connect and build relationships between content words. Content words (lexical words: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) carry the sentence's actual, real-world meaning. Function-word categories are **closed classes** — their membership is fixed and essentially never grows; content-word categories are **open classes** — new words (like "to google") are coined and borrowed into them all the time.

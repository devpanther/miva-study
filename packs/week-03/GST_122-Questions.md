## Section A - Multiple choice (12)

**1.** For a group of words to be a complete sentence it must contain:
a) An adjective and an adverb
b) A subject (noun or pronoun) and a predicate (verb)
c) A conjunction and an article
d) At least two clauses

**2.** A sentence can be analysed in two ways. They are by:
a) Length and tone  b) Function and structure  c) Stress and spelling  d) Subject and object

**3.** "She sings beautifully." By structure this sentence is:
a) Compound  b) Complex  c) Simple  d) Compound-complex

**4.** "I wanted to go to the store, but it started raining." By structure this is:
a) Simple  b) Compound  c) Complex  d) Compound-complex

**5.** "Although it was raining, we went for a walk." By structure this is:
a) Complex  b) Compound  c) Simple  d) Compound-complex

**6.** "Nneoma cooked dinner while Lara slept, and Esohe swept the floor." By structure this is:
a) Compound  b) Complex  c) Compound-complex  d) Simple

**7.** A compound sentence is formed by joining two or more independent clauses using:
a) A subordinating conjunction such as "although"
b) A relative pronoun such as "who"
c) A coordinating conjunction such as "and", "but", "or", "so", or a semicolon
d) An article

**8.** "Please pass the salt." By function this sentence is:
a) Declarative  b) Interrogative  c) Exclamatory  d) Imperative

**9.** "Wow, that was amazing!" By function this sentence is:
a) Exclamatory  b) Declarative  c) Imperative  d) Interrogative

**10.** Which set contains ONLY function words?
a) Table, drown, robust, quietly
b) The, but, under, has
c) Mountain, beautiful, bury, loudly
d) Boy, was, plaza, clever

**11.** Content words, also called lexical words, are typically:
a) Articles, prepositions, conjunctions and pronouns
b) Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs
c) Modals, qualifiers and question words
d) Auxiliary verbs and articles

**12.** In spoken English, according to the lesson:
a) Function words are stressed and content words are unstressed
b) Both classes are stressed equally
c) Content words are stressed and function words are unstressed
d) Stress depends only on sentence length

## Section B - Short answer (3)

**13.** Define the four sentence types by structure and give one example sentence of each.

**14.** Classify "If it rains, we will stay indoors." by both function and structure, and explain why a sentence needs two labels.

**15.** Distinguish function words from content words, name the parts of speech in each class, and say why the distinction matters for pronunciation.

## Answers

**1. b** — *definition of a sentence*: a group of words expressing a complete thought, requiring a subject and a predicate.
**2. b** — *function vs structure*: function is the sentence's purpose; structure is how its clauses and elements are built.
**3. c** — *simple sentence*: one independent clause, one subject and one predicate, no dependent clause.
**4. b** — *compound sentence*: two independent clauses joined by the coordinating conjunction "but"; either half could stand alone.
**5. a** — *complex sentence*: one independent clause ("we went for a walk") plus a dependent clause introduced by the subordinator "although".
**6. c** — *compound-complex sentence*: two independent clauses ("Nneoma cooked dinner", "Esohe swept the floor") plus the dependent clause "while Lara slept".
**7. c** — *coordination*: compound sentences are built with coordinating conjunctions or a semicolon; subordinators build complex sentences instead.
**8. d** — *imperative*: a command, instruction or request with an understood subject "you"; "Please" does not make it a question.
**9. a** — *exclamatory*: expresses strong emotion and ends with an exclamation mark.
**10. b** — *function words*: articles, conjunctions, prepositions and auxiliary verbs carry grammatical relations rather than meaning; the other options are all content words.
**11. b** — *content (lexical) words*: nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs carry the main meaning of a sentence.
**12. c** — *stress*: English emphasis falls on content words while function words remain unstressed, which is why the distinction refines pronunciation and listening.
**13.** *Sentence structure*: **Simple** — one independent clause: "I like pizza." **Compound** — two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or semicolon: "She loves to read; he prefers to watch movies." **Complex** — one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause: "Because she studied hard, she aced the exam." **Compound-complex** — two or more independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause: "She went to the store because she needed groceries, and I stayed home to cook dinner."
**14.** *Double classification*: by function it is a **conditional** sentence (it states a condition and its likely result; the lesson adds conditionals to the four main functional types, and it is also declarative in form). By structure it is **complex**: "we will stay indoors" is the independent clause and "If it rains" is a dependent clause that cannot stand alone. Two labels are needed because function describes the sentence's purpose while structure describes its clause make-up, and every sentence has both at once.
**15.** *Function vs content words*: function words (also called grammatical, structure, empty or form words) have a mainly grammatical role and little meaning alone — conjunctions, auxiliary/helping verbs, prepositions, articles and pronouns, plus modals, qualifiers and some question words; they form closed classes. Content or lexical words carry the main meaning — nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs; they form open classes. The distinction matters for pronunciation because English stresses content words and leaves function words unstressed, so knowing which is which improves both natural speech rhythm and listening comprehension. Pennebaker notes function words are under 0.1% of vocabulary but nearly 60% of the words we actually use.

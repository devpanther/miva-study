## Answers

**1. a** — *Definition of computational approaches.* The course's wording is that computational approaches **express solutions to problems in a form that can be run on a computer**, using advances in computing such as **algorithms, models and simulations**. The definition is about the *form the solution takes*, not about difficulty. (b) mistakes one member of the family — data mining, with its machine learning and statistical analysis — for the whole category; nothing in the definition says human judgement is replaced. (c) imports Week 2: solvability by the UTM is a different question, asked about problems, not a description of an approach. (d) confuses choosing an approach with tuning efficiency; brute force is an approach in this week's list and is explicitly **very slow**, so speed cannot be what makes something a computational approach.

**2. b** — *The three factors that gate the use of computational methods.* Clearly defined (current situation, end goal, how to reach it, possible obstacles), computable (are the calculations possible, given processing time and capacity), data requirements examined (data types and storage capacity) — and only **after all three** is the decision made. (a) is the trap: those are **problem recognition's** three factors, which are about identifying the problem, not about deciding whether a computer can be used on it. If you picked (a), relearn the two triples side by side. (c) and (d) both smuggle in a later stage — a null hypothesis belongs to hypothesis testing, and testing before release is performance testing; neither is a precondition for deciding to use a computer at all.

**3. c** — *Computability as the course frames it.* The factor is stated as: **determine if the required calculations are possible, considering processing times and capacity.** It is a practical question about whether the machine can do the arithmetic in a usable time, not a theoretical one. (a) is the **clearly defined** factor. (b) is the **data requirements** factor. Both are real factors, which is why they are the wrong answers here — the question asks which of the three this phrase names. (d) is problem recognition's reduction to a more general case.

**4. b** — *The backtracking mechanism.* The defining move is: at a final state, **check whether that state is accepted or rejected**, then **backtrack to a previous state to try other unconsidered options**. That reversal is what no other approach this week does. (a) is **dynamic programming** — saving sub-problem results so nothing is recomputed. (c) is the **greedy** algorithm — commit to the best option at each stage and never revisit. (d) is **divide and conquer**. Each distractor is a genuine method; the question is whether you know which mechanism belongs to which name.

**5. d** — *Data mining starts from data, not from a question.* The course defines it as extracting valuable information from **large data sets** through **unnoticed patterns** and **underlying relationships within the data**, in order to make informed decisions or predict trends. The music-network example is exactly this: ratings and shares are already collected, and the questions ("which artists are most popular?") come out of the data. (a) is **hypothesis testing**, which runs the opposite way — prediction first, evidence second. (b) is **performance testing**. (c) is **brute force**. If you chose (a), fix the direction: discovery versus verification is the distinction being tested.

**6. a** — *Visualisation serves humans.* The course says explicitly that visualisation helps **humans rather than computers** to solve problems more effectively, because people process and retain information better as an image than as text. (b) inverts the relationship: a diagram may help you define the problem, but the requirement that the problem **be** clearly defined is a precondition that visualisation does not lift. (c) is **backtracking's** description. (d) misreads the definition, which says visualisation illustrates **what a problem entails and how to approach its solution** — it is used before and during the work, which is the whole point of the Dijkstra picture.

**7. d** — *The conditions on divide and conquer.* Splitting is only worth doing if the smaller parts are genuinely **simpler than the complex whole**, and if **combining their solutions is less complicated than attacking the original problem directly**. Fail either and the split has added work rather than removed it. (a) is what **dynamic programming** adds on top of splitting — storage of sub-solutions — and is the commonest wrong answer here. (b) is **pipelining**: a chain in which each part feeds the next, not independent parts recombined. (c) is **greedy**, and it also contradicts divide and conquer, which abandons nothing — every part must be solved.

**8. b** — *Performance testing is a pre-release activity.* The definition is: evaluating whether a system or program **works as expected or not before it is released**, and **using this knowledge to make it more effective**; the method is to write test scenarios with success criteria and measure against them, including erroneous data and simulated real-life circumstances (stress testing). (a) is the misunderstanding that testing means watching live users; that loses both the "before release" clause and the pre-written criteria. (c) invents a dependency on the data-requirements factor. (d) describes comparing two candidates, which is the shape of **hypothesis testing** with its null hypothesis that the two are equally good.

**9.** *Concept: what each approach does at a decision point, and what it guarantees.* **Brute force** does not choose at all: it **goes through all possible choices until a solution is found**, trying every possibility instead of improving efficiency with advanced techniques, and it therefore **relies solely on computing power**. It cannot miss an existing answer, but it is **simple and consistent yet very slow** — the padlock with four digits from 0 to 9 obliges it to face 10 000 combinations. **Greedy** chooses: at each stage it **applies the best possible solution available there**, always selecting the best optimal path, and never reconsiders. This makes it fast, and the course notes that where greedy solutions are optimal they are **always simple and highly efficient** — but it **might not always produce an optimal solution**, because the option that is best locally at one stage can close off a better whole. **Dynamic programming** breaks the problem into sub-problems and **saves their solutions so that no result has to be computed again**; the stored sub-results mean the choice at any point is made in the light of the sub-problems rather than of the current step alone. It is the one with the guarantee: **provided that a solution to the problem exists, dynamic programming guarantees that an optimal solution can be found**, which is why the course says it is mostly used for **optimization problems** — finding the minimum or maximum. Greedy carries no such guarantee precisely because "best at this stage" and "best overall" are different quantities: greedy is only ever looking at one stage, so it has no means of discovering that an inferior choice now would have paid later.

**10.** *Concept: pipelining versus divide and conquer — dependent chain versus independent parts.* **Divide and conquer** first **splits a complex problem into smaller, easier parts**; each part is then **solved**, and the solutions of the parts give the solution of the whole. The parts are **the same kind of problem in smaller form** — merge sort splits a list into lists — they can be worked **independently**, and there is a **combining step at the end** (the merge). It is subject to its two conditions: the parts must be simpler than the whole, and combining must be less complicated than solving the original directly. **Pipelining** **divides a task into a series of subtasks, where each subtask leads to the next**; each subtask is **executed by a different process in a series**, and **the result of one process feeds into the next**, repeated until the whole task is finished — as when the output of one function is the input of the next, or when an order total is produced by summing the product costs and then adding VAT to that sum. **The structural difference is dependency between the parts.** In divide and conquer the parts do not need one another's results and could in principle be solved in any order, so a combining step is required to bring them back together; in pipelining each part **cannot start until the previous one has finished**, because the previous one's output is its input, and no combining step exists because the last stage of the chain already holds the answer. So: if the pieces are smaller copies of the same problem that must later be recombined, it is divide and conquer; if the pieces are different stages that must run in a fixed order because each consumes what the one before produced, it is pipelining.

**11.** *Concept: criteria for success in problem recognition versus success criteria in performance testing.* In **problem recognition**, specifying **the problem requirement or criteria for success** is one of the three factors — alongside identifying the nature and parameters of the problem and determining whether it can be grouped as a specific type — and its purpose is to make sure the **proposed solution will deal with the root cause of the problem**. These criteria are written **at the very beginning, before anything is built**, and they define what would count as having solved the *problem*. In **performance testing**, the **success criteria** accompany a set of **test scenarios**, and their purpose is to let the team **measure the performance of the system or program against them before it is released**, so that what is learned can be used to make it more effective; erroneous data and simulated real-life circumstances are the standard scenarios. These criteria are written **after there is something to test**, and they define what would count as the *program* behaving as expected. A team that writes only the second set has criteria for whether the program does what it was built to do, but none for whether that was the right thing to build: the program can pass every scenario and still leave the root cause untouched, because nothing ever recorded what the problem required. That is the failure mode problem recognition exists to prevent.

**12.** *Concept: null hypothesis, significance level, p-value and the accept/reject rule.* A **hypothesis** is a **tentative, intelligent guess about the solution to the problem** — a **specific statement of prediction** describing in concrete terms what you expect to happen, and an assumption that **can be tested and proved right or wrong**. The **null hypothesis** is the initial assumption you set out to test: in the course's example, comparing **method A with method B** to see whether both are good, the initial assumption that **both methods are equally good** is the null hypothesis, and the task then becomes determining whether that null hypothesis is true or not. The **significance level** is **the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true** — a level of 0.05 means a **5% risk of concluding that something exists when it does not**. The **p-value** is a number **between 0 and 1** that **describes how likely it is that the null hypothesis is true**; **the smaller the p-value, the stronger the evidence that you should reject** the null. **The rule:** when the p-value of the data is **less than or equal to** the significance level, the **null hypothesis is rejected**; when the p-value is **higher** than the significance level, the **null hypothesis is accepted**. Finally, the course's caution: **rejecting the null hypothesis does not prove that something does not exist — it only means the evidence is not enough.** A rejection is a statement about the strength of the evidence at the level you chose, not a proof about the world; had you set a stricter significance level, the same data might not have licensed the rejection at all.

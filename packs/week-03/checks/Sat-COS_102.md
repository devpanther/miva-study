## Answers

**1. c** — *Performance testing carried out as the course specifies.* The method is to **come up with a set of test scenarios and success criteria** and then **measure the performance of the system or program against them**, and the course's own examples of scenarios are **testing with erroneous data** to check error handling and **simulating real-life circumstances (stress testing)**. (a) is the timing error: performance testing happens **before release**, and using complaints after release abandons the pre-written criteria that make it a measurement rather than an anecdote. (b) substitutes **data mining** — patterns found in already-gathered data — for testing the actual artefact; it may suggest scenarios but it measures nothing about the new portal. (d) substitutes **brute force**; enumerating all inputs is neither scenario-based nor feasible, and the definition asks for measurement against criteria, not exhaustive search.

**2. d** — *Sizing a brute-force space.* Four wheels, ten digits each, chosen independently, gives 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = **10 000** possibilities, and brute force **goes through all possible choices until a solution is found**, which is exactly why the course calls it **simple and consistent, but very slow**. (a) is the additive error — adding 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 instead of multiplying — which treats the wheels as if only one moved at a time. (b) treats the four digits as a permutation of a fixed set (4! = 24), but the digits may repeat and are drawn from ten values, not four. (c) contains a true statistical remark used to draw a false conclusion: the expected number of trials is about half the space, but brute force must still be **prepared** for all 10 000, and averaging does not make it fast — note that the option also attaches the average to the wrong property.

**3. b** — *The backtracking move on a dead end.* The definition says that on reaching a final state the algorithm **checks whether the state is accepted or rejected and then backtracks to a previous state to try other unconsidered options** — so a rejected leaf sends the search back to the nearest junction with an untried branch, not further forward and not back to the start. (a) is **dynamic programming's** memoisation grafted onto backtracking; storing the dead end is harmless but it is not what defines the next move, and "continue forward" from a dead end is impossible. (c) is the misunderstanding that backtracking means restarting: it discards all the work done above the failed junction, which is precisely the waste backtracking avoids. (d) is a **greedy** rule, and it also proposes a jump between leaves that the tree structure does not permit — you can only reach another leaf by going back up.

**4. a** — *Pipelining as a chain of processes.* The definition is that the task is **divided into a series of subtasks, each leading to the next**, each **executed by a different process**, with **the result of one process feeding into the next**. Summing the costs and handing that subtotal to the VAT step is exactly the course's own order-total example. (b) is the commonest error: two computations run side by side and added at the end are **independent**, so nothing feeds anything — that is parallel work, not a pipeline. (c) is **divide and conquer**: the task is split into like parts that are solved separately and combined. (d) is **dynamic programming**: storing results so nothing is recomputed. All three describe real approaches; only one describes a chain.

**5. d** — *What dynamic programming adds to an existing recursion.* Dynamic programming breaks a problem into sub-problems and **saves the solutions of the sub-problems so that there is no need to compute the result again**. Applied to this code, that means memoising: the first call to `fib(3)` computes and stores it, and the two later calls read the stored value, so the repeated work vanishes. (a) is the sharpest distractor because it is **what the code already does** — splitting into `fib(n-1)` and `fib(n-2)` is plain divide and conquer without storage, which is exactly the situation the question describes; if you chose it, the point to fix is that storage, not splitting, is dynamic programming's contribution. (b) is **brute force** over pairs of terms. (c) mixes a bottom-up table with a **greedy** "take the largest" step that has no meaning here — the term needed is fixed by `n`, not chosen for being largest.

**6. c** — *Running the greedy activity-selection rule.* Pair the times: (2,3), (4,5), (1,7), (6,8), (9,10), (6,10). Order by finish time and take greedily. (2,3) finishes at 3; the earliest finisher starting at or after 3 is (4,5); after 5 the earliest finisher available is (6,8) — note (1,7) is unavailable because it started at 1; after 8 only (9,10) remains available. That is **four** activities. (a) is the earliest-**start**-first rule: taking (1,7) because it begins first blocks both (2,3) and (4,5) and leaves room only for (9,10) — this is the standard demonstration that the greedy criterion has to be finish time. (b) chooses (6,10) over (6,8) among the two activities starting at 6, which are tied on start but not on finish; the later finish then blocks (9,10) and costs one activity. (d) simply ignores the no-overlap constraint and counts every activity that starts after 2 — (6,8) and (6,10) cannot both be worked, nor (6,10) and (9,10).

**7. d** — *Reducing a problem to a more general case, under problem recognition.* The course's example is exactly this: an algorithm to optimise the **power cable infrastructure of a building** reduces to determining the **minimum spanning tree** of a given graph. Every room must be reached, connections have lengths, and the least total length that connects everything with no redundancy is a spanning tree of minimum weight. (a) is the Dijkstra confusion — shortest path connects **two** points, whereas the requirement here is to reach **all** rooms; the Dijkstra picture in the notes belongs to **visualisation**, a different method. (b) is a **greedy** rule applied to the wrong quantity: distance from the meter is not the cost of the cable runs between rooms, so sorting by it can miss much cheaper connections between neighbouring rooms. (c) is **brute force**, which would eventually work but abandons the reduction altogether — and the point of problem recognition is to land on a general case that is already solved.

**8. b** — *Applying the accept/reject rule.* The course's rule is that when the **p-value is less than (or equal to) the significance level, the null hypothesis is rejected**, and when it is higher, the null hypothesis is accepted. Here 0.03 ≤ 0.05, so the null is rejected. (a) inverts the meaning of the p-value: the smaller the p-value, the **stronger** the evidence that the null should be rejected, not supported — this is the single most common error on this topic. (c) gets the decision right and the interpretation wrong, and the course states the caution explicitly: rejecting the null hypothesis does not prove anything about existence, it means the evidence is not enough — a rejection at 0.05 carries a 5% risk of being made when the null is in fact true. (d) has the comparison backwards; the significance level is fixed **in advance**, and adjusting it after seeing the p-value would destroy the meaning it is defined to have.

**9.** *Concept: tracing merge sort as divide and conquer, and the condition that justifies the merge.* **Split.** `[2, 8, 4, 9, 3]` divides into `[2, 8]` and `[4, 9, 3]`. `[2, 8]` divides into `[2]` and `[8]`. `[4, 9, 3]` divides into `[4]` and `[9, 3]`, and `[9, 3]` divides into `[9]` and `[3]`. Single elements are already sorted, which is the base case — the parts have become simpler than the whole. **Merge, descending, always taking the larger head first.** `[2]` with `[8]`: 2 against 8 → 8, then 2 → **`[8, 2]`**. `[9]` with `[3]`: 9 against 3 → 9, then 3 → **`[9, 3]`**. `[4]` with `[9, 3]`: 4 against 9 → 9; 4 against 3 → 4; then 3 → **`[9, 4, 3]`**. **Final merge** of `[8, 2]` with `[9, 4, 3]`: 8 against 9 → **9**; 8 against 4 → **8**; 2 against 4 → **4**; 2 against 3 → **3**; the left list is exhausted, so **2** is appended. Result: **`[9, 8, 4, 3, 2]`**. Note that descending order is produced **inside** the merge, by taking the larger head each time — not by sorting ascending and reversing afterwards. **The condition relied on:** divide and conquer is worth using only when **combining the solutions of the smaller parts is less complicated than solving the original problem directly** (its companion condition being that the parts must genuinely be simpler than the whole). Merging two already-sorted lists needs only one comparison per element placed, because each list's largest remaining element is always at its head; that is cheaper than sorting the original five-element list directly, which is what makes the split pay.

**10.** *Concept: applying greedy and divide and conquer to the delivery-routing case study.* **Greedy sequence:** (i) start each driver at the restaurant with a list of undelivered orders; (ii) from the driver's current position, compute the travel time to every undelivered order; (iii) go to the nearest one — the best possible choice available at this stage; (iv) mark it delivered and repeat from (ii) until the list is empty. This applies the best option **at each stage**, is **simple and highly efficient** to compute, and needs no view of the route as a whole. **Divide-and-conquer sequence:** (i) split the city into zones — say four quadrants — so that each zone holds a manageable set of orders; (ii) further split any zone still holding too many orders; (iii) solve each small zone: find a good route through that zone's orders alone; (iv) combine the zone routes by ordering the zones and joining each zone's exit point to the next zone's entry point, giving a route for the whole city. **The condition:** routing one zone must genuinely be **simpler than routing the whole city**, and **joining the zone routes must be less complicated than routing the city directly**. That fails if the zone boundaries cut through clusters of orders — then the joins criss-cross the city and cost more than they saved, and the split has added work. **Where greedy is not optimal:** a busy city has one-way streets, so travel times are not symmetric. Take two orders, A and B. From the restaurant, A is **2 minutes** away and B is **5 minutes** away; because of a one-way ring, A → B takes **12 minutes** while B → A takes only **3**; the return legs are A → restaurant 2 minutes and B → restaurant 5. Greedy takes the best option available at this stage — A, at 2 minutes — and is then committed to the 12-minute leg to B and a 5-minute return: **19 minutes**. Delivering B first costs 5 + 3 + 2 = **10 minutes**. The greedy rule cannot detect this, because at each stage it compares only the legs **leading out of where it now stands**; it never prices the remainder of the route, so it cannot see that paying 3 extra minutes on the first leg buys 9 back on the second. This is exactly the course's caveat that greedy **might not always produce an optimal solution**, while remaining simple and highly efficient wherever it does.

**11.** *Concept: laying out a pipeline and the dependency that defines it.* **Subtask 1 — collect line items.** Input: the order id. Output: a list of (product, quantity) pairs. **Subtask 2 — price the line items.** Input: that list. Output: a list of line costs (unit price × quantity). **Subtask 3 — sum the line costs.** Input: the list of line costs. Output: one number, the subtotal. **Subtask 4 — compute VAT.** Input: the subtotal. Output: the VAT amount. **Subtask 5 — add VAT to the subtotal.** Input: subtotal and VAT amount. Output: the total cost. (A sixth, formatting the total as currency for display, is a legitimate final stage.) **What makes it a pipeline:** each subtask is **executed by a different process in a series**, and **the result of one process is the input of the next** — subtask 3 cannot start until subtask 2 has produced its list, and subtask 4 cannot start until the subtotal exists. A mere list of steps has an order in which someone happens to do things; a pipeline has an order **forced by the data**, because each stage consumes what the previous stage produced, repeated until the whole task is completed. **If an output does not match the next input,** the chain breaks at that junction and everything downstream is wrong or cannot run at all: if subtask 3 hands on a list instead of a single number, the VAT stage has nothing to multiply; if subtask 4 emits a percentage rate rather than an amount, subtask 5 adds 7.5 to the subtotal instead of 7.5 per cent of it and the total is silently wrong. Because the stages are chained rather than independent, a fault at any stage propagates to the end — which is also why a pipeline is tested stage by stage, checking each stage's output against the next stage's expected input.

**12.** *Concept: applying the three factors that gate the use of computational approaches.* **Factor one — is the problem clearly defined?** Partly. The current situation (ten years of GPS pings) and the end goal (traffic at every junction, one week ahead) are stated, but "exact traffic" is not a defined quantity — vehicles per hour? queue length? — and no obstacles have been identified, so how the end goal is to be reached is unstated. This factor is weak but repairable by writing the goal down properly. **Factor two — is the problem computable?** This is where the proposal fails first, and fails hardest. The factor asks whether the **required calculations are possible, considering processing times and capacity**. Predicting the **exact** state of every junction in a city a week ahead is not a calculation that can be completed to that standard by any amount of processing: traffic depends on weather, incidents and human choices not present in the data, so no run time and no capacity delivers the accuracy demanded. The demand is impossible in kind, not merely expensive. **Factor three — have the data requirements been examined?** They have not. Every GPS ping from every vehicle over ten years is a **big data** volume whose **data types and storage capacity** have not been identified, and the proposal names no plan for storing or moving it. **The change that would let it pass:** replace "the exact traffic one week ahead at every junction" with a bounded, computable target — for example, **predict the average congestion band (light / moderate / heavy) at the fifty busiest junctions for each hour of the next day**, using pings from the last two years sampled to a stated resolution. That makes the goal definite, brings the calculation inside realistic processing times and capacity, and lets the data types and storage be specified — after which the **data mining** techniques the course names, machine learning and statistical analysis, are exactly the right instrument for the retained data.

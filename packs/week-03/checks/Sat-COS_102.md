d) Split into `[2,8]` and `[4,9,3]`, split again to single elements, merge each half into `[8,2]` and `[9,4,3]`, then place the sorted halves one after the other to give `[8,2,9,4,3]`

## Answers

**1. a** — *Merge sort as divide and conquer: divide to single elements, then combine by merging.* Divide and conquer splits a complex problem into **smaller, easier parts of the same kind**, solves them, and **combines** the solutions. `[2,8,4,9,3]` splits into `[2,8]` and `[4,9,3]`; those split into `[2] [8]` and `[4]`, `[9] [3]`; a single element is already sorted, which is the base case. Merging for **descending** order means repeatedly taking the **larger** of the two front elements: `[2]+[8] → [8,2]`; `[9]+[3] → [9,3]`; `[4]+[9,3] → [9,4,3]`; finally `[8,2]+[9,4,3] → 9, 8, 4, 3, 2`. (b), (c) and (d) are the reason this question is not answerable by picking the familiar final list: three of the four options mention it. (b) is the widespread belief that merge sort has a fixed direction and descending order is obtained by reversing afterwards — the comparison inside the merge is what sets the order, and reversing is an extra pass merge sort does not perform. (c) skips the recursion: "sort each half by inspection" is not merge sort, and a five-element list is only small enough for inspection by accident — the algorithm is defined by dividing until each part holds one element. (d) does everything right and then **concatenates** instead of merging; concatenation is what makes the split worthless, because the combining step is the only place the two halves are ever compared with each other.

**2. b** — *Greedy: the best possible solution applied at each stage, where "best" is the choice that leaves the most room for later ones.* The activities are (2,3), (4,5), (1,7), (6,8), (9,10), (6,10). Take the compatible activity finishing earliest each time: (2,3) finishes at 3; (4,5) starts at 4 ≥ 3, take it; (1,7) starts at 1, which clashes, skip; (6,8) starts at 6 ≥ 5, take it; (9,10) starts at 9 ≥ 8, take it; (6,10) starts at 6 < 8, skip. **Four activities.** (a) is the answer produced by the most natural wrong greedy rule — take whatever starts earliest — which grabs the long (1,7) first and blocks everything until 9, ending with two; it shows that greedy is only as good as the quantity it is greedy about. (c) is the same trap one step later: after (4,5) the candidate finishing at 10 is taken instead of the one finishing at 8, and that single choice destroys the (9,10) slot, giving three. (d) is the "mutual clash" error — (6,8) and (6,10) conflict with each other, but the algorithm never has to choose between them as a pair; it takes the earlier finisher and simply passes over the other.

**3. d** — *Brute force is simple and consistent but very slow, and the computability factor is where that bites.* The course states brute force "relies solely on computing power and works by trying every possibility instead of improving efficiency with advanced techniques", and that such algorithms are **simple and consistent, but very slow**. Fifteen stops give 15! orderings — 1,307,674,368,000 of them — which is precisely what the second precondition tests: **is the problem computable, considering processing times and capacity?** It is not, so a computational solution must come from elsewhere, and the week's case study names greedy and divide and conquer for exactly this delivery-route problem. (a) is the strongest distractor because everything in it is true except its conclusion: brute force **is** consistent and it **would** find the shortest route — the objection is not correctness but that the calculation cannot be performed, which is why the three factors are weighed before choosing a method rather than after. (b) mislabels the approach and then leans on dynamic programming's guarantee: saving partial routes is real technique, but the guarantee is conditional ("provided that a solution exists, an optimal solution can be found") and says nothing about the time taken; more importantly, enumerating every ordering is not made into dynamic programming by storing results. (c) imports last week's vocabulary: an undecidable problem is one for which **no algorithm returns an accurate true/false for every input**, whereas here an algorithm plainly exists — it is merely too slow.

**4. c** — *Greedy's caveat, stated by the course and demonstrated.* "Nearest undelivered address" is the greedy rule applied to routing: **the best possible solution is applied at each stage**, and by always selecting the best optimal path it **aims** at the optimal solution. The long final leg is not a defect in the implementation but the documented behaviour: greedy **might not always produce an optimal solution**, although it produces one for many problems, and where it does the solution is **simple and highly efficient** — which is why it remains the right tool for a dispatcher who must decide in seconds. (a) is the error the whole week is built to prevent: it treats "selects the best optimal path" as a guarantee about the finished route, when it is only a statement about each individual step; the guarantee, hedged as it is, belongs to dynamic programming. (b) misreads the situation as an incomplete backtracking algorithm — backtracking is defined by reaching a **final state**, testing whether it is accepted or rejected, and returning to a previous state to try **unconsidered options**, none of which the dispatcher does or could do while the driver is moving. (d) transplants divide and conquer's effectiveness condition onto a problem that was never divided: the legs are not sub-problems whose solutions get combined, they are the successive choices of a single greedy pass.

**5.** *Concept: the three preconditions for a computational approach, and pipelining as the shape of the decomposition.* **(i) The problem must be clearly defined** — state the **current situation** (drivers currently choose their own order, average delivery 40 minutes, fuel spend known), the **end goal** (minimum total delivery time and fuel cost with every order delivered), **how the end goal is reached** (compute an order of drops before the driver leaves, and re-compute when a new order arrives), and the **possible obstacles** (traffic that changes during the run, orders arriving mid-route, a fixed number of drivers, food that spoils past a deadline). **(ii) The problem must be computable** — can the required calculations be done **within the processing times and capacity available**? A route must be produced in seconds on a phone or a modest server, which rules out enumerating all orderings and points to an approach that trades guaranteed optimality for speed. **(iii) The data requirements must be examined** — identify the **data types** (addresses and coordinates, pairwise travel times, time windows, vehicle capacity, live traffic) and the **storage capacity** for the distance matrix and the order history. **A pipeline for the task** — each subtask executed by a different process, the result of one feeding the next: **(1) ingest and clean the orders** → hands out a validated list of drops with coordinates and deadlines; **(2) build the distance/time matrix** for every pair of drops → hands out the travel cost between any two points; **(3) cluster the drops into per-driver zones** → hands each driver a small set of drops; **(4) order the drops within each zone** → hands out a sequence; **(5) monitor and re-plan** as traffic and new orders arrive → feeds updated drops back into stage 2. **Divide and conquer does its work at stage 3**: the one large routing problem is split into smaller, easier per-driver problems, each genuinely simpler than the whole and combined at the end simply by dispatching each driver — the cheap combining step that the technique's condition demands. **Greedy does its work at stage 4**: within a zone, take the best available next drop at each stage (nearest, or nearest that still meets its deadline), which is fast, simple and good enough even though it might not return the optimal ordering.

**6.** *Concept: dynamic programming is decomposition plus stored sub-solutions, and it pays only where sub-problems repeat.* Both algorithms break a problem into sub-problems, so the difference cannot lie there. Plain recursive Fibonacci breaks `fib(n)` into `fib(n-1)` and `fib(n-2)` — but those two sub-problems **overlap**, and the same value is recomputed again and again: computing `fib(6)` this way makes 25 calls, evaluating `fib(3)` three times and `fib(2)` five times, and the waste grows explosively with `n`. Dynamic programming breaks the problem into sub-problems and **saves the solutions of the sub-problems so that there is no need to compute the result again**, so the table converts those 25 calls into 7 stored entries, one per value from `fib(0)` to `fib(6)` — that is the whole of the speed-up. Merge sort is **divide and conquer**: it splits the list into **disjoint** halves, and no sub-list is ever requested twice, because sorting the left half and sorting the right half have nothing in common. A table of stored results is only ever written, never usefully read, so it costs memory and returns nothing. **What dynamic programming guarantees:** provided that a solution to the problem exists, **an optimal solution can be found** — which is why it is used mostly for **optimization** problems, where the task is to find a minimum or a maximum. **What divide and conquer requires:** that the **smaller, easier parts really are simpler than the complex whole**, and that **combining their solutions is less complicated than solving the original problem directly** — merge sort satisfies both, since a one-element list is trivially sorted and merging two sorted lists is a single linear pass. The lesson to carry: saving results is not a general accelerator, it is the specific remedy for **repeated** sub-problems.

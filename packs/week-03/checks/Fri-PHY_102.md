**1.** A cell of emf 6.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected across a 2.5 Ω resistor. What is the terminal potential difference of the cell?
A. 6.0 V
B. 4.8 V
C. 5.0 V
D. 1.0 V

**2.** In an experiment the terminal p.d. of a cell is measured at two currents: V = 4.6 V when I = 0.40 A, and V = 3.6 V when I = 1.4 A. What are the emf and internal resistance of the cell?
A. ε = 5.0 V, r = 1.0 Ω
B. ε = 4.6 V, r = 1.0 Ω
C. ε = 5.0 V, r = 2.6 Ω
D. ε = 3.6 V, r = −1.0 Ω

**3.** A rod 0.40 m long slides on rails at 4.0 m/s across a 0.50 T field perpendicular to the plane of the rails. The circuit resistance is 0.80 Ω. What force is needed to keep the rod moving at constant speed?
A. 0.80 N
B. 0.13 N
C. 2.0 N
D. 0.20 N

**4.** A 50-turn coil of area 2.0 × 10⁻³ m² lies with its plane perpendicular to a magnetic field. The field rises steadily from 0.10 T to 0.50 T in 0.20 s. What is the magnitude of the induced emf?
A. 0.25 V
B. 0.20 V
C. 4.0 × 10⁻³ V
D. 0.040 V

**5.** A parallel-plate capacitor in vacuum has plate area 0.020 m² and separation 0.50 mm. It is connected to a 100 V supply. Take ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m. What charge does it hold?
A. 3.54 × 10⁻⁸ C
B. 3.54 × 10⁻¹¹ C
C. 1.77 × 10⁻⁸ C
D. 3.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ C

**6.** A 20 pF capacitor is charged to 12 V and then disconnected from the supply. A dielectric of constant K = 3 is slid in to fill the gap. What are the new potential difference and stored energy?
A. V = 4.0 V, U = 1.44 × 10⁻⁹ J
B. V = 36 V, U = 1.30 × 10⁻⁸ J
C. V = 4.0 V, U = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ J
D. V = 12 V, U = 4.3 × 10⁻⁹ J

**7.** Between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor in vacuum the field is 1.0 × 10³ N/C. The plates have area 0.040 m² and are 2.0 mm apart. Take ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m. What total energy is stored in the field between the plates?
A. 3.5 × 10⁻⁷ J
B. 4.4 × 10⁻⁶ J
C. 7.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ J
D. 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ J

**8.** The north pole of a bar magnet is pushed toward a coil, so the flux through the coil increases. What does the minus sign in ε = −N dΦ/dt tell you about the current induced in the coil?
A. It is negative in value and so flows backwards through the meter
B. It flows so that its own magnetic field opposes the increase in flux
C. It makes the flux through the coil decrease to zero
D. It flows in the direction the magnet is moving

**9. (show your working)** A defibrillator capacitor of 200 μF is charged to 2.0 kV and then discharged through a patient in 4.0 ms. Calculate the energy delivered and the average power during the discharge. Show your working.

**10. (show your working)** A rod of length l moves at speed v perpendicular to a uniform field B. Starting from the magnetic force F = qvB on a free charge in the rod, derive ε = Bvl. Then, for B = 0.30 T, l = 0.50 m and v = 2.0 m/s, find the emf and check it by the rate of change of the flux Blx enclosed after the rod has moved for 2.0 s. Show your working.

**11. (show your working)** Two identical 10 μF capacitors are to store energy from a 12 V supply. Calculate the total energy stored when they are connected across the supply in series, and when in parallel, and state which arrangement stores more and by what factor. Show your working.

**12. (show your working)** A battery moves a total charge Q onto a capacitor, leaving a final potential difference V across it. Explain why the energy stored is ½QV and not QV, even though every coulomb ends up at the potential difference V.

## Answers

**1. C** — *Current and terminal p.d. from ε = I(R + r).* Round the loop ε = I(R + r), so I = 6.0/(2.5 + 0.50) = 6.0/3.0 = 2.0 A. Then V = ε − Ir = 6.0 − 2.0 × 0.50 = 5.0 V. Check: V = IR = 2.0 × 2.5 = 5.0 V.

6.0 V ignores the internal resistance; 4.8 V uses I = ε/R = 2.4 A, leaving r out of the loop; 1.0 V is the lost volts Ir, not the terminal p.d.

**2. A** — *Emf and internal resistance from a V-I graph.* V = ε − rI is a straight line with gradient −r and intercept ε. Gradient = (3.6 − 4.6)/(1.4 − 0.40) = −1.0/1.0 = −1.0 V/A, so r = 1.0 Ω. Intercept: ε = V + Ir = 4.6 + 0.40 × 1.0 = 5.0 V. Check with the other point: 3.6 + 1.4 × 1.0 = 5.0 V.

ε = 4.6 V takes the first reading as the emf instead of extrapolating to I = 0; r = 2.6 Ω is V/I at the second point, the external resistance, not r; the last option reads the negative gradient as a negative resistance and a reading as ε.

**3. D** — *Motional emf, induced current and the retarding force.* ε = Bvl = 0.50 × 4.0 × 0.40 = 0.80 V. I = ε/R = 0.80/0.80 = 1.0 A. The field pushes on that current with F = BIl = 0.50 × 1.0 × 0.40 = 0.20 N, opposing the motion, so an equal applied force of 0.20 N keeps the speed constant. Check: Fv = 0.80 W = I²R = (1.0)² × 0.80.

0.80 N confuses the power εI (0.80 W) with the force; 0.13 N uses I = εR instead of ε/R; 2.0 N uses the speed in place of the length in F = BIl.

**4. B** — *Faraday's law with flux from B and A.* The flux per turn is Φ = BA, so the change is ΔΦ = (0.50 − 0.10) × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. |ε| = N ΔΦ/Δt = 50 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁴/0.20 = 0.20 V.

0.25 V uses the final field 0.50 T instead of the change 0.40 T; 4.0 × 10⁻³ V forgets the 50 turns; 0.040 V is N ΔΦ without dividing by the time.

**5. A** — *Charge on a parallel-plate capacitor from its geometry.* C = ε₀A/d = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.020/0.50 × 10⁻³ = 3.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ F. Then Q = CV = 3.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 100 = 3.54 × 10⁻⁸ C.

3.54 × 10⁻¹¹ C leaves the separation in millimetres; 1.77 × 10⁻⁸ C halves Q as if it were the energy formula; 3.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ C is the capacitance in farads, quoted as if it were the charge.

**6. C** — *Inserting a dielectric at constant charge.* With the supply disconnected the charge is fixed: Q = C₀V₀ = 20 × 10⁻¹² × 12 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ C. The capacitance becomes C = KC₀ = 60 pF, so V = Q/C = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁰/60 × 10⁻¹² = 4.0 V, that is V₀/K. Energy: U = ½QV = ½ × 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 4.0 = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ J, down from 1.44 × 10⁻⁹ J by the factor K, because the field does work pulling the dielectric in.

The first option keeps the old energy as if it were conserved; V = 36 V multiplies V by K instead of dividing; V = 12 V is the case with the supply still connected, where V stays fixed and U rises to 4.3 × 10⁻⁹ J.

**7. D** — *Energy from energy density times volume.* Energy density u = ½ε₀E² = ½ × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × (1.0 × 10³)² = 4.43 × 10⁻⁶ J/m³. Volume between the plates = Ad = 0.040 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ m³. U = u × Ad = 4.43 × 10⁻⁶ × 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ J. Check: C = ε₀A/d = 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ F, V = Ed = 2.0 V, ½CV² = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ J.

3.5 × 10⁻⁷ J leaves d in millimetres when finding the volume; 4.4 × 10⁻⁶ J reports the energy density as if it were an energy; 7.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ J leaves out the ½.

**8. B** — *Meaning of the minus sign in Faraday's law.* The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced emf drives a current whose field opposes the change that produced it. Here the flux is increasing, so the coil's own field points against the magnet's field and the coil face repels the approaching north pole. The sign fixes direction only; the magnitude is N dΦ/dt.

A current is not 'negative in value' in any absolute sense; the induced field only opposes the change, it cannot cancel the flux; and the current circulates in the coil, it does not travel along the magnet's line of motion.

**9.** *Capacitor energy and the power of a fast discharge.* U = ½CV² = ½ × 200 × 10⁻⁶ × (2.0 × 10³)² = ½ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 4.0 × 10⁶ = 400 J. Average power P = U/t = 400/4.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.0 × 10⁵ W. The battery fills the capacitor slowly at modest power; the release in milliseconds gives a power far above what the battery could supply directly.

Final answer: U = 400 J (accept 4.0 × 10² J), P = 1.0 × 10⁵ W (accept 100 kW). 800 J leaves out the ½; 0.20 J uses V instead of V²; 1.6 W multiplies by the time instead of dividing.

**10.** *Deriving motional emf from the Lorentz force.* A charge q carried along with the rod feels F = qvB directed along the rod. Moving it from one end to the other, a distance l, does work W = Fl = qvBl. Emf is work per unit charge, so ε = W/q = Bvl. Numerically ε = 0.30 × 2.0 × 0.50 = 0.30 V. Check: after 2.0 s the rod has moved x = vt = 4.0 m, enclosing flux Φ = Blx = 0.30 × 0.50 × 4.0 = 0.60 Wb; the flux grows at Φ/t = 0.60/2.0 = 0.30 Wb/s = 0.30 V, the same emf.

A correct answer shows W = qvBl, divides by q, gives ε = 0.30 V, and shows the flux check giving 0.30 V (accept 0.30 Wb/s or dΦ/dt = Blv = 0.30 V). Leaving q in the final expression, or giving 0.60 V by reporting the flux as the emf, is wrong.

**11.** *Stored energy for series versus parallel capacitors.* Parallel: C = 10 + 10 = 20 μF, U = ½CV² = ½ × 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = 1.44 × 10⁻³ J. Series: 1/C = 1/10 + 1/10, so C = 5.0 μF, U = ½ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ J. Parallel stores more, by a factor 1.44 × 10⁻³/3.6 × 10⁻⁴ = 4, because at a fixed supply voltage U = ½CV² grows with C and the parallel combination has the larger capacitance.

Final answer: parallel 1.44 × 10⁻³ J, series 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ J, parallel is larger by a factor of 4 (accept 1.4 mJ and 0.36 mJ). Giving 20 μF for the series combination, or the same energy for both, is wrong.

**12.** *Origin of the factor ½ in capacitor energy.* The p.d. across the capacitor is not V throughout the charging. It starts at zero and rises in proportion to the charge already there, v = q/C, reaching V only when the last of the charge arrives. Each small amount of charge dq is moved through the p.d. present at that moment, so the total work is the area under the straight-line graph of v against q: a triangle of area ½QV. Equivalently, the average p.d. during charging is ½V. QV would be the work if the whole charge were moved through the full p.d. V, which is what a battery holding a constant V supplies; the other half is lost as heat in the wires.

A correct answer says the p.d. grows from 0 to V as charge is added, that the work is the area under the v-q line (a triangle) or uses the average p.d. ½V, and concludes ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/2C.

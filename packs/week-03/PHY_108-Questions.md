## Short answer (3)

**13.** Explain, as a chain of cause and effect, why the I–V graph for the Tungsten (or Iron) resistor in this experiment is curved rather than a straight line through the origin.

**14.** A student's P vs I² graph passes through the points (I² = 0.04 A², P = 2.0 W) and (I² = 0.16 A², P = 6.8 W). Calculate the gradient of the graph, state what physical quantity it represents, and give its unit.

**15.** List and briefly justify three precautions a student should observe when running this experiment, based on the procedure and the two graphs it produces.

## Answers

**1. B** — *aim*: the experiment studies the nonlinear current–voltage relation in a non-ohmic resistor, not a linear (ohmic) one.
**2. B** — *apparatus*: voltmeter, ammeter, DC power supply, plus Tungsten and Iron resistor samples selectable from a drop-down list.
**3. B** — *Joule's law*: P = I²R — heat dissipated scales with the square of the current, not with I·R (that combination is just V·I) or I/R.
**4. A** — *units*: power in watts (W), current in amperes (A), resistance in ohms (Ω); P = I²R is dimensionally consistent only with this set.
**5. C** — *why nonlinear*: self-heating (P = I²R) raises the conductor's temperature, and temperature changes its resistance, so R is no longer constant across the run.
**6. B** — *Graph 1*: I vs V is a curve for a non-ohmic resistor — this curvature is the experiment's direct evidence of non-ohmic behaviour.
**7. B** — *Graph 2*: comparing P = R·I² with y = mx + c gives y ≡ P, x ≡ I², m ≡ R, c = 0 — a straight line through the origin with gradient R.
**8. C** — *procedure range*: readings are taken from about 0.1 V up to 50 V, using the three push buttons above the voltage-dial knob.
**9. B** — *setup step*: the material (Tungsten or Iron) is chosen from the drop-down list before recording; this is a virtual-lab setup step, not a circuit correction.
**10. B** — *end of run*: the run ends automatically once all data are recorded, and the readings are exported as a downloadable Excel sheet.
**11. B** — *mid-lesson example*: thermistors are non-ohmic resistors deliberately used in temperature-sensing circuits because their resistance changes sharply and predictably with temperature.
**12. B** — *why Tungsten*: its very high melting point lets it withstand the large temperature rise from self-heating, and its strongly temperature-dependent resistivity is exactly the non-ohmic behaviour exploited in lamps and heating elements.
**13.** *Cause-and-effect chain*: as the applied voltage increases, the current I through the resistor increases; the power dissipated as heat is P = I²R, so heating grows with the square of the current; this heat raises the conductor's temperature; the resistance of a metal like Tungsten or Iron changes (rises) with temperature; so R is not constant across the run, meaning V is no longer simply proportional to I — the I–V graph curves instead of forming a straight line through the origin.
**14. Gradient = 40 ohm, representing R (resistance), unit ohm (ohm)** — *calculation*: gradient = (6.8 - 2.0) W / (0.16 - 0.04) A^2 = 4.8/0.12 = 40. Since P = R x I^2 is of the form y = mx, the gradient m equals R, so R = 40 ohm.
**15.** *Precautions* (any three): select the correct material from the drop-down before recording, since Tungsten and Iron have different resistivities and switching mid-run mixes two different data sets; take enough readings spread across the full 0.1 V-50 V range rather than bunching them at one end, so the curve on the I-V graph and the line on the P-I^2 graph are both well defined; compute I^2 and P = IV from the same recorded row (matching V and I values), since pairing mismatched rows corrupts both graphs; and read the axes carefully before quoting a gradient, since only the P vs I^2 graph gives R directly - the raw I vs V graph is curved and has no single constant gradient.

## Strand 1 — The Portuguese incursion into the Niger Area (15th century)

**The event.** **Portuguese sailors began exploring the West African coast in the early 15th century**, motivated by **the desire for new markets**. In doing so they **significantly improved seafaring techniques and ship designs** — the **caravel** is the named vessel. **Portugal dominated world trade for nearly 200 years**, on the strength of these technological advances and of access to valuable commodities: **fabrics, spices and gold**.

**Impact on the Niger Area — the four named consequences (memorise as a list):**
1. **Opening up the area to the rest of the world, which eventually led to colonialism and exploitation.**
2. **Leading to a series of wars, in order to be able to continue the supply of slaves.**
3. **Contributing to the abandonment of African cultures.**
4. **Playing a major role in the trans-Atlantic slave trade.**

**Trade relations along the Nigerian coast** — two named local effects: the growth of **brass casting in Benin**, and the emergence of **trading in the Lagos Lagoon**.

**Link to the slave trade.** The Portuguese were **the first European power to establish a regular trade in slaves from West Africa to the Americas**, and were **responsible for developing the triangular trade route connecting Europe, Africa and the Americas**. Over **15 million Africans** were sold as slaves and transported to the Americas. The trade also shaped **European and American economies** and **contributed to the rise of racism and white supremacy**.

**Case study title:** *Portuguese Exploration and the Transformation of West Africa* — motivations, technological advancements (the caravel), the effects of the incursion, and the transition **from the transatlantic slave trade to the era of colonialism**. Key reference throughout: **Walter Rodney, *West Africa and the Atlantic Slave-Trade* (1967)**.

**The week's spine (learn this order):** **Portuguese incursion, 15th c.** → **trans-Atlantic slave trade** (Portuguese → Dutch 17th c. → British 18th c.) → **abolition, 1792/1833** → **'legitimate' trade** (palm oil, ivory) → **annexation of Lagos, 1861** → **company rule** (NAC → RNC, 1886) → **formal colonial occupation** after the charter's revocation.

## Lesson 1 — The Slave Trade

**Definitions.** *Slaves*: persons bought, stolen, captured in wars and pawned, expected to render services to a master who exercises full power over them, even their life and death. *Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade*: the inhuman buying and selling of humans from Africa across the Atlantic to the Americas and Europe to serve as slaves on the plantations and in the homes of their masters.

**Named characterisations**
- **Phillip Curtin** — "the largest intercontinental migration in western History, spanning a period of 300 years in which the Africans in mass numbers crossed the Atlantic."
- **Triangular trade** — **Africa (source); Americas (destination); Europeans (organisers)**. The sea leg is the **Atlantic or Middle Passage**.

**Origin and division of the world**
- The trade began with the **Portuguese in the 15th century**, after they developed maritime capabilities; on landing in Africa they began seizing people to work in **Portugal and Spain**.
- The **Pope** divided the Old World into two halves: **Africa ceded to Portugal; the Americas ceded to Spain**.
- **Christopher Columbus** discovered the Americas; once **sugar plantations** were established, European traders realised they could make enormous profits exploiting African labour.
- Africans were taken to **North, Central and South America and the Caribbean** for **gold and silver mines** and plantations growing **cotton, sugar and tobacco**.

**The Asiento** — the **Spanish** system granting *the right to trade in slaves in the New World*.
- **Before 1600 the Portuguese were the chief slave traders in West Africa**, taking Africans to **Brazil** or selling them to Spanish settlers in the Americas.
- The Asiento **later became transferable**, other Europeans (including the Portuguese) obtaining the licence.
- As the slave population swelled they could mobilise and rebel → **the uprising in Puerto Rico in 1527**.

**Sequence of dominant slaving nations (memorise the order)**
- **15th–16th c.**: the Portuguese.
- **17th century**: the **Dutch** joined the Portuguese as the leading slave-trading nation.
- **18th century**: the **British** were the biggest slave traders — British ships carried **more than half** of all slaves from Africa.
- **19th century**: European nations were no longer active; **Europeans who had themselves settled in the New World** organised a large part of the trade.

**How the trade was conducted**
- It began on the West African coast as **direct attacks by Europeans**; raiding later became unsafe as Africans defended themselves, so they turned to **trade**, since they needed commodities like gold — the Portuguese exchanging **manufactured goods** for slaves and African commodities.
- **Nigerian chiefs forced Europeans to pay a tax on each slave** at the trade ports.
- **Middle Passage: 5 to 8 weeks** in terrible conditions. Space allotted each slave: **6 ft × 1 ft × 4 inches**; a ship carried **between 451 and 600 slaves**. Diseases: **smallpox, dysentery and ophthalmia**.
- At first the victims were **coastal people**; later raiders travelled **into the interior**. By the **end of the 18th century** the stretch of the **Niger River** had been combed for slaves, who arrived at the coast exhausted.

**Impact — the named authorities**
- **Wole Soyinka**: Africa's underdevelopment is the outcome of "the twin evils of slavery and colonialism inflicted by the Western World." **Walter Rodney** shares this view.
- **Paul Lovejoy**: over **7 million died in transit**; and "many communities relocated as far from the slavers' route as possible… their technological and economic development was hindered as they devoted their energy to hiding and defending themselves."
- **Washington Alcott**: the transatlantic slave trade caused the forced removal of millions of Africans, "including a large percentage of skilled tradesmen and women from a range of occupations."
- **Claude Ake**: "it was mainly because the development of productive forces stagnated at a certain stage in African history that the colonisers were able to subordinate the continent."
- Listed implications: **depopulation, destruction of Africa's socio-economic and political structures, unbridled warfare, insecurity leading to underdevelopment.**

**The end of the trade**
- An abolition movement emerged in **Britain, the United States and parts of Europe**.
- The economic motive: rapid economic and technological development **in the metropole** removed the need for slave labour — Europeans now needed Africans **to stay in Africa** to grow **cash crops** for European industries and to be a **ready market for finished goods**.
- **From 1792** the British began **seizing slave ships** and repatriating them to **Sierra Leone**.
- **1833** — a bill to abolish slavery sponsored by **Thomas Fowell Buxton**, a British parliamentarian. Its significance: the existing strategy had not achieved much success, so he advocated that **for the slave trade to stop, it must stop from Africa**. The implication of this was the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**.
- Conclusion: the end of the trade **ushered in a new form of exploitation known as colonialism**; it also psychologically made the black African the least regarded race in the world.

## Lesson 2 — 'Legitimate' Trade to Formal Occupation

**End of the slave trade / rise of legitimate trade**
- **By 1840** the **Bight of Benin** was the **last centre of the slave trade in West Africa**, and **Lagos was the leading slave port**.
- Trade with Europe in **palm oil, ivory and other "legitimate" commodities** was expanding.
- **1849 — Beecroft appointed** "for the purpose of control and supervision"; **the beginning of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria**.

**Annexation of Lagos — 1861 (the chain)**
1. Rulership feud between **Kosoko** and **Akitoye**.
2. **1851** — the British sign a **treaty with Kosoko** to end the slave trade and embrace "legitimate" trade.
3. **Kosoko's refusal** → the **consequent bombardment**.
4. **29 December 1851** — **Akitoye regained the seat of Lagos**; **Kosoko fled to Epe**.
5. **1861** — the **annexation of Lagos**.
- Immediately after the bombardment, more ships began coming to Lagos to buy **palm oil** and other produce.
- **1852 — a treaty** covering **the protection of British traders, the right to trade, and the erecting of storage facilities in Lagos**; in return **European merchants were to pay import and export duties**.

**Expeditions and company rule**
- **1857 — Macgregor Laird's expedition.** Three trading ports were established in the Delta as a result: **Ado, Onitsha and Gbebe**. Laird was **granted admiralty in the Delta**.
- **1858 — Laird forms a joint stock company, the Central African Company.**
- **1861 — Laird's death.**
- **1862 — the floating of the River Niger Navigation and Trading Company**; also **the Company of African Merchants**. A proliferation of companies followed, which was an advantage to locals.
- Big Delta-trade companies: **the West African Company, Alexander Miller Bros, Mr James Pinnock, the Central African Company of London**.

**Amalgamation → NAC → RNC**
- The companies' amalgamation created the **United African Company** under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**, later known as the **National African Company (NAC)** after **buying two rival French firms**.
- **By 1882** the company was powerful enough to control large areas of the **Niger Delta** but **not powerful enough to monopolise the trade**.
- **1884** — to support the NAC the British government instructed **Consul Hewett** to embark on a **treaty-making tour**; many chiefs placed themselves under **British protection** → **Sphere of Influence** and the **Berlin Conference**.
- Charter sequence: **applied 1881 → refused, on the grounds of lack of capacity**; **by 1882 capital raised to £1 million, but the presence of French firms made a British charter impossible**; **by 1886 the Royal Charter was granted and it became the Royal Niger Company (RNC)**.

**The Royal Niger Company**
- Operated as **both a trading company and an administrator**; **paid dividends solely from trade profits**.
- **By 1887 the RNC controlled all the territories in the basin of the Niger.**
- Strategy — build a **trade monopoly** in the Niger territories it controlled: it **bought competing companies, chased them with high taxes, monopolised the supply of imported goods, and manipulated the prices of African produce**.
- **Critics: Harry Johnston, Ajayi Crowther, Hoenigsberg.** The criticisms did little damage because the RNC had the favour of the **Colonial Secretary, Salisbury**.

**Oil River Protectorate (ORP)**
- **Established in 1884, after the Berlin Conference.**
- **7 districts: Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo.**
- A request for the **amalgamation of the ORP and the RNC** was made, and opposed.
- **Renamed the Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893.**

**Decline of the RNC**
- Causes: **alliances of opponents of the RNC**; the appointment of **Sir Ralph Moor as Consul of the ORP in 1891**; a **price war**; **smuggling**.
- **By 1895, two significant but unrelated events had combined to weaken it**: the appointment of **Joseph Chamberlain as Colonial Secretary**, and the **raid on Akassa by the Brass people**.
- The **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** was set up to look into the cause of the **Akassa raid**.
- Outcome: **revocation of the RNC's charter** — company rule gives way to formal colonial occupation.

## Commonly confused

- **1881 vs 1882 vs 1886** — 1881 the charter is *applied for and refused* (lack of capacity); 1882 capital reaches **£1 million** but French firms block it; **1886** the charter is actually **granted**.
- **1882 vs 1887** — 1882: controls large areas of the Delta but **cannot monopolise the trade**; 1887: **controls all the territories in the basin of the Niger**.
- **Kosoko vs Akitoye** — **Kosoko** refused the 1851 treaty and **fled to Epe**; **Akitoye** regained the seat on **29 Dec 1851**.
- **1851 vs 1852 vs 1861** — 1851: treaty with Kosoko, bombardment, Akitoye restored; **1852**: treaty on protection of British traders, right to trade, storage facilities, and duties; **1861**: annexation of Lagos (also the year of Laird's death).
- **Salisbury vs Joseph Chamberlain** — Salisbury, as Colonial Secretary, **shielded** the RNC from its critics; Chamberlain's appointment (by 1895) helped **weaken** it.
- **Beecroft (1849) vs Consul Hewett (1884) vs Sir Ralph Moor (1891)** — Beecroft = start of direct British influence; Hewett = treaty-making tour to support the NAC; Moor = Consul of the ORP, a cause of RNC decline.
- **Oil River Protectorate (1884) vs Niger Coast Protectorate (1893)** — the same body, renamed in 1893.
- **United African Company vs National African Company vs Royal Niger Company** — UAC (Goldie-Taubman's amalgamation) → NAC (after buying **two rival French firms**) → RNC (**1886** charter).
- **Central African Company (Laird, 1858) vs Central African Company of London** — the latter is listed among the big firms controlling the Delta trade.
- **1792 vs 1833** — 1792 the British begin **seizing slave ships** (repatriated to Sierra Leone); 1833 **Buxton's bill** to abolish slavery.
- **15 million vs 7 million** — over **15 million reached the Americas**; **Lovejoy**: over **7 million died in transit**.
- **Curtin vs Lovejoy vs Soyinka vs Claude Ake** — Curtin = "largest intercontinental migration… 300 years"; Lovejoy = deaths in transit and communities relocating; Soyinka (with Walter Rodney) = "twin evils of slavery and colonialism"; Ake = stagnation of productive forces let colonisers subordinate the continent.
- **Dutch (17th c.) vs British (18th c.)** — the Dutch joined the Portuguese as leading traders in the 17th century; the British were the biggest in the 18th, carrying more than half of all slaves.
- **1527 Puerto Rico uprising** belongs to the **Asiento** discussion (slave revolt risk), not to abolition.
- **Order of the four eras** — **Portuguese incursion (15th c.)** → **trans-Atlantic slave trade** → **'legitimate' trade / abolition (1792–1861)** → **company rule (1858–1899)**. Colonialism comes *after* the slave trade ends, not before it.
- **15th century vs 1840s** — the Portuguese *opened* the Niger area in the 15th century; the Bight of Benin was the *last* slaving centre by 1840. Opposite ends of the same story.
- **"First European power" claims** — the **Portuguese** were first to run a **regular slave trade** from West Africa to the Americas and built the **triangular route**; the **British** were merely the **biggest** traders, in the 18th century.
- **Nearly 200 years of Portuguese world-trade dominance** vs **Curtin's 300 years** of the trans-Atlantic trade — different spans, different claims.
- **Brass casting in Benin vs trading in the Lagos Lagoon** — both are effects of Portuguese coastal trade relations; Benin = a craft industry grew, Lagos Lagoon = trading emerged.
- **Caravel (Portuguese ship design, 15th c.)** vs **Laird's 1857 steam expedition** — both are "technology enabled penetration", four centuries apart.

# GST_112 — Week 3 Summary

*Nigerian Peoples and Culture · Week 3 (21–27 Sep 2026) · Topic: The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade, the Rise of "Legitimate" Trade, and the Road to Company Rule*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **The slave trade's origin and structure.** Portugal began the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade in the **15th century** after developing maritime capability. It ran as the **triangular trade**: **Africa** (source), the **Americas** (destination), **Europeans** (organisers). Philip **Curtin** called it "the largest intercontinental migration in western History," spanning **300 years**. The **Pope** divided the Old World: **Africa to Portugal, the Americas to Spain**. The **Asiento** was the Spanish licence granting the right to trade slaves in the New World; before 1600 the **Portuguese** were the chief slave traders, then it became transferable — a swollen slave population caused a rebellion in **Puerto Rico in 1527**.
2. **Who led the trade, by century.** **17th century — Dutch** (joined the Portuguese); **18th century — British** (carried more than half of all slaves out of Africa); **19th century** — European states no longer directly active, but Europeans **settled in the New World** organised much of the remaining trade.
3. **The numbers and the human cost.** An estimated **over 15 million** Africans reached the Americas; **Paul Lovejoy** notes a further **over 7 million died in transit**. The Middle Passage took **5–8 weeks**; each enslaved person was allotted **6ft × 1ft × 4 inches**, so a single ship carried **451–600** people, who suffered **smallpox, dysentery, and ophthalmia**.
4. **The end of the trade and its stated cause-and-effect.** The British began **seizing slave ships from 1792**, repatriating captives to **Sierra Leone**. In **1833**, British parliamentarian **Thomas Fowell Buxton** sponsored an abolition bill arguing the trade had to be stopped **"from Africa"** itself — the source names this reasoning as the direct chain that led to the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**.
5. **The annexation of Lagos (1861), told through the Kosoko–Akitoye feud.** **1851**: Britain signed a treaty with **Kosoko** to end the slave trade for "legitimate" trade; Kosoko refused, so the British **bombarded** Lagos. On **29 December 1851**, **Akitoye** regained the seat of Lagos and **Kosoko fled to Epe**. In **1852** a further treaty secured protection and trading rights for British merchants (plus storage facilities) in exchange for **import/export duties**. Britain formally **annexed Lagos in 1861**. **Beecroft** was appointed **1849** for control/supervision — the start of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria.
6. **From trading company to chartered monopoly: the name chain.** **Macgregor Laird's 1857 expedition** opened three Delta trading ports — **Ado, Onitsha, Gbebe** — and led to the **Central African Company (1858)**; Laird died in **1861**. The **River Niger Navigation and Trading Company** floated in **1862**. Amalgamation under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman** produced the **United African Company**, renamed the **National African Company (NAC)** after buying two rival French firms. NAC's charter application was **refused in 1881** (lack of capacity); by **1882** its capital reached **£1 million**, but French firms still blocked a British charter; NAC finally got its **Royal Charter in 1886** and became the **Royal Niger Company (RNC)**. By **1887**, RNC controlled the whole Niger basin.
7. **RNC's methods, critics, and protectors.** RNC ran as both trading company and administrator, paying dividends **only from trade profits**, and built its monopoly by buying out rivals, imposing high taxes on competitors, monopolising imported-goods supply, and manipulating produce prices. Critics included **Harry Johnston, Ajayi Crowther, and Hoenigsberg**, but RNC had the favour of Colonial Secretary **Salisbury**, so criticism did little damage. In **1884**, Consul **Hewett** ran a treaty-making tour that put many chiefs under British protection (feeding into the **Berlin Conference** and "spheres of influence").
8. **The Oil River Protectorate and RNC's fall.** The **Oil River Protectorate (ORP)** was established in **1884** after the Berlin Conference, with **7 districts**: **Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo**. It was renamed the **Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893**. RNC declined through opponent alliances, **Sir Ralph Moor's appointment as ORP Consul (1891)**, a price war, and smuggling; by **1895**, **Joseph Chamberlain's appointment as Colonial Secretary** and the **Brass people's raid on Akassa** combined to weaken it further. The **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** investigated the Akassa raid, and RNC's **Charter was revoked**.

## Framing

The two lectures share one throughline: European contact with the Niger area moved from **slave trade → "legitimate" commodity trade (palm oil, ivory) → chartered-company rule → colonial administration**. The Summary PDF adds that Portuguese incursion (15th century) also reshaped local economies directly — it fed the growth of **brass casting in Benin** and trading in the **Lagos Lagoon**. On the trade's impact, the source cites **Wole Soyinka** ("twin evils of slavery and colonialism"), **Walter Rodney** (shares Soyinka's view), **Paul Lovejoy** (communities relocated away from slave routes, stalling development), **Washington Alco** (loss of skilled tradesmen and women), and **Claude Ake** (stagnated productive forces let colonisers subordinate the continent).

## Commonly confused

- **Beecroft (1849) vs Consul Hewett (1884)** — Beecroft's appointment marks the *start* of direct British influence; Hewett's treaty-making tour, 35 years later, is what feeds the Berlin Conference "sphere of influence" claims. Different men, different decades — don't merge them.
- **Kosoko vs Akitoye** — **Kosoko** is the ruler who signed (then defied) the 1851 anti-slave-trade treaty and fled to Epe; **Akitoye** is the one Britain restored to the Lagos throne. The bombardment happened *because* Kosoko refused; Akitoye did not need bombarding.
- **NAC vs RNC — one company, changing names.** United African Company → National African Company (NAC, after buying French rivals) → Royal Niger Company (RNC, only after the 1886 charter). A "Royal Niger Company" reference before 1886 is wrong.
- **1881 vs 1882 vs 1886** — 1881: charter *refused*. 1882: capital raised to £1m, charter still blocked by French competition. 1886: charter *finally granted*. Three different years, three different states of the same application.
- **Oil River Protectorate vs Niger Coast Protectorate** — same territory. ORP is the 1884 name (7 districts: Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo); Niger Coast Protectorate is the 1893 rename. Don't invert the years.
- **Laird's 1857 ports vs ORP's 1884 districts** — Laird's expedition opened **Ado, Onitsha, Gbebe**; the ORP's seven districts are a separate, later list (**Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo**). Different lists, 27 years apart.
- **Ralph Moor vs Joseph Chamberlain vs Kirk** — Moor (1891) was appointed **Consul of the ORP**; Chamberlain (1895) was appointed **Colonial Secretary** in Britain; **Kirk** chaired the Commission of Enquiry into the Akassa raid. Three different roles in RNC's decline — don't swap their titles.
- **Trans-Saharan (Week 2) vs Trans-Atlantic (this week)** — this week's trade is the **Trans-Atlantic** slave trade (Africa–Americas–Europe, organised by Portugal then others). Don't reach for last week's trans-Saharan material (Kanem Bornu, the Hausa–Tuareg routes) here.
- **15 million reached the Americas vs 7 million died in transit** — these are Lovejoy's *two separate* figures, not alternatives: over 15 million landed; a further over 7 million perished during the crossing.

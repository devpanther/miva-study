## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. **The date chain.** 1792 (Britain begins seizing slave ships) → 1833 (**Thomas Fowell Buxton's** abolition bill) → 1840 (Bight of Benin the **last** slave-trade centre, **Lagos** the leading slave port) → 1849 (**Beecroft** appointed) → 1851 (treaty with **Kosoko**, refusal, bombardment; **29 December 1851** Akitoye restored) → 1852 (traders' treaty) → 1857 (**Laird's** expedition) → **1861 (annexation of Lagos)**.
2. **The company chain — one company, four names.** Central African Company (Laird, **1858**) → amalgamation into the **United African Company** under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman** → **National African Company** (after buying **two rival French firms**) → **Royal Niger Company (1886)**, charter **revoked** after 1895.

4. **The triangular trade**, per **Phillip Curtin** "the largest intercontinental migration in western History, spanning a period of **300 years**": **Africa = source, Americas = destination, Europeans = organisers.**
5. **The numbers**: over **15 million** Africans reached the Americas; **Paul Lovejoy** — over **7 million** died in transit. Middle passage **5 to 8 weeks**; space per slave **6ft × 1ft × 4 inches**; **451 to 600 slaves** per ship; diseases **smallpox, dysentery, ophthalmia**.
6. **The sequence of leading slave-trading nations**: **Portuguese** (15th c., chief traders before 1600) → **Dutch** join them as leading nation in the **17th century** → **British** biggest in the **18th century** (more than **half** of all slaves) → **19th century**, Europeans no longer active; **settlers in the New World** organised much of it.
7. **The Oil Rivers Protectorate, 1884, seven districts**: **Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo**. Renamed the **Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893**.
8. **Buxton's logic and its consequence**: for the slave trade to stop, **it must stop from Africa** — the implication of which was the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**. And the end of the trade "ushered in a new form of exploitation known as **colonialism**".

## Part 1 — The slave trade

**Definitions.** Slaves are persons **bought, stolen, captured in wars and pawned**, expected to render services to a master who exercises full power over them, **even their life and death**. The **Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade** is the inhuman buying and selling of humans from Africa across the Atlantic to the Americas and Europe to serve at the **plantations and homes** of their masters.

**Origin.** Began with the **Portuguese in the 15th century**, after they developed **maritime capabilities**. On landing they **seized people** and took them to work in **Portugal and Spain**. The **Pope** divided the Old World in two: **Africa ceded to Portugal, the Americas ceded to Spain**. After **Christopher Columbus** reached the Americas and **sugar plantations** were established, Europeans saw the profit in African labour. Africans were taken to **North America, Central America, South America and the Caribbean** for **gold and silver mines** and plantations growing **cotton, sugar and tobacco**.

**The Asiento** = the **Spanish** system granting the **right to trade in slaves in the New World**. **Before 1600 the Portuguese were the chief slave traders in West Africa**, taking Africans to **Brazil** or selling them to **Spanish settlers**. The Asiento later became **transferable**, other Europeans (including the Portuguese) obtaining the licence. As the slave population swelled it could rebel — hence the **uprising in Puerto Rico in 1527**.

**Method on the coast.** At first **direct attacks/raids by Europeans**; raiding became unsafe as Africans defended themselves, so Europeans **traded** instead, the **Portuguese exchanging manufactured goods** for slaves and African commodities. **Nigerian chiefs forced Europeans to pay a tax on each slave** at the trade ports. Victims were at first **coastal peoples**; by the **end of the 18th century** the stretch of the **Niger River** had been combed for slaves, who reached the coast exhausted from the march.

**Named opinions on impact — learn who said what.**
- **Wole Soyinka** — Africa's underdevelopment is the outcome of "the **twin evils of slavery and colonialism** inflicted by the Western World." **Walter Rodney** shares this view.
- **Paul Lovejoy** — over 7 million died in transit; also that "many communities **relocated as far from the slavers' route as possible**", hindering their technological and economic development as they devoted energy to **hiding and defending themselves**.
- **Washington Alco** — the trade forced the removal of millions including a **large percentage of skilled tradesmen and women**.
- **Claude Ake** — "it was mainly because the **development of productive forces stagnated** at a certain stage in African history that the colonisers were able to subordinate the continent."
- Listed implications: **depopulation, destruction of Africa's socio-economic and political structures, unbridled warfare, insecurity leading to underdevelopment.**

**Why it ended.** An abolition movement arose in **Britain, the United States and parts of Europe**; but the economic reason given is **rapid economic and technological development in the metropole** — Europe now needed Africans **to stay in Africa** to grow **cash crops** for its industries and to be a **ready market for finished goods**. Britain seized slave ships **from 1792**, repatriating them to **Sierra Leone**. **1833**: abolition bill sponsored by **Thomas Fowell Buxton**, a **British parliamentarian**, remarkable for changing strategy — abolition **must start from Africa**.

## Part 2 — 'Legitimate' trade to formal occupation

**The end of the slave trade and the rise of legitimate trade.** By **1840** the **Bight of Benin** was the **last centre of the slave trade in West Africa** and **Lagos the leading slave port**. Trade with Europe in **palm oil, ivory and other 'legitimate' commodities** was expanding. **Beecroft** was appointed in **1849** for **control and supervision** — the **beginning of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria**.

**The annexation of Lagos, 1861 (chronology).**
- Rulership feud between **Kosoko** and **Akitoye**.
- **1851** — Britain signed a treaty with **Kosoko** to end the slave trade and embrace legitimate trade.
- **Kosoko's refusal** → the **bombardment** of Lagos.
- **29 December 1851** — **Akitoye regained the seat of Lagos**; **Kosoko fled to Epe**.
- After the bombardment, more ships came to Lagos for **palm oil** and other produce.
- **1852** — treaty covering **protection of British traders, the right to trade, and the erection of storage facilities** in Lagos; in return **European merchants were to pay import and export duties**.
- **1861** — **annexation of Lagos**.

**Expeditions and the birth of company rule.**
- **Macgregor Laird's expedition, 1857** — established **three trading ports in the Delta: Ado, Onitsha and Gbebe**. Laird was **granted admiralty in the Delta**.
- **1858** — Laird formed a **joint stock company, the Central African Company**. **Laird died in 1861**.
- **1862** — floating of the **River Niger Navigation and Trading Company**; also the **Company of African Merchants**. Proliferation of companies, which was an **advantage to locals**.
- Big firms taking charge of the **Delta trade**: the **West African Company, Alexander Miller Bros, Mr James Pinnock, and the Central African Company of London**.
- Amalgamation created the **United African Company** under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**, later the **National African Company (NAC)** after **buying two rival French firms**.
- **By 1882** the company controlled **large areas of the Niger Delta** but was **not powerful enough to monopolise the trade**.
- **1884** — to support the NAC the British government instructed **Consul Hewett** to make a **treaty-making tour**; many chiefs placed themselves **under British protection** — the basis of a **sphere of influence** claim at the **Berlin Conference**.

**The Royal Charter and the Royal Niger Company.** NAC **applied in 1881** — refused for **lack of capacity**. **By 1882** it had raised capital to **£1 million**, but the presence of **French firms** made a British charter impossible. **By 1886** it was granted a **Royal Charter** and became the **Royal Niger Company (RNC)**.

- The RNC operated **both as a trading company and as an administrator**, and paid **dividends solely from trade profits**.
- **By 1887 the RNC controlled all the territories in the basin of the Niger.**
- **Monopoly strategy**: **bought competing companies, chased them with high taxes, monopolised the supply of imported goods, manipulated the prices of African produce.**
- **Critics: Harry Johnston, Ajayi Crowther, Hoenigsberg** — but the RNC had the favour of the **Colonial Secretary, Salisbury**, so the criticism did little damage.

**The Oil Rivers Protectorate (ORP).** Established **1884, after the Berlin Conference**. **Seven districts: Bonny, Forcados, Old Calabar, New Calabar, Benin, Brass, Opobo.** A request to **amalgamate the ORP with the RNC** was made and **opposed**. **Renamed the Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893.**

**Decline of the RNC.** Causes: **alliances of the RNC's opponents; the appointment of Sir Ralph Moor as Consul of the ORP in 1891; a price war; smuggling.** **By 1895** two significant but **unrelated** events combined to weaken it: the appointment of **Joseph Chamberlain as Colonial Secretary**, and the **raid on Akassa by the Brass people**. The **Kirk Commission of Enquiry** was set up into the cause of the **Akassa raid**. Outcome: **revocation of the RNC's charter** — company rule gives way to formal colonial administration.

## Part 3 — The Portuguese incursion (from the Week 3 Summary PDF)

- **Portuguese sailors began exploring the West African coast in the early 15th century**, motivated by the **desire for new markets**, and greatly improved **seafaring techniques and ship designs** (the **caravel**).
- **Portugal dominated world trade for nearly 200 years**, on technological advantage plus access to **fabrics, spices and gold**.
- They were the **first European power to establish a regular trade in slaves from West Africa to the Americas** and **developed the triangular trade route** connecting **Europe, Africa and the Americas**.
- Effects on the Nigerian coast: trade relations that fed the **growth of brass casting in Benin** and the **emergence of trading in the Lagos Lagoon**; the area was **opened up to the rest of the world**, which eventually led to **colonialism and exploitation**; a **series of wars** to keep up the supply of slaves; and the **abandonment of African cultures**.
- The trade also shaped **European and American economies** and contributed to the **rise of racism and white supremacy**.

## Commonly confused

- **Legitimate trade vs the slave trade** — 'legitimate' trade means trade in **commodities, not people**: **palm oil, ivory**, later produce. It **expanded as the slave trade ended**; it is not a nicer kind of slave trading. Note that it is the source's own word, in quotation marks.
- **Abolition dates vs effective end** — Britain **seized slave ships from 1792**; **Buxton's bill was 1833**; but the Bight of Benin was **still the last active centre in 1840** and Lagos still the leading slave port. Abolition on paper ≠ end in practice; the enforcement step was the **1851 bombardment** and the **1861 annexation**.
- **Kosoko vs Akitoye** — **Kosoko** signed then **refused** the 1851 treaty and **fled to Epe**; **Akitoye** was the one **restored, on 29 December 1851**. Do not swap them.
- **1851 bombardment vs 1861 annexation** — ten years apart. **1851** = treaty, refusal, bombardment, Akitoye restored. **1861** = annexation of Lagos (and also the year **Laird died**).
- **Trading company vs colonial administration** — the **RNC was both**, which is the trap: it traded **and administered**, paying **dividends solely from trade profits**. Formal colonial rule only follows the **revocation of its charter** after 1895.
- **UAC / NAC / RNC** — same organisation, three successive names: **United African Company** (amalgamation under **Goldie-Taubman**) → **National African Company** (after buying **two French firms**) → **Royal Niger Company** (**on the 1886 charter**). The **Central African Company** is **Laird's 1858** company, an ingredient, not the same thing.
- **1881 vs 1882 vs 1886** — **applied** 1881; **capital raised to £1m** 1882 (still refused, French firms); **granted** 1886. Also keep **1884** (Hewett's tour, ORP founded, Berlin Conference) and **1887** (RNC controls the whole Niger basin) apart.
- **Oil Rivers Protectorate vs Niger Coast Protectorate** — **same territory, renamed in 1893**. ORP founded **1884**; its consul from **1891** was **Sir Ralph Moor**.
- **Salisbury vs Joseph Chamberlain** — **Salisbury** was the Colonial Secretary who **favoured** the RNC and blunted its critics; **Chamberlain's appointment by 1895** helped **weaken** it.
- **Trans-Saharan (Week 2) vs trans-Atlantic (this week)** — this week is the **Atlantic/Middle Passage** trade: Europeans, coast, the Americas. Do not import Kanem Bornu and the desert routes.
- **Asiento vs Royal Charter** — the **Asiento** is the **Spanish** licence to supply slaves to the **New World**; the **Royal Charter** is the **British** grant of 1886 to the NAC. Different centuries, different powers.
- **15 million vs 7 million** — **over 15 million reached the Americas**; **over 7 million died in transit (Lovejoy)**. The larger figure is the arrivals.
- **Who said what** — **Soyinka**: twin evils of slavery and colonialism. **Rodney**: agrees. **Lovejoy**: numbers and relocation away from slavers' routes. **Alco**: loss of skilled tradesmen. **Ake**: stagnation of productive forces. **Curtin**: largest intercontinental migration, 300 years.

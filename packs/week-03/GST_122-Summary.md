# GST_122 — Week 3 Summary

*Communication in English II · Week 3 (21–27 Sep 2026) · Topic: Sentences: Function and Structure; Word Classes: Function and Content Words; Concord*

## The 8 most examinable things this week

1. A sentence is classified on **two separate axes** — never conflate them: **function** (what it does) and **structure** (what clauses it's built from).
2. **Function**: declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory (plus a non-core fifth — conditional).
3. **Structure**: simple, compound, complex, compound-complex — tested purely by counting independent vs dependent clauses.
4. Words split into **function words** (closed class: conjunctions, auxiliaries, prepositions, articles, pronouns) which carry little meaning alone, and **content words** (open class: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) which carry the sentence's real meaning; content words are stressed in speech, function words are not.
5. **Concord** is grammatical agreement between sentence elements — most importantly, **subject-verb agreement**: the number of the subject (singular/plural) must match the number of the verb.
6. The **Rule of Proximity Agreement**: when a subject has more than one element, the verb agrees with whichever element sits **closest to it**, not with the whole compound subject.
7. **"And" joins subjects into one plural unit** (usually a plural verb); **"or"/"nor" keeps subjects separate**, so the verb takes its number from the nearer one — this is where proximity agreement actually bites.
8. Concord is examined by trap sentence, not by definition: watch for words sitting between the subject and its verb, and for collective nouns, both of which tempt you into agreeing with the wrong word.

## Sentence function and structure

- **Declarative** — statement; test: could you call it true/false? *"The sun rises in the east."*
- **Interrogative** — question; test: question word/mark. *"Where are you going?"*
- **Imperative** — command/request; test: subject "you" is understood, even under "please." *"Close the door."*
- **Exclamatory** — strong emotion; test: exclamation mark. *"Wow, that was amazing!"*
- **Simple** — one independent clause; a compound verb/subject inside it does not change this. *"She sings beautifully."*
- **Compound** — 2+ independent clauses, coordinating conjunction or semicolon, no dependent clause. *"She loves to read; he prefers to watch movies."*
- **Complex** — one independent + at least one dependent clause, via a subordinating conjunction (although, because, while, since...). *"Although it was raining, we went for a walk."*
- **Compound-complex** — both patterns at once. *"Nneoma cooked dinner while Lara slept, and Esohe swept the floor."*

## Function words vs content words

- **Function words** — closed class (conjunctions, auxiliary/helping verbs, prepositions, articles, pronouns, plus modals and qualifiers); glue the sentence together; unstressed in speech. *"between," "the," "was."*
- **Content words** — open class (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs); carry the sentence's actual information; stressed in speech. *"mountain," "difficult," "quietly."*
- Pennebaker's exam-favourite statistic: function words are **under 0.1% of your vocabulary** but make up **almost 60% of the words you actually use.**

## Concord (grammatical agreement)

- **What it is** — the set of rules requiring different elements of a sentence to agree with each other (chiefly, subject and verb agree in number). Concord matters because a mismatched subject-verb pair is one of the fastest ways to look grammatically unreliable. *Test:* find the true subject, then check the verb's number against it. *Example:* "The student writes well" (singular-singular), not "the student write well."
- **Subject-verb agreement (the fundamental rule)** — a singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb, regardless of any words sitting between them. *Example:* "The list of items on the shelf **is** complete" — the verb agrees with "list" (singular), not with the nearer noun "items."
- **Rule of Proximity Agreement** — with a multi-element subject, the verb agrees with the **nearest** element to it. *Example:* "Neither the students nor the teacher **is** ready" (verb agrees with "teacher," singular, the nearer subject) vs. "Neither the teacher nor the students **are** ready" (verb agrees with "students," plural, now the nearer subject) — swapping the order of the same two subjects changes the correct verb.
- **"Or"/"nor" vs "and"** — "and" combines subjects into a single compound unit, which normally takes a plural verb: "The manager and the clerk **were** present." "Or"/"nor" (and pairs like either...or, neither...nor) keep the subjects logically separate, so proximity decides the verb: "Either the clerks or the manager **is** responsible."

## Commonly confused

- **Compound vs complex** — not about length, but clause type: compound = independent + independent (coordinating conjunction/semicolon); complex = independent + dependent (subordinating conjunction). If one half can't stand alone, it's complex.
- **A simple sentence with a compound verb is still simple** — "Ngozi cooked dinner and cleaned the kitchen" is one subject with a compound verb, hence one independent clause (simple), not compound.
- **A word's class depends on its job in that sentence** — "have" is a content/main verb in "I *have* three books" (means "possess") but a function/auxiliary verb in "I *have* finished" (only builds the tense).
- **Either/neither ... or/nor agreement** — students default to a plural verb because two subjects are visible; the rule is proximity, not addition — the verb always tracks the nearer subject, not the total count.
- **A prepositional phrase between subject and verb is a decoy** — "The **quality** of the products **is** excellent," not "are": the object of the preposition ("products") is never the subject; find the head noun before the phrase.
- **Collective nouns (team, committee, family, government)** — normally take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit ("The committee **decides** today"), but can take a plural verb when members are acting individually ("The committee **are** divided on the issue" — more common in British usage). Know which reading a sentence intends before choosing the verb.

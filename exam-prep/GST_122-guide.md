## Orientation

Everything in GST_122 sits inside one frame, stated on the first slide of Week 1: there are four key language skills, and they are the building blocks of effective communication — **listening, speaking, reading and writing**. That order is the spine of the course.

Weeks 1 and 2 set up theory: the four skills, then how meaning travels (semiotics, denotation, connotation, homophones, ambiguity). Weeks 3 to 6 build writing from the ground up — words, sentences, punctuation and paragraphs, the writing process, then three finished products: summary, letter, report. Weeks 7 and 8 cover reading and vocabulary: SQ3R and reading strategies, then vocabulary expansion and word formation. Weeks 9 and 10 cover speaking — responding to prompts, stress, delivery — and carry the course's logic unit, inductive and deductive reasoning. Weeks 11 and 12 cover listening: why it matters, active listening, note-taking, and logic applied to presentations.

The arc: **four skills → meaning → words → sentences → punctuation → paragraphs → process → products → reading → vocabulary → speaking → reasoning → listening → note-taking.**

**How this course is examined.** It is a General Studies course, tested on recall: named lists, exact definitions, named methods, named people, exact figures. Naming three of four items is a wrong answer. So this guide gives every list complete, numbered, in the slides' order, with hooks where they help, and reproduces definitions in the course's own words.

**On the source.** Six topics survive only in a weekly summary sheet, because the lecture deck for them is not in the material; where that happens this guide says so. Where the material contradicts itself — about a dozen places — both versions are given. Part 15 gathers all of it.

## Part 1 — The Four Language Skills (Week 1)

### The core list

Headed "Key Language Skills — Building Blocks of Effective Communication":

1. **Listening** 2. **Speaking** 3. **Reading** 4. **Writing**

Hook: it is the order a human acquires them. A baby listens before it speaks, speaks before it reads, reads before it writes.

### The definitions, exactly as given

- **Listening:** "The ability to understand, interpret, and respond to spoken language." *Example: listening to a TED Talk and summarising the main ideas.* Listening takes many forms — listening tasks, audio books or textbooks, instructions, descriptions.
- **Speaking:** "The ability to articulate thoughts, ideas, and information coherently and effectively." *Example: answering questions in a job interview.*
- **Reading:** "The ability to comprehend, interpret, and analyse written text." *Example: reading a research paper and extracting key findings.*
- **Writing:** "The capacity to convey thoughts, information, and emotions through written language." *Example: composing a well-structured persuasive essay.*

Writing alone is a *capacity*, not an *ability*, and writing alone names **emotions**. Listening ends in *respond*; reading ends in *analyse*.

### Two pairs the course keeps separate

- **Hearing vs listening** (answered in Week 11): hearing is **passive and involuntary**; listening is **active and voluntary**, requiring conscious effort to interpret and understand.
- **Active vs passive listening:** "Active listening involves full engagement, while passive listening is merely hearing without deep comprehension." *Example: active listening during a lecture versus passive listening to background music.*

### Effective speaking — the six

1. **Clear pronunciation** 2. **Appropriate tone** 3. **The ability to engage the audience** 4. **Voice control** 5. **Body language** 6. **Delivery**

The course contrasts two answers to "Tell me about yourself": Job Seeker A gives a structured, relevant reply; Job Seeker B talks about friends, TV and a cat named Whiskers. The difference is control of relevance, tone and delivery. The other model is a persuasive speech on "The Legalisation of Assisted Suicide: A Case for Personal Autonomy", structured as position → historical context → arguments in favour → ethical counter-considerations.

### Reading and writing in Week 1

Effective reading involves understanding **main ideas, context, and subtext**. The comprehension passage, "The Dangers of Faking Mental Health Issues", has four named takeaways:

1. **Undermining Legitimacy** — faking diminishes the legitimacy of genuine cases and perpetuates stigma.
2. **Obstructing Access to Support** — it blocks those who truly need help.
3. **Misallocation of Resources** — it diverts treatment and professional attention.
4. **Honesty and Understanding** — promoting honesty ensures authentic sufferers receive care.

**Writing styles — four:** 1. **Persuasive** (argues) 2. **Descriptive** (paints) 3. **Narrative** (tells) 4. **Expository** (explains). This is *not* the eleven-item list of writing strategies in Week 4.

### Summary points and references

Combining the skills effectively "enhances your critical thinking, ability to engage complex discourse, and even gain a global perspective on issues." The Week 1 summary adds five points: expressing yourself matters more than fancy words; communication skills are needed everywhere; attention to grammar improves critical thinking; good grammar makes you credible; and the four skills are the foundations.

Skills acquired: **Active Listening, Effective Speaking, Reading Comprehension, Persuasive Writing, Critical Thinking.**
References: **Pinker (1995), *The Language Instinct*** (language); **Kermode (2015), *Speak So Your Audience Will Listen: 7 Steps to Confident and Authentic Public Speaking*** (speaking); **Dehaene (2009), *Reading in the Brain*** (reading).

### Where people go wrong

- **Hearing ≠ passive listening.** Hearing is involuntary and is contrasted with listening; passive listening is contrasted with active listening.
- **Writing styles (4) vs writing strategies (11).** Different weeks, different lists.
- **Swapping the definition verbs.** Reading ends in *analyse* because you can go back over a text; listening ends in *respond* because speech goes past you.
- **Writing "ability" for writing.** The course says *capacity*.

### In the exam

- "List the four key language skills in order."
- "Define listening as given." — The ability to understand, interpret, and respond to spoken language.
- Which item is missing from the six for effective speaking?
- "Which is a writing style: problem-solution, expository, chronological order, process analysis?" — Expository.
- "Differentiate active and passive listening with an example."

## Part 2 — Communication and Meaning (Week 2)

**Week 2 has no lecture slides in the material.** Only the weekly summary sheet survives, which raises the odds that every fact in it is used.

### The central claim

The ultimate goal of communication is **that meaning is accurately conveyed**. Communication is **not solely about words**; it also involves **signs and symbols**, and that field is **semiotics** — "the study of signs and symbols, their interpretation, and their role in communication" (wording from Week 8's vocabulary list). A red traffic light, a skull-and-crossbones, a raised hand: none is language, all convey meaning.

### Denotative and connotative meaning

- **Denotative meaning = literal meaning.**
- **Connotative meaning = implied or suggested meaning**, carrying the **cultural and emotional** aspects of communication.

*Home* and *house* share a denotation — a place where a person lives — but *home* carries warmth, safety, belonging. An estate agent advertises a home; a surveyor inspects a house. Denotation is stable across cultures; connotation is not.

Hook: **D for Dictionary, C for Culture.**

### The two sources of confusion

1. **Hazardous homophones** — words that sound alike but differ in meaning and usually spelling: *their/there/they're*, *principal/principle*, *stationary/stationery*. In speech there is no spelling to disambiguate them.
2. **Ambiguity** — a sentence carrying more than one meaning: "The manager told the supervisor that he would be promoted." Who is *he*?

The case study, "The Ambiguity Challenge in Project Coordination", gives a project team a deliberately ambiguous task-assignment sentence and collects their interpretations: an ambiguous instruction produces as many workplans as readers.

### The five summary points

1. Communication involves signs and symbols, constituting **semiotics**.
2. **Denotative (literal)** and **connotative (implied or suggested)** meanings differ, emphasising cultural and emotional aspects.
3. **Hazardous homophones** and **ambiguity** are two potential sources of confusion, to be avoided for clarity.
4. The multifaceted nature of meaning requires **active interpretation by the listener**, considering context and recognising ambiguities.
5. Effective communication involves conveying intended meanings accurately and being mindful of the **broader cultural and emotional associations** of words.

Point 4 puts work on the listener; that idea returns in Weeks 11 and 12 as active listening.

Skills acquired: **Semiotic Awareness, Contextual Interpretation, Clarity in Expression, Active Listening, Conflict Resolution, Cultural Sensitivity.**

### Where people go wrong

- **Reversing denotation and connotation.** Use D-for-Dictionary.
- **Saying "homonym".** The course names only **homophones**. Homonym and homograph are standard terms but **not in the course material**.
- **Treating semiotics as grammar or language.** Signs and symbols are wider than language.
- **Merging the two confusions.** Homophones are a **word-level** problem; ambiguity is a **sentence-level** problem.

### In the exam

- "Difference between denotative and connotative meaning?"
- "Name the two potential sources of confusion." — Hazardous homophones and ambiguity.
- "Define semiotics."
- A stem about *their/there* (homophones) versus one about a two-reading sentence (ambiguity).

## Part 3 — Word Classes: Function and Content Words (Week 3)

### The two classes

Words fall into **two main classes**, based on **the grammatical and semantic roles they play in sentences**: **function words** and **content words**.

**Function words:** "also known as grammatical words or structure words, are words that have a primarily grammatical function and convey little or no meaning on their own. Instead, they serve to connect or build relationships between content words. Function words are crucial for the grammatical structure of a sentence." The course adds that this does not make them useless.

**Content words:** "also referred to as **lexical words**, carry the main meaning in a sentence. They convey information about the real-world or abstract entities, actions, qualities, or relationships."

### The Pennebaker figures — learn exactly

**James Pennebaker:** "function words account for **less than one-tenth of 1 percent of your vocabulary** but make up **almost 60 percent of the words you use**." Few words, used constantly.

### Seven other names for function words

1. **Grammatical morphemes** 2. **Function morphemes** 3. **Empty words** 4. **Structure words** 5. **Grammatical words** 6. **Grammatical functors** 7. **Form words**

Hook: remember the three outliers — **Empty, Structure, Form** — then the grammatical family and function morphemes.

### The five function-word parts of speech

1. **Conjunctions** — join clauses or items (*but, although, since, so, for*).
2. **Auxiliary/Helping verbs** — support a main verb (*was, been, has, have, did*).
3. **Prepositions** — show relation in space or time (*between, in, at, up, over*).
4. **Articles** — mark definiteness (*a, an, the*).
5. **Pronouns** — stand in for nouns (*they, she, them, it, my*).

Word examples given on the slide: *a, an, and, be, under, the, he.* A further slide adds **Modals** (*can, could, may, should, will*), **Qualifiers** (*very, quite, too, much, somewhat*) and **Question words (in some cases)** (*who, why, where, when, which*) — reproduce that hedge.

### The four content-word parts of speech

1. **Nouns** (Table, Bag, Boy, Mountain, Plaza) 2. **Verbs** (Drown, Dig, Bury, Kill, Suffocate) 3. **Adjectives** (Robust, Clever, Abrasive, Difficult, Beautiful) 4. **Adverbs** (Quietly, Loudly, Irritably, Surreptitiously, Predictably)

Four carry meaning; five carry structure.

### The stress rule

**In English conversation, emphasis is placed on content words, while function words remain non-stressed.** Familiarity with this "contributes not only to comprehension but also, crucially, to refining pronunciation skills." In "She BOUGHT a NEW CAR at the MARKET" the stressed words are verb, adjective, noun, noun. This is the default that Week 9's sentence stress deliberately breaks.

### Two errors in the source

**The two lists of function words disagree.** The body slide lists five (conjunctions, auxiliary/helping verbs, prepositions, articles, pronouns); the summary slide lists "articles, prepositions, conjunctions, pronouns, and **certain adverbs**" — dropping auxiliaries, adding adverbs, which the same lecture calls content words. **Use the body slide's five.**

**A mislabelled heading.** The slide introducing nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs is headed "The parts of speech that fall under the category of **function** words are:". Copy-paste error; the list is the content-word list.

### Where people go wrong

- **Calling pronouns content words.** They feel meaningful; they are function words, as are articles, prepositions and auxiliaries.
- **Auxiliary verbs are not content words.** In "She **has** eaten", *has* is a helper (function), *eaten* is lexical (content).
- **Lexical vs grammatical.** Content = lexical. Function = grammatical/structure/form. *Lexicon* means vocabulary, so lexical words are the vocabulary words.
- **Reversing Pennebaker's figures.** Tiny share of vocabulary, huge share of usage.

### In the exam

- "Function words are also called ___" — any of the seven; "list five" needs five.
- "According to Pennebaker, function words make up almost what percentage of the words you use?" — Almost 60 percent.
- Classify underlined words in a sentence as function or content.
- "Which is NOT a content word class: noun, adverb, preposition, adjective?" — Preposition.
- "Which class of words is stressed in spoken English?" — Content words.

## Part 4 — Sentences: Function and Structure (Week 3)

### What a sentence is

**"In the most simple form, a sentence is a group of words that express a complete thought or an idea. For a sentence to be deemed complete, it must possess two important parts of speech which are 'a noun' or 'pronoun' (subject) and a 'verb' (predicate)."**

**A sentence is an aspect of syntax, which is itself an aspect of language.** Example: "**I** danced at the party" — *I* subject, *danced at the party* predicate.

### The two ways to analyse a sentence

- **Function:** the sentence described by its **purpose**.
- **Structure:** how a sentence is made up of **different parts or elements like subjects, predicates, objects, nouns, verbs**.

Ask "what is it doing?" (function) versus "what is it made of?" (structure).

### Types by STRUCTURE — the four

1. **Simple** — "one independent clause, which is a complete thought that can stand alone as a sentence. It typically contains a subject and a predicate." *"She sings beautifully." "I like pizza."*
2. **Compound** — "formed by combining two or more independent clauses using coordinating conjunctions (such as 'and,' 'but,' 'or,' 'so,' etc.) or a semicolon." *"I wanted to go to the store, but it started raining." "She loves to read; he prefers to watch movies."*
3. **Complex** — "one independent clause and at least one dependent clause (also called a subordinate clause). Dependent clauses cannot stand alone… Complex sentences often use subordinating conjunctions (such as 'although,' 'because,' 'while,' 'since,' etc.)." *"Although it was raining, we went for a walk." "Nneoma cooked dinner, while Lara slept."*
4. **Compound-complex** — "a combination of a compound sentence and a complex sentence. It includes two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause." *"Nneoma cooked dinner while Lara slept, and Esohe swept the floor."*

| Type | Independent clauses | Dependent clauses |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | 1 | 0 |
| Compound | 2 or more | 0 |
| Complex | 1 | 1 or more |
| Compound-complex | 2 or more | 1 or more |

Count the independent clauses, then check for a dependent one. Two questions, four answers. The course notes these structures let writers vary sentence length and style, and sets an expectation: **"at this level, you will be expected to write more compound and compound-complex sentences."**

### Five tips for creating compound-complex sentences

1. **Relative clauses** 2. **Parenthesis** 3. **Context clues (use the various types)** 4. **Punctuations — (Semicolon, hyphen)** 5. **Details and explanations**

The slides show that a subordinate clause can move: *Jane squeaked loudly, while the orthopedic surgeon cracked her ankle in place* / *While the orthopedic surgeon cracked her ankle in place, Jane squeaked loudly* / *Jane, while the orthopedic surgeon cracked her ankle in place, squeaked loudly.*

Stated purpose: **"The essence of compound-complex sentences is that they help to flesh out and boost your paragraph writing."** And: **"A good paragraph must contain a wide range of sentences (function and structure), a range of punctuation, context clues, connectives, and a lot more."** That is the hinge into Part 7.

### Types by FUNCTION — the four, plus one

1. **Declarative** — "make statements or provide information… used to convey facts, opinions, or ideas." *"The sun rises in the east."*
2. **Imperative** — "give commands, instructions, or requests… tell someone to do something or make a suggestion." *"Please pass the salt."*
3. **Interrogative** — "used to ask questions. They seek information and typically begin with question words like who, what, where, when, why, or how." *"What is your name?"*
4. **Exclamatory** — "express strong emotions or exclamations. They often end with an exclamation mark (!) and convey surprise, excitement, anger, or other intense feelings." *"Wow, that was amazing!"*

**Plus a fifth:** "In addition to these four main types, there is also a category of **Conditional Sentences** (also known as 'if' sentences) that express conditions and their likely results. Conditional sentences can be complex and are used to describe hypothetical situations." *"If it rains, we will stay indoors."*

### Errors in the source

- **The function list appears in two orders.** Body slide: Declarative, Imperative, Interrogative, Exclamatory. Summary slide: Declarative, **Interrogative, Imperative**, Exclamatory. Use the body slide's order when order is asked for.
- **A duplicated slide.** The lecture's "Further Reading" slide is a verbatim copy of the Conditional Sentences slide; the real further reading is missing. The reading for the week's other lecture is **Nordquist, "Definition and Examples of Function Words in English"** and **Beare, "Content and Function Words."**
- **A truncated list.** The "Sentence Analysis — examples" slide opens a numbered list with one item and stops; the rest was on an image.

### Where people go wrong

- **Compound vs complex.** Compound joins clauses of **equal** rank; complex joins clauses of **unequal** rank. Compound = coordinate = equal partners; complex = subordinate = boss and assistant.
- **Imperative vs declarative.** A declarative *tells you something*; an imperative *tells you to do something*. Both end in a full stop, so only purpose settles it.
- **Exclamatory vs interrogative.** "Are you serious!" is emotional but grammatically a question. Judge by purpose.
- **Mixing the two four-item lists.** Structure: simple, compound, complex, compound-complex. Function: declarative, imperative, interrogative, exclamatory.
- **Forgetting the semicolon compound.** No conjunction is required.

### In the exam

- "Define a sentence."
- "State the two ways a sentence may be analysed." — By function and by structure.
- "Classify: 'Although it was raining, they decided to go for a walk, and they took umbrellas just in case.'" — Compound-complex.
- "Which type gives commands, instructions or requests?" — Imperative.
- "What fifth category does the course add?" — Conditional ("if") sentences.

## Part 5 — Grammar: Concord (Week 3)

**The lecture slides on concord are not in the material.** Week 3's decks are Sentences and Word Classes; the weekly summary sheet is entirely about concord, so every line of it is potential exam material.

### The definition

**Concord** is grammatical agreement — **"a set of rules governing the agreement between different elements in a sentence"**, which **"ensures linguistic harmony."** It is "essential for ensuring coherence in language, preventing distractions caused by bad grammar", and fosters **credibility** in written and spoken language. That word runs through Weeks 1, 3 and 4: correctness buys credibility.

### The two named rules

**1. Subject–verb agreement** — *"the number of the subject must match the number of the verb in a sentence."* *The boy **runs**. / The boys **run**.* The trap: the *-s* that marks a plural noun marks a **singular** verb.

**2. The Rule of Proximity Agreement** — *"requires the verb or noun to align with the closest subject in sentences with multiple elements."* *Neither the students nor the **lecturer is** available. / Neither the lecturer nor the **students are** available.* Same nouns, same conjunction, different verb, because the order changed. Memorise that pair.

### The role of "or"

**"The presence of 'or' in a sentence can influence the choice between singular and plural verbs."** With **and**, subjects add together and take a plural verb; with **or / nor** they do not, and the verb agrees with the nearer one. Hook: **AND adds. OR chooses** — the nearest.

The case study, "Navigating Proximity in Team Project Assignments", varies which team member sits nearest the success or failure in a sentence and asks readers who was responsible. The finding: **sentence proximity shapes perceived accountability.**

Skills acquired: **Concord Proficiency, Structural Analysis, Communication Precision, Critical Thinking, Team Communication, Conjunction Mastery.**
References: **"Understanding and Using English Grammar" workbooks by Azar, Koch, Hagen**; **"Basic English Grammar" by Betty Azar.**

### Where people go wrong

- **Over-applying proximity.** It governs *or*/*nor*. "The bag of apples **is** heavy" — the subject is *bag*; *of apples* is a prepositional phrase. The nearest **noun** is not always the nearest **subject**.
- **Dropping a word from the name.** It is the **Rule of Proximity Agreement**, in full.
- **Treating concord as grammar in general.** Concord is specifically agreement between elements.

### In the exam

- "Define concord."
- "State the rule of subject–verb agreement."
- "What is the Rule of Proximity Agreement?"
- "Neither the manager nor the workers ___ (is/are) to blame." — *are*.

## Part 6 — Writing Mechanics: Punctuation (Week 4)

**"Punctuation is a crucial element of writing that helps convey meaning, clarify sentences, and guide the reader through your text."**

### The four uses of punctuation

1. **Changing the Meaning** — *"Let's eat, Grandma."* vs *"Let's eat Grandma."*
2. **Emphasising a Point** — *"We won the game!"* vs *"We won the game."*
3. **Conveying Nuance** — *"I appreciate your help."* vs *"I appreciate your help!"*
4. **Clarifying Ambiguity** — *"I like cooking, my family, and pets."* vs *"I like cooking my family and pets."*

The course also puts up one unexplained sentence: **"A woman without her man is nothing."** Punctuated *A woman, without her man, is nothing* it means one thing; punctuated *A woman: without her, man is nothing* it means the opposite.

### The fifteen marks, in course order

1. **Period (.)** 2. **Comma (,)** 3. **Semicolon (;)** 4. **Colon (:)** 5. **Apostrophe (')** 6. **Quotation Marks (" ")** 7. **Question Mark (?)** 8. **Exclamation Point (!)** 9. **Hyphen (-)** 10. **Dash (— or -)** 11. **Ellipsis (...)** 12. **Parentheses ( )** 13. **Brackets [ ]** 14. **Slash (/)** 15. **Ampersand (&)**

Hook, four groups: sentence-builders (1–4); speech-and-feeling marks (5–8); joiners and breakers (9–11); enclosers and symbols (12–15). **Build, feel, join, enclose.**

### Uses, mark by mark

**Period** — ends declarative sentences and statements; used in abbreviations (Dr., Mr.).

**Comma — five uses:** 1. separates items in a list; 2. sets off introductory phrases and clauses; 3. separates adjectives when they modify the same noun; 4. separates independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction; 5. sets off non-essential or parenthetical information.

**Semicolon** — connects closely related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction; separates items in a list when the items themselves contain commas. Three named uses: 1. **Joining Independent Clauses** (*"significant results; however, further analysis is required"*) 2. **Separating Items with Commas** (*"London, UK; Berlin, Germany; and Paris, France"*) 3. **Balancing Sentence Structure**. The second use is the "super-comma".

**Colon** — introduces a list, explanation or quotation; emphasises or introduces a key point. Three named uses: 1. **Introducing a List** 2. **Introducing an Explanation** (*"The key to success is simple: hard work…"*) 3. **Introducing a Quotation** (*"Shakespeare's famous line reads: 'To be or not to be…'"*).

**Apostrophe** — 1. **Possession** (*the cat's tail*) 2. **Contraction** (*it's a beautiful day*) 3. **Plural Possession** (*the students' essays*). Placement is the examinable point: *student's* is one, *students'* is many.

**Quotation Marks** — 1. **Direct Speech** 2. **Titles of Short Works** (articles, short stories, chapters) 3. **Sarcastic or Ironic Use** (*his so-called "expertise"*).

**Question Mark** — 1. **Direct Questions** 2. **Rhetorical Questions** 3. **Indicating Uncertainty**.

**Exclamation Point** — 1. **Strong Emotion** 2. **Urgency or Surprise** 3. **Expressing Outrage**. **Use sparingly in formal writing.**

**Hyphen** — joins compound words (*mother-in-law*); connects compound adjectives before a noun (*well-known author*); breaks a word at line end. Three named uses: 1. **Compound Adjectives** (*state-of-the-art*) 2. **Breaking Words** 3. **Number Ranges** (*April 5-7*).

**Dash** — sets off or emphasises information. **An em dash (—) is longer and is often used in place of parentheses or commas. An en dash (-) is shorter and used to indicate a range (e.g., "pages 10-15").** Three named uses: 1. **Emphasising Information** 2. **Replacing Parentheses** 3. **Creating a Dramatic Pause**.

**Ellipsis** — 1. **Omitted Words** 2. **Trailing Off** 3. **Pause or Uncertainty**.

**Parentheses** — 1. **Enclosing Extra Information** 2. **Acronyms or Abbreviations** (*World Health Organization (WHO)*) 3. **Citing Sources** (*According to Smith (2019)…*).

**Brackets** — 1. **Adding Information** to a quotation (*'I look forward to [the] challenge'*) 2. **Editorial Comments** (*'The city [London]…'*) 3. **Mathematics** — grouping and intervals.

**Slash** — 1. **Indicating Alternatives** (*red/green*) 2. **Dates or Ratios** (*10/15/2023*) 3. **Paths in URLs**.

**Ampersand** — 1. **Abbreviations** (*R&D*) 2. **Company Names** (*Johnson & Johnson*) 3. **Citations** (*Smith, J., & Brown, A. (2021)*).

**The pattern:** from the apostrophe onward, almost every mark has exactly **three** named uses. The comma is the exception with five; the period has two.

### Two problems in the source

- **Two slides are headed "Comma (,)".** The second gives *the colon's* three uses with the colon's examples. **Learn them as colon uses.**
- **Number ranges are claimed twice.** The hyphen slide claims them ("April 5-7"); the dash slide gives ranges to the **en dash** ("pages 10-15"). Answer according to the mark the question names.

### The course's summary and references

**"Punctuation is vital for written communication, clarifying meaning, ensuring clarity, expressing emotion, and distinguishing ideas. Its correct use in professional and academic writing underscores credibility and enhances overall quality."**

References: **Truss (2003), *Eats, Shoots & Leaves: The Zero Tolerance Approach to Punctuation*; Trask (2008), *The Penguin Guide to Punctuation*; Linder (1998), *Punctuation..? A Primer of Punctuation*.** Truss's title is itself a comma joke.

### Where people go wrong

- **Semicolon vs colon.** A semicolon joins two things of equal weight; a colon introduces something. **Semicolon = balance; colon = announcement.**
- **Hyphen vs dash.** A hyphen is short and joins *words*; a dash is long and separates *ideas*. Em dash for interruption, en dash for ranges.
- **Parentheses vs brackets.** **Round for your own aside; square for interfering inside someone else's quotation.**
- **Quotation marks for emphasis.** A sign reading *Fresh "Fish"* says the fish is not really fish.
- **Apostrophes in plurals.** *Bananas*, not *banana's*. And *it's* = *it is*; *its* is possessive with no apostrophe.

### In the exam

- "State four uses of punctuation."
- "State three uses of the semicolon."
- "Punctuate the following sentence." — Usually a Grandma-type sentence.
- "Which mark introduces a list, an explanation or a quotation?" — The colon.
- "Difference between an em dash and an en dash?"

## Part 7 — Paragraphs (Week 4)

### The definition

**"Paragraphs represent individual units of thought within a written composition. In an essay, they serve as discrete segments of discourse, with the expectation that each paragraph maintains independence while contributing to the collective meaning of the entire essay through its connection to other paragraphs."**

**"The fundamental purpose of paragraphs is to uphold the structural integrity of our essays."** They are **"the building blocks of effective writing."** The course's image: the paragraph is the **filling of a burger** — where the juicy details are. (The burger picture was an image slide and did not extract.)

### Components of a Good Paragraph — the four

1. **Unity** 2. **Coherence** 3. **Topic sentence** 4. **One idea at a time**

Various sources list various components, **"But one component remains constant, 'Topic sentence'."**

**Unity** — "requires a singular central theme, with all sentences relating to this theme to fulfil the paragraph's purpose. **Avoiding digression is essential.**"

**Coherence** — "involves ensuring meaningful contributions from all parts, including **lexical and syntactic structures**. Orderly and logically connected ideas, facilitated by **cohesive links like conjunctions and pronouns** to prevent monotony and ambiguity, are crucial." Coherence ensures ideas **"flow smoothly and logically."** The course demonstrates with a paragraph about a morning run, pancakes and a park whose sentences each make sense but do not connect, then revises it by adding only joins. **Coherence is a matter of joins, not of facts.**

**One idea at a time** — "Paragraphs should focus on one idea exclusively… Transitioning from one paragraph to another should not involve the continuation of a point." Example: all advantages in one paragraph, all disadvantages in another.

**Topic sentence** — "the sentence highlighting the key message of that particular paragraph"; it provides "a clear and concise preview" and acts as **a roadmap**. Placement: "often found at the beginning of a paragraph. Sometimes in the middle or at the end." The counting rule: **"if there are five paragraphs in an essay, there must be five topic sentences."**

**Supporting sentences** — "Since the topic sentence is just one sentence, then the other sentences in the paragraph will support or add more details to the topic sentence; this is why the other sentences are called **supporting details or supporting sentences**." The worked example is a paragraph on **Body Positivity** whose topic sentence names the subject and announces the angle — controversy — after which each supporting sentence delivers a piece of that controversy.

### Transition words — the core six

1. **Additionally** 2. **Furthermore** 3. **Moreover** 4. **However** 5. **Nevertheless** 6. **On the contrary**

Three that add, three that contrast.

### The full transition tables — ten categories

| Addition | Contrast | Comparison | Cause and Effect | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Additionally | However | Similarly | Therefore | Indeed |
| Furthermore | Nevertheless | Likewise | Thus | Certainly |
| Moreover | On the contrary | In the same way | Consequently | Specifically |
| In addition | Conversely | Just as | As a result | In particular |
| Likewise | In contrast | Compared to | Accordingly | Especially |
| Not only… but also | Although | Like | Because | Above all |

| Sequence/Order | Illustration/Example | Conclusion/Summary | Time | Clarification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First, second, … | For example | In conclusion | Meanwhile | In other words |
| Next | For instance | To sum up | During | To clarify |
| Then | Namely | Overall | Simultaneously | That is to say |

Ten categories: **Addition, Contrast, Comparison, Cause and Effect, Emphasis, Sequence/Order, Illustration/Example, Conclusion/Summary, Time, Clarification.** Some words appear in two buckets (*Likewise, Consequently, Specifically, Namely, In particular*) — that is in the source.

The transition-spotting exercise uses a paragraph on climate change and highlights four: **Firstly, Consequently, Furthermore, In conclusion** — one each from sequence, cause and effect, addition and conclusion.

References: **Adedeji (2005), "Grammatical ties in English Discourse", in Olateju & Oyeleye (eds), *Perspectives on Language and Literature*, Obafemi Awolowo University, pp. 157–166; Aremo (2001), *Advanced English Compositions*; Aremo (1995), *An Introduction to English Sentences (1)*; Aremo (1997), *(2)*.**

### Where people go wrong

- **Unity vs coherence.** **Unity = one topic** — digression breaks it. **Coherence = good order** — the sentences must flow. A paragraph can be unified but incoherent, or coherent but not unified.
- **Unity vs one idea at a time.** Unity is about the sentences *within* the paragraph; one idea at a time is about *not carrying a point across a paragraph break*.
- **Topic sentence vs thesis statement.** A topic sentence governs one paragraph; a thesis governs the essay. Five paragraphs, five topic sentences.
- **Supporting sentences vs transitions.** Supporting sentences carry content; transitions join ideas.

## Part 8 — The Writing Process and Writing Strategies (Weeks 4 and 5)

### The three stages

**"Crafting impactful writing tasks necessitates the thoughtful creation of cohesive sets of activities encompassing pre-writing, during-writing, and post-writing stages."** Three stages, though only two are taught in detail.

**Pre-writing:** "the crucial phase of the writing process that precedes the actual composition. It involves a series of strategic activities aimed at **generating and organizing ideas, establishing a purpose, and constructing a preliminary framework**." Analogy: **an architect planning a structure.**

**Post-writing:** "encompasses the activities undertaken after the initial draft is completed. It involves a meticulous revision process, ensuring **coherence, clarity, and adherence to academic conventions**." Writers focus on **revising, editing, and refining**. Analogy: **polishing a gem.**

### The ten pre-writing activities, in slide order

1. **Brainstorming Techniques** — divergent thinking through **mind mapping, free-writing, or concept clustering**. *Mind-mapping an essay on environmental sustainability.*
2. **Freewriting** — unstructured writing that discovers unexpected details. *A novelist exploring a character's backstory.*
3. **Research** — gathering information before writing. *Types of renewable energy, their benefits and challenges.*
4. **Mind mapping** — visualising connections between ideas. *Branches for the pros and cons of social media.*
5. **Outlining** — key points, supporting evidence and a logical flow; a **roadmap for writing**. *Planning a speech.*
6. **Identifying Audience and Purpose** — which shapes **tone, style and content**. *A report for executives, to inform or persuade.*
7. **Storyboarding** — organising **narrative structure** visually. *A filmmaker planning scene sequence.*
8. **Drafting Questions** — preparing questions in advance. *A journalist before an interview.*
9. **Clustering** — associating words or images around a central theme. *A poet generating ideas.*
10. **Listing** — listing items to guide structure. *Similarities and differences before a compare-and-contrast essay.*

Note the three named **divergent thinking** methods, which also appear in the learning objectives: **mind mapping, free-writing, concept clustering.** Each example is tied to a different professional — if the exam says "a filmmaker plans the sequence of scenes", it wants **storyboarding**. One slide in this run, "Brainstorming Techniques", was an image and extracted as nothing.

### The ten post-writing activities, in slide order

1. **Revision** — revisiting the introduction, reorganising paragraphs. About **structure and content**.
2. **Editing for Grammar and Style** — eliminating grammatical errors, improving sentence structure, ensuring consistency of style.
3. **Peer Review** — exchanging drafts with a peer for feedback on clarity and strength of arguments.
4. **Proofreading** — catching typographical errors, misspellings and punctuation mistakes; the last, surface-level pass.
5. **Seeking Feedback from Instructors or Editors** — criticism on plot development, character arcs, overall storytelling.
6. **Checking Citations and References** — accuracy and formatting; crucial for **academic integrity**.
7. **Polishing Transitions** — smoothing the flow between paragraphs and ideas.
8. **Formatting and Presentation** — **font size, margins, spacing**.
9. **Finalising Conclusions** — ensuring the conclusion summarises key points and closes impactfully.
10. **Publishing or Submitting** — "marks the **end of the writing process** and the transition to sharing the work with its intended audience."

The order runs from the largest unit down to the smallest, then out the door: revise the building, edit the rooms, proofread the dust, submit.

### The eleven writing strategies (Week 4 summary)

Writing strategies are **akin to programming languages**, each designed for specific purposes, "each with its syntax, rules, and best practices."

1. **Persuasion** — argues a position. *Exercise should be compulsory in schools.*
2. **Narration** — tells a story in sequence. *An account of registration week.*
3. **Description** — paints a sensory picture. *Lagos traffic at 6 a.m.*
4. **Exposition** — explains without arguing. *How the electoral system works.*
5. **Comparison** — sets out similarities. *Online and on-campus study.*
6. **Contrast** — sets out differences.
7. **Cause and effect** — traces why and what followed. *Why subsidy removal raises fares.*
8. **Problem-solution** — states a problem, proposes remedies. *Examination malpractice.*
9. **Definition** — establishes what a term means. *What plagiarism is.*
10. **Chronological order** — arranges by time. *The history of the university.*
11. **Process analysis** — explains how something is done, step by step. *How to write a report.*

The first four are Week 1's four **writing styles** reordered; learn those, then add the remaining seven.

### Bruce Edward's analogy and the other summary points

Week 4's summary attributes to **Bruce Edward** the claim that **learning often starts in your hand, travels up your arm, and lands in your brain** — you learn writing by doing it, not by planning it in your head. It adds that **tone and style** impact audience engagement, that **audience awareness** determines both, that **editing and revision** refine writing, and that sentence structuring contributes to effective communication.

### The five Week 5 summary points

1. Pre-writing involves **researching, brainstorming, and outlining**.
2. **Brainstorming** sparks creativity and explores various ideas.
3. An **outline** acts as a **roadmap**, organising thoughts and giving direction.
4. **Paragraphs are likened to slices of cake**, each with a distinct flavour and a central theme expressed through a clear topic sentence.
5. **Post-writing** includes **editing and proofreading**.

Point 4 is worth flagging: Week 4 compares a paragraph to a **burger filling**, Week 5 to a **slice of cake**. Both are in the course.

Skills acquired: **Research Proficiency, Creative Thinking, Structural Organization, Editing Expertise, Proofreading Mastery, Professional Communication.**
References: **Mogahed (2013), "Planning out pre-writing activities"; Patthey, Matsumura & Valdés (2004), "Investigating the process approach to writing instruction in urban middle schools"; Coleman (2010), "Elementary Teachers' Instructional Practices Involving Graphical Representations."**

### Where people go wrong

- **Revision vs editing vs proofreading.** Revision reworks **content and structure**; editing fixes **grammar, sentence structure and style**; proofreading catches **typos, misspellings and punctuation**. **Big, medium, small.**
- **Peer review vs seeking feedback from instructors or editors.** Peer review is an exchange with an equal; the other is going to an authority. Week 8 defines *peer review* differently — "the evaluation of scholarly work by experts in the same field before it is published" — and that is the wording for a definition question.
- **Clustering vs mind mapping vs brainstorming.** Brainstorming is the umbrella; mind mapping is the visual branching version; clustering is association around a central theme.
- **Eleven strategies vs four styles.** Different lists, different weeks.
- **Forgetting "during-writing".** Three stages, not two.

## Part 9 — Three Types of Writing: Summary, Letter, Report (Week 6)

The framing analogy: **summarising is like distilling information into a concise map; letter writing is akin to composing a message to a friend; report crafting is like planning a successful expedition.**

### The three definitions, exactly as given

**Summary:** "a **condensed and concise version of a larger text or document**, highlighting the main ideas, key points, and essential information. It provides a brief overview without including unnecessary details, allowing readers to grasp the core content quickly."

**Letter:** "a **written message or communication addressed to a specific person or group**. It typically follows a formal or informal format and may serve various purposes, such as **conveying information, expressing thoughts or feelings, making requests, or establishing and maintaining connections**. Letters can be used in **personal, professional, or business** contexts."

**Report:** "a **formal document that presents detailed information, analysis, findings, and conclusions on a specific topic or issue**. It often follows a structured format, including sections like **introduction, methodology, findings, conclusions, and recommendations**. Reports are commonly used in **academic, business, and professional** settings."

### Steps to Writing a Summary — eight

1. **Read the Text Carefully** — understand the main ideas and key points.
2. **Identify the Thesis or Main Idea** — determine the primary argument or message.
3. **Highlight Key Points** — identify the supporting details and examples.
4. **Condense Information** — summarise **in your own words**, focusing on brevity and clarity.
5. **Maintain Structure** — retain the logical order of the original.
6. **Eliminate Unnecessary Details** — omit minor details unless crucial.
7. **Check for Coherence** — ensure the summary flows smoothly.
8. **Review and Revise** — edit for conciseness, accuracy and clarity.

The first four get the content; the last four shape it. Step 4 is the one students break — a summary that copies sentences is not a summary.

### Steps to Writing a Letter — nine

1. **Identify Purpose and Audience** 2. **Choose the Right Format** (formal, informal, business) 3. **Begin with a Greeting** 4. **Introduction** — state the purpose 5. **Body** — organised paragraphs, each addressing a specific point 6. **Politeness and Tone** 7. **Closing** — summarise, express gratitude or goodwill 8. **Closing Salutation** — a closing phrase and your signature 9. **Review and Edit**

Steps 7 and 8 are separate: the Closing is the final paragraph; the Closing Salutation is "Yours sincerely" plus signature.

### Key Parts of the Letter — five

1. **Greeting** — "Dear Mr Azeez,"
2. **Opening Sentence** — "I hope this letter finds you well."
3. **Purpose of the Letter** — "I am writing to express my gratitude for your unwavering support…"
4. **Closing** — a polite closing such as "Best regards" or "Sincerely."
5. **Signature** — sign with your name.

The sample is a **Letter of Appreciation** from Katie Afam, PD, Hollie Holdings, to Mr Azeez, signed "Yours sincerely." **Two lists, two questions:** *steps* = nine (a procedure); *parts* = five (an anatomy).

### Steps to Writing a Report — eleven

1. **Define the Purpose** 2. **Research and Gather Information** 3. **Outline the Structure** 4. **Write an Introduction** — topic, purpose, scope 5. **Present Methodology** — ensuring **transparency and reliability** 6. **Analyse and Present Findings** — with supporting evidence, using **graphs, charts or tables** if necessary 7. **Draw Conclusions** 8. **Make Recommendations (if applicable)** 9. **Write a Summary** 10. **Include References and Appendices** 11. **Review and Revise**

Reproduce the qualifier in step 8.

### The worked samples

**The summary sample** condenses a dense passage on politics — power, ideology, public sentiment, elections, the spectrum from **conservatism to progressivism**, and the influence of lobbying and vested interests. The summary keeps every proposition and drops the metaphors. That is the technique.

**The report sample**, on global poverty, is laid out under the step headings: an introduction on causes, consequences and solutions; a methodology of systematic review of **statistical agencies, research institutions and NGOs**; findings that rates are declining but with **Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia** worst affected; conclusions calling for multifaceted intervention; and recommendations of investment in education and healthcare, targeted economic development, social safety nets, and collaboration between **governments, NGOs and the private sector**. If asked to outline a report, reproduce that skeleton.

### The course's closing summary

Summarising distils main ideas with brevity and clarity. **Letter writing is "a creative process, akin to sculpting, where words are the sculpting tools shaping intent."** Report crafting requires a structured approach with defined sections. Note the second analogy for letters — **sculpting** — alongside "composing a message to a friend".

### Where people go wrong

- **Nine steps given when five parts are asked for.** Steps = procedure; parts = anatomy.
- **Merging "Closing" and "Closing Salutation".** They are steps 7 and 8.
- **Confusing the report's five sections with its eleven steps.** Sections are what appears on the page; steps are what you do.
- **Summarising by copying.** Step 4 says *in your own words*; step 5 says keep the original's order.

- "Define a report."
- "List the sections of a report." — Introduction, methodology, findings, conclusions, recommendations.
- "State the steps in writing a summary."
- "Name the five key parts of a letter."

## Part 10 — Reading: SQ3R and Reading Strategies (Week 7)

### What reading strategies are

**"Reading strategies are techniques and approaches that individuals use to enhance their reading comprehension, retention, and overall effectiveness when engaging with written text. These strategies help readers process information, extract meaning, and gain a deeper understanding of the material."** The course then says: **"SQ3R happens to be one of these strategies"** — one strategy, not the definition of one.

### SQ3R

**S = Survey. Q = Question. 3R = Read, Recite, Review.** Five steps.

1. **Survey** — "quickly scanning the entire text before you start reading in detail… look at **headings, subheadings, images, and any other features that provide an overview**." *In a history textbook: chapter titles, subtitles, captions of illustrations.*
2. **Question** — "**This is where you start asking questions, not answering the questions from the passage.** Here, you generate questions based on what you've discovered during the survey." *After surveying a research paper: "What is the primary hypothesis being tested?"*
3. **Read** — "the time to read the actual text." **Two goals:** (i) **read to answer the questions you have generated**; (ii) **read to engage with the material to gain a fuller understanding.**
4. **Recite** — "**Vocalise your thoughts!** This is where you recite or **verbally explain the key points, words, and concepts** from the text." Saying it exposes gaps that reading conceals.
5. **Review** — "you create **summaries, outlines, or notes** to reinforce your understanding." It "ensures that you maintain a deep understanding of the material over time and can recall and apply it when needed, such as in exams."

**Recite is spoken and immediate; Review is written and for the long term.** The course's compact restatement: Survey — scan for structural elements; Question — generate thought-provoking questions; Read — actively read, seeking answers; Recite — verbally or mentally summarise; Review — revisit notes and text for long-term retention. Its definition of the whole: **"SQ3R is a systematic and effective reading strategy designed to enhance comprehension and retention of complex texts."**

**The demonstration text**, used at every step, is "Chapter 4: The Renaissance Period in Europe": the Renaissance **spanned roughly from the 14th to the 17th century**, marked a shift **from medieval traditions to humanism and individualism**, and featured **Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and William Shakespeare**. Those are the only historical facts in the course. The SQ3R diagram was an image; its labels survive, its layout does not.

### The four other reading strategies (Week 7 summary)

1. **Context clues** — "hints within a text aiding the reader in inferring the meaning of unfamiliar words." *"The senator's remarks were so vituperative that three delegates walked out"* — the walk-out tells you the word means bitterly abusive.
2. **Distinguishing facts from opinions** — "Critical thinking involves separating **objective, verifiable facts** from **subjective opinions**." *"Nigeria has 36 states"* is a fact; *"Nigeria has too many states"* is an opinion.
3. **Drawing inferences** — "logical conclusions drawn from **explicit details and background knowledge**." *"She came in shaking the rain off her umbrella"* never says it is raining; you infer it.
4. **Understanding explicit and implicit meanings** — **explicit meaning** is "directly stated in the text, requiring no inference"; **implicit meaning** "involves unspoken or suggested information, necessitating reader inference." Together they give understanding "beyond surface-level comprehension."

The summary adds that reading "involves cognitive processes beyond decoding, requiring mental activity for comprehension."

Skills acquired: **Vocabulary Acquisition, Critical Thinking, Inference Drawing, Textual Analysis, Decision-Making, Effective Communication.**
References: **Adler & Van Doren (1972), *How to Read a Book: The Classic Guide to Intelligent Reading*; Armstrong (1982), *Study Is Hard Work*.** These recur as the key references for Weeks 9 and 10.

### Where people go wrong

- **The 3R order.** It is **Read, Recite, Review**. You cannot recite what you have not read, or usefully review what you have not recited.
- **Answering during the Question step.** You ask; you do not answer. Answering happens in Read.
- **Explicit vs implicit.** **Ex**plicit = **ex**pressed. **Im**plicit = **im**plied.
- **Inference vs opinion.** An inference is a logical conclusion from stated details plus background knowledge and can be defended; an opinion is a subjective preference.
- **Treating SQ3R as the definition of a reading strategy.** It is one among several.

### In the exam

- "What does SQ3R stand for?"
- "In which step do you generate your own questions?" — Question, the second step.
- "State the two goals of the Read stage."
- "Differentiate explicit and implicit meaning."
- "Define context clues."

### Word formation processes (Week 8 summary)

**The lecture slides on word formation are not in the material.** Only the summary sheet covers it. The analogy: word formation is **akin to culinary experimentation** — chefs combine ingredients for new recipes, linguists combine elements for new words.

1. **Affixation** — affixes, **including prefixes and suffixes**, modify existing words. *unadulterated, correctly.*
2. **Compounding** — merging whole words. *raincoat.*
3. **Conversion** — alters a word's grammatical category **without affixes**. *email* used as a verb.
4. **Derivation** — adds a **derivational suffix** to create a new word. *teacher.*
5. **Blending** — combines **parts** of words. *brunch.*
6. **Acronyms** — take the **initial letters** of a phrase or title to create a new word. *NASA.*
7. **Borrowing** — introduces words from other languages. *sushi.*

Hook: three ways of *joining* (compounding, blending, acronym), two of *adding* (affixation, derivation), one of *switching* (conversion), one of *importing* (borrowing).

Skills acquired: **Analytical Thinking, Creative Expression, Cultural Sensitivity, Linguistic Adaptability, Critical Reflection, Communication Proficiency.**

**A labelling error:** the Week 8 summary sheet is headed "Topic: Comprehension Strategies" — Week 7's heading — over content about word formation.

### Where people go wrong

- **Affixation vs derivation.** Affixation attaches any affix to modify a word (*correctly*); derivation adds a **derivational suffix** creating a new word, usually of a different class (*teach* → *teacher*).
- **Compounding vs blending.** Compounding joins **whole words** (rain + coat); blending joins **parts** (br- + -unch). **raincoat = compounding, brunch = blending.**
- **Conversion vs derivation.** Conversion changes word class with **no affix**; derivation does it **by adding a suffix**.
- **Acronyms vs abbreviations.** The course defines acronyms as initial letters forming **a new word** — NASA. It does not teach initialisms separately.
- **Didactic vs didacticism.** *Didactic* is the adjective; *didacticism* the practice.
- **Thesis vs dissertation.** They overlap in the course's definitions, but *thesis* has the extra sense of **a claim to be proved** — the sense used in Week 6 ("Identify the Thesis or Main Idea") and Week 12 ("Thesis Establishment").

## Part 12 — Speaking: Prompts, Stress and Delivery (Weeks 9 and 10)

### Speaking prompts

**"Speaking prompts are questions or statements designed to elicit a spoken response from you. They are commonly used in various contexts such as interviews, discussions, language learning, and public speaking. Speaking prompts serve as a stimulus to initiate and guide verbal communication."** They range from open-ended questions to complex scenarios.

### Importance of speaking prompts — nine

1. **Communication Skill Development** — practice articulating thoughts coherently.
2. **Critical Thinking** — analysing information, considering different perspectives, giving well-reasoned responses.
3. **Expressing Ideas Clearly** — clear and concise expression, crucial in professional, academic and everyday settings.
4. **Language Proficiency** — practising **vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation** in context.
5. **Interviews and Assessments** — evaluating the ability to communicate effectively, think on one's feet and present coherently.
6. **Promoting Active Participation** — encouraging contribution in discussions and group settings.
7. **Building Confidence** — regular engagement builds comfort in verbal expression.
8. **Cultural and Social Awareness** — some prompts touch cultural or social issues, developing competence and sensitivity.
9. **Enhancing Listening Skills** — in interactive settings, responding requires actively listening to others.

### Guidelines for responding to speaking prompts — thirteen

1. **Understand the Prompt/Question** — identify key components and specific instructions.
2. **Organise Your Thoughts** — a mental outline or jotted key points before responding.
3. **Introduction** — a concise opening giving context or outlining your main points.
4. **Address the Key Points** — respond directly to the core elements of the prompt.
5. **Provide Examples and Evidence** — concrete examples make a response convincing.
6. **Speak Clearly and Concisely** — avoid jargon; mind your **pace and enunciation**.
7. **Elaborate and Expand** — sufficient detail to fully address the prompt.
8. **Stay Relevant** — no unrelated tangents.
9. **Balance** — consider different angles or counterarguments.
10. **Conclude Effectively** — a brief conclusion reinforcing the key message.
11. **Adapt to the Context** — an academic setting needs a more formal tone than a casual one.
12. **Practice Active Listening** — listen to follow-up questions and adapt.
13. **Manage Time Effectively** — succinct yet comprehensive answers within the allotted time.

Hook by grouping: **before you speak** (1–2); **the shape of the answer** (3, 4, 10); **the quality of the answer** (5–9); **the situation** (11–13). The course closes: "practice is key… Regularly engage in speaking exercises, mock interviews, or discussions."

## Part 13 — Reasoning: Inductive and Deductive (Weeks 10 and 12)

### The opening question

**"A village wishes to estimate the population for the next 15 years. Which reasoning will be appropriate to use, inductive or deductive?"** The answer is **inductive** — you project from observed past patterns to a probable future. Note the figure: **15 years.**

### The two definitions, exactly as given

**Inductive reasoning:** "a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is considered **likely or probable, but not certain**, based on the premises." Plainly: "the method here is to **look for patterns** so that you can draw conclusions or make a generalisation."

**Deductive reasoning:** "a form of reasoning in which the conclusion is **necessarily true if the premises are true**. In other words, if the premises are true, the conclusion must also be true." Plainly: "using a set of data to make a specific conclusion that comes up to be a fact."

**Deduction is certain; induction is probable. Induction goes specific → general; deduction goes general → specific.** Week 12 states the directions outright: "Utilize deductive reasoning to draw **specific conclusions from general premises**. Apply inductive reasoning by **starting with specific observations to make broader generalizations**."

### Inductive reasoning worked out

"Inductive arguments are used to **generalise from specific observations or past experiences**… They involve moving from specific instances to a probable or likely conclusion."

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| **Specific observation** | My swan is white. |
| **Pattern recognition (premise)** | Every observed swan is white. |
| **General conclusion** | Therefore, all swans are white. |

The conclusion is false — black swans exist. That is why induction is only probable. "Inductive reasoning provides conclusions that are probable or likely, but not guaranteed to be true. **The strength of the conclusion depends on the quantity and quality of the evidence.**"

In the disease-outbreak case, two premises — past increases in mosquito activity always brought increases in a mosquito-borne disease, and activity has recently increased — yield the conclusion that **it is likely that** there will be an outbreak. Note the hedge; an inductive conclusion is always hedged.

### Types of inductive reasoning — the three taught

1. **Generalisation** — "a broad statement about a group or category based on specific observations." *After seeing many people in sunglasses and hats on sunny days: "Many people wear sunglasses and hats on sunny days."*
2. **Analogical Reasoning** — "drawing conclusions about one situation or object based on its **similarities** to another." *Cats are good hunters; this new breed shares their characteristics; it is likely a good hunter too.*
3. **Statistical Reasoning** — "drawing conclusions based on **statistical data or probability**." *Based on past results and polling, Candidate A has a **70% probability** of winning.*

### Four further types, named but not taught

1. **Causal inference** — concludes that one thing causes another. 2. **Prediction** — projects a future case from past cases. 3. **Abductive reasoning** — infers the most likely explanation. 4. **Enumerative induction** — generalises about a class from observed members.

The slide says only "find out more about these types", so the glosses above are not course doctrine; the four are examinable as names.

### Deductive reasoning worked out

"Deductive arguments are often used in **mathematics and formal logic**… **The most famous example is a syllogism**, like:

> All humans are mortal. (Premise)
> Socrates is a human. (Premise)
> Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (Conclusion)"

**Syllogism** is also defined in Week 8: "a form of reasoning in which a conclusion is drawn from two given or assumed propositions (premises); a common form of deductive reasoning." On certainty: "Deductive reasoning provides conclusions that are **certain or guaranteed to be true when the premises are true, assuming that the argument is valid**."

| Form | Premise 1 | Premise 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Categorical Syllogism** | All mammals have a backbone. | Whales are mammals. | Therefore, whales have a backbone. |
| **Modus Ponens** | If it's raining, then the ground is wet. | It is raining. | Therefore, the ground is wet. |
| **Mathematical Proof** | The sum of two even numbers is always even. | 2 and 4 are even numbers. | Therefore, the sum of 2 and 4 is even. |

**Modus Ponens** "demonstrates that if you **affirm the antecedent** (the 'if' part of a conditional statement) and the conditional statement is true, then the **consequent** (the 'then' part) must be true." Learn both terms: **antecedent** = the "if" part; **consequent** = the "then" part.

**Modus Tollens**, given under "Types of Deductive Reasoning" — "another valid deductive argument that **denies the consequent**. It follows the pattern **'If A, then B; Not B; therefore, Not A.'**" *"If it's raining, the ground is wet. The ground is not wet. Therefore, it's not raining."*

Hook: **Ponens Puts it forward (affirms). Tollens Takes it away (denies)** — Latin *tollere*, to take away.

### Valid versus sound

**"A deductive argument is considered VALID if the conclusion follows logically from the premises, and it is SOUND if the premises are true. Valid deductive arguments guarantee certainty in the conclusions."** Validity is about **form**; soundness is about **the truth of the premises**. "All cats can fly; Tom is a cat; therefore Tom can fly" is valid and unsound.

### Limitations of inductive reasoning — three

1. **The possibility of sampling bias** 2. **The reliance on incomplete data** 3. **The potential for coincidental patterns**

Plus: inductive conclusions are "contingent on the **quality and quantity of the evidence**."

### The course's summary and references

**"Deductive reasoning provides certainty in conclusions when the argument is valid and the premises are true, while inductive reasoning offers probable conclusions based on patterns, evidence, and generalizations… Deductive reasoning is akin to 'all or nothing' logic, while inductive reasoning acknowledges the nuances and probabilities inherent in many real-world situations."**

References: **Smith (2019), *The Art of Deductive Reasoning*** (deductive); **Johnson & Brown (2020), *Inductive Logic: A Comprehensive Guide*** (inductive). The same two are given for Week 12.

### Reasoning applied to presentations (Week 12 summary)

**The lecture slides on this are not in the material.** The summary sheet gives eight points:

1. **Establish a central thesis** and structure the presentation logically with an **introduction, main points, supporting evidence, counterarguments, and a compelling conclusion**.
2. **Utilize deductive reasoning** to draw specific conclusions from general premises.
3. **Apply inductive reasoning** by starting with specific observations to make broader generalizations.
4. **Be mindful of logical fallacies**, such as **strawman arguments** and **hasty generalizations**.
5. **Eliminate faulty reasoning** to maintain the integrity of the presentation.
6. **Cite information from reliable sources** to build trust with the audience.
7. **Seek external feedback** for constructive criticism.
8. **Reflect** on instances where logical and critical reasoning enhanced persuasiveness and credibility.

The five parts of a logical presentation structure in point 1: **introduction, main points, supporting evidence, counterarguments, conclusion.** The inclusion of **counterarguments** distinguishes it from an ordinary essay outline and echoes the "Balance" guideline of Week 9.

**The two named logical fallacies — the only two in the course.** A **strawman** misrepresents an opponent's position into something weaker, then attacks that: *"My opponent wants to reduce fuel subsidies, so he wants Nigerians to suffer."* A **hasty generalization** draws a broad conclusion from too little evidence: *"Two students from that department failed, so it is poorly taught."* It is inductive reasoning done badly — the "incomplete data" weakness from Week 10.

**The seven-step presentation methodology:** 1. **Purpose Definition** 2. **Audience Analysis** 3. **Thesis Establishment** 4. **Logical Structure Implementation** 5. **Deductive and Inductive Reasoning Application** 6. **Fallacy Avoidance** 7. **Source Citation.**

The framing image: logical reasoning and critical thinking are **"the indispensable compass and map"**. "A presentation devoid of **logical reasoning** may **wander**, while a lack of **critical thinking** may leave the presenter **ill-equipped to spot potential obstacles**." Logic keeps you on course; critical thinking spots the hazards.

Skills acquired: **Audience Adaptability, Strategic Argumentation, Source Evaluation, Critical Thinking Facilitation, Visual Communication Mastery, Adaptability, and Quick Thinking.**

### Where people go wrong

- **Reversing the directions.** **Induction: specific → general. Deduction: general → specific.** Hook: **Deduction Descends** from a general rule to a particular case.
- **Calling an inductive conclusion "true".** It is *probable*, never certain.
- **Valid vs sound.** Valid = correct form. Sound = valid *and* true premises.
- **Modus Ponens vs Modus Tollens.** Ponens affirms the antecedent; Tollens denies the consequent and concludes by denying the antecedent — the denial travels backwards.
- **Categorical syllogism vs modus ponens.** The first is about *categories*; the second about a *conditional*.
- **Analogical vs statistical reasoning.** Analogical argues from *similarity*; statistical from *numbers*.

### In the exam

- "Define inductive reasoning." / "Define deductive reasoning."
- "State the three types of inductive reasoning discussed."
- "What is Modus Tollens? Give its pattern."
- "Distinguish a valid from a sound argument."
- "Name two logical fallacies." — Strawman argument and hasty generalization.
- A scenario question of the village-population type.

## Part 14 — Listening and Note-Taking (Weeks 11 and 12)

### Why listening matters

**"Listening is a crucial skill that plays a fundamental role in various aspects of personal, professional, and social life. Its importance extends across different contexts and can have a significant impact on communication, relationships, and overall success."**

**Hearing versus listening:** **"Hearing is passive and involuntary while listening is an active and voluntary process requiring a conscious effort to interpret and understand spoken messages."**

### The six reasons listening is important

1. **Effective Communication** — "not just hearing words but actively paying attention to and understanding the message being conveyed." Good listeners **respond appropriately, ask relevant questions, and provide thoughtful feedback**.
2. **Building Relationships** — when people **feel heard and understood, trust and rapport are strengthened**; it demonstrates **empathy**.
3. **Conflict Resolution** — it lets parties **express concerns, feelings and viewpoints without interruption**, and helps individuals **identify common ground, find solutions, and prevent misunderstandings**.
4. **Learning and Growth** — attentive listening enables you to **absorb information, gain insights, and acquire new knowledge and skills**.
5. **Leadership Effectiveness** — leaders who listen **make informed decisions, boost morale, and create an inclusive and collaborative work environment**; they are seen as **approachable** and **inspire trust and loyalty**.
6. **Avoiding Miscommunication** — poor listening leads to **misunderstandings, misinterpretations, and conflicts**.

Hook, as a story: listening lets you **communicate**, which builds **relationships**, which lets you **resolve conflict**; it lets you **learn**, which lets you **lead**; and all of it **avoids miscommunication**. The closing claim: **"Listening is a foundational skill that underpins successful communication, relationships, and personal growth."**

### Listening strategies (Week 11) — six

1. **Active listening** — full attention, eye contact, nodding, verbal cues.
2. **Nonverbal communication** — attending to body language and facial expressions.
3. **Empathetic listening** — understanding the speaker's emotions and expressing empathy.
4. **Paraphrasing** 5. **Summarizing** 6. **Note-taking**

The case study adds two further headings — **Cultural Awareness** (recognising and respecting communication differences across cultures) and **Reflective Practice** (regular self-assessment) — and a list within the list, the **practical strategies**: **paraphrasing, summarizing, asking clarifying questions, minimizing distractions, avoiding prejudgment, and note-taking.** The academic case: effective listening is crucial for **understanding course content, following instructions, and grasping key concepts**.

### Active listening (Week 12)

**"Active listening is a fundamental component of effective communication and plays a crucial role in both personal and professional interactions."**

**Three benefits:**
1. **Enhanced Understanding** — grasping "not only the words spoken but also the underlying **emotions, intentions, and context**."
2. **Conflict Resolution** — it lets individuals **acknowledge each other's perspectives**, a crucial step to common ground.

### B. Every definition, in the course's own words

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Listening | The ability to understand, interpret, and respond to spoken language. |
| Speaking | The ability to articulate thoughts, ideas, and information coherently and effectively. |
| Reading | The ability to comprehend, interpret, and analyse written text. |
| Writing | The capacity to convey thoughts, information, and emotions through written language. |
| Hearing vs listening | Hearing is passive and involuntary; listening is active and voluntary, requiring conscious effort to interpret and understand spoken messages. |
| Active vs passive listening | Active listening involves full engagement; passive listening is merely hearing without deep comprehension. |
| Denotative meaning | The literal meaning. |
| Connotative meaning | The implied or suggested meaning; carries cultural and emotional associations. |
| Semiotics | The study of signs and symbols, their interpretation, and their role in communication. |
| Sentence | A group of words that express a complete thought or an idea; must possess a noun or pronoun (subject) and a verb (predicate). |
| Function (of a sentence) | The sentence described by its purpose. |
| Structure (of a sentence) | How a sentence is made up of different parts or elements like subjects, predicates, objects, nouns, verbs. |
| Simple sentence | One independent clause. |
| Compound sentence | Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or a semicolon. |
| Complex sentence | One independent clause and at least one dependent (subordinate) clause. |
| Compound-complex sentence | Two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. |
| Function words | Words with a primarily grammatical function that convey little or no meaning on their own; they connect or build relationships between content words. |
| Content words | Also called lexical words; they carry the main meaning in a sentence. |
| Concord | A set of rules governing the agreement between different elements in a sentence; ensures linguistic harmony. |
| Subject–verb agreement | The number of the subject must match the number of the verb. |
| Rule of Proximity Agreement | The verb or noun must align with the closest subject in sentences with multiple elements. |
| Punctuation | A crucial element of writing that helps convey meaning, clarify sentences, and guide the reader through your text. |
| Paragraph | An individual unit of thought within a written composition. |
| Unity | A singular central theme, with all sentences relating to it; avoiding digression. |
| Coherence | Orderly and logically connected ideas, facilitated by cohesive links like conjunctions and pronouns. |
| Topic sentence | The sentence highlighting the key message of a paragraph; its main idea or central point. |
| Supporting sentences | The other sentences in a paragraph, which support or add details to the topic sentence. |
| Pre-writing | The phase preceding actual composition: generating and organising ideas, establishing a purpose, constructing a preliminary framework. |
| Post-writing | The activities undertaken after the initial draft: a meticulous revision process ensuring coherence, clarity and adherence to academic conventions. |
| Summary | A condensed and concise version of a larger text or document, highlighting the main ideas, key points and essential information. |
| Letter | A written message or communication addressed to a specific person or group. |
| Report | A formal document that presents detailed information, analysis, findings and conclusions on a specific topic or issue. |
| Reading strategies | Techniques and approaches used to enhance reading comprehension, retention and overall effectiveness when engaging with written text. |
| SQ3R | A systematic and effective reading strategy designed to enhance comprehension and retention of complex texts. |
| Context clues | Hints within a text aiding the reader in inferring the meaning of unfamiliar words. |
| Inference | A logical conclusion drawn from explicit details and background knowledge. |
| Explicit meaning | Directly stated in the text, requiring no inference. |
| Implicit meaning | Unspoken or suggested information, necessitating reader inference. |
| Vocabulary expansion | The process of increasing one's repertoire of words and expressions in a language. |
| Speaking prompts | Questions or statements designed to elicit a spoken response. |
| Stress (linguistics) | The emphasis on a particular syllable or segment within a word, phrase or sentence during speech. |
| Public speaking | The act of delivering a speech or presentation to a live audience, effectively conveying information, ideas or opinions. |
| Inductive reasoning | A form of reasoning in which the conclusion is considered likely or probable, but not certain, based on the premises. |
| Deductive reasoning | A form of reasoning in which the conclusion is necessarily true if the premises are true. |
| Valid argument | One in which the conclusion follows logically from the premises. |
| Sound argument | One that is valid and whose premises are true. |
| Syllogism | A form of reasoning in which a conclusion is drawn from two given or assumed propositions (premises); a common form of deductive reasoning. |
| Modus Ponens | Affirms the antecedent: If A then B; A; therefore B. |
| Modus Tollens | Denies the consequent: If A, then B; Not B; therefore, Not A. |
| Note-taking | A critical skill for students and professionals, particularly when listening to lectures, presentations or spoken content. |
| Cornell method | Dividing your paper into sections for notes, cues and a summary; summarising the main ideas shortly after the lecture. |

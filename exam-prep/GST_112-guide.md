## Part 0 — Orientation

The course is one argument told in twelve weeks. Nigeria's peoples were already in contact before Europeans came; European trade and then European rule forced them into one state; the way that state was built left problems the country still pays for; and citizens today have rights, duties and choices that decide whether it gets better.

Five blocks:

1. **The peoples.** Culture, society, nation-building; who lives here; pre-colonial intergroup relations. Weeks 1–2.
2. **The intrusion.** Portuguese contact, the slave trade, abolition, legitimate trade, the annexation of Lagos, company rule. Week 3.
3. **The making of the state.** Four constitutions, the parties, independence, the First Republic, the coups, the civil war, military rule. Weeks 4–5.
4. **The state today.** Economy, human rights, citizenship, reorientation campaigns. Weeks 6–9.
5. **The society today.** Pop culture, EndSARS, Makers of Nigeria, social vices. Weeks 10–12.

Four links the examiner likes. **Slavery led to colonialism**: when slaving ended, Europe needed Africans to stay in Africa growing cash crops and buying finished goods. **Colonialism produced the national question**: regions existed before a nation did, so after 1960 "Who are Nigerians?" had no answer, and power ran through the region, breeding fear of domination. **Military rule produced today's decay**: corruption, contempt for the rule of law, human rights abuse. **Economics produces the vices**: unemployment feeds kidnapping; militarisation and school decay feed cultism.

## Part 1 — Culture, Society and Nation-Building (Week 1)

**Culture** is a complex concept encompassing traditional behaviours, beliefs, practices and values. **Nation-building** is mobilising resources for socio-economic and political development and constructing a national identity. **National integration** is welding diverse groups into one political community.

**The three components of culture:**

1. **Technological** — material culture (tools, buildings, crafts).
2. **Sociological** — institutions and norms (family, chieftaincy, law).
3. **Ideological** — beliefs and values (religion, morality).

Hook: **T-S-I, "Things, Structures, Ideas."**

**The two forms of society: communal and associational.** They differ in three named respects: **division of labour, social interactions, and traditions.** A lineage-based farming village is communal; Lagos, organised by contract and occupation, is associational.

**Two named theories.** **Karl Marx** envisioned a communist society with collective ownership of the means of production and distribution of wealth. **Functionalism** views society as an organism in which each component plays a crucial role.

**Colonialism** altered three named things in African societies: **governance, culture, and family structures.** The worked example is pre-colonial Yoruba communities, whose family dynamics and social structures were reshaped by new systems.

**Where people go wrong.** A market building is technological, its trading rules sociological, its patron deity ideological — students dump everything into "sociological". Communal versus associational is defined by the three criteria, not by rural-versus-urban feel. Marx prescribes ownership; functionalism describes fit. Nation-building is the whole project; national integration is specifically the welding of groups.

**In the exam.** "The three fundamental components of culture are ___." "Which theory views society as an organism where each component plays a crucial role?" (Functionalism.) Essay: challenges to national integration — ethnic diversity, colonial legacy, the civil war, and initiatives such as NYSC and "No Victor, No Vanquished".

## Part 2 — Peoples of Nigeria and Pre-Colonial Intergroup Relations (Week 2)

**Inter-group relations** is the interaction between two or more distinct linguistic and cultural groups. **The two factors for identifying groups: culture and language.**

**Figures:** over **348 language groups** and **374 ethnic groups**. History dating to **39,000 BC**. Nigeria is the **most populous country in Africa**. *Contradiction: the week 5 summary says "over 250 distinct groups". Give 374, and note 250 is used loosely elsewhere.*

### The four indicators of interaction

**1. Traditions of origin. 2. Conflict. 3. Trade and commerce. 4. Religion.** Hook: **T-C-T-R, "The Cousins Traded Rituals."**

**Traditions of origin.** Kanuri and Hausa share origin myths and **both trace to Baghdad**: the **Kanuri from Saif**, the **Hausa from Bayajidda**. Bayajidda settled among the Kanuri and married **the Mai's daughter**; his legend shows ties between **Gwari, Nupe and Yoruba**. The **Kisra legend** also indicates Yoruba ties — **Kisra originated in Baghdad**, founded the **Yoruba and Borgu Kingdoms**, and considered the **Ooni of Ife** his brother. Others: the **Yoruba–Benin connection through Oranmiyan and Eweka**; the Igbo **Umu-Ezechinma** connection with the **Edo**; the **Isoko** tracing roots to **Edo**, another section to the **Igbo**.

**Conflict.** Wars between **Oyo and Nupe**, producing a **Bariba–Yoruba alliance** against the Nupe. **Kano fought Katsina for control of trade routes.** The **Jukun** extended influence by military conquest over the **Kanuri, Hausa, Igala, Idoma and Ebira**. **Benin** conquered towards **Auchi, Idah, Itsekiri, Urhobo, Isoko and Okpe** and campaigned as far as **Eko (Lagos)**; early Lagos kings such as **Ado and Asipa** were from Benin. **Igala and Nsukka Igbo** fought over territory, which forged political ties: the **Illah speak Igbo but their masquerades speak Igala**. The **Ikwo, Ezzi and Ezza** are warlike, though **there is no record that Ezza defeated an Idoma sub-group called Agila**. Cultural diffusion followed war: **Emowa and Isoko** sub-groups say their gods are still located at **Patani, an Ijaw town**.

**Trade and commerce.** **The Jukun exchanged salt for firewood from the Tiv.** **Kanem Bornu** played a major role in the **trans-Saharan trade**, through which the **Yoruba and Nupe** participated. **Ibibio land served as a trade corridor** between the hinterland **Igbo** and the coastal **Efik and Ijaw**. **The Hausa traded with the Tuareg, Kanuri, Gonja, Oyo, Igala and Kwararafa.** **The Nupe exchanged shea butter, bangles, brasswork, potash and straw hats with the Yoruba.**

**Religion — six shared practices:** 1. male priests and female priestesses; 2. chanting songs and incantations; 3. slaughtering of animals and foods as sacrifices to the gods; 4. pantheon worship; 5. nature worship; 6. masquerades. **Five shared deities and institutions:** 1. the Yoruba **Igunu** is traceable to the Nupe **Ndakogbaya**; 2. **Ibini Ukpabi** has Ibibio origins but is today common among the Igbo; 3. **Ekpe and Ekpo** originated among the Efik but are today popular among some Igbo subgroups; 4. similar religious symbols exist between the **Jos people and the Jukun**; 5. the **Bori Cult** is common throughout Hausa land.

### The six geo-political zones

| Zone | Peoples | Course note |
|---|---|---|
| 1. North West | Hausa, Fulani | Heritage traced to conquest and assimilation; common language and Islamic faith |
| 2. North East | Kanuri, Jukun | Unique socio-political systems |
| 3. North Central | Borgu, Nupe, Igala, Tiv | **Most ethnically diverse** zone |
| 4. South West | Yoruba | Distinct subgroups; lineage and leadership |
| 5. South East | Igbo | Democratic **age-grade system** |
| 6. South South | Edo, Itsekiri, Ijaw, Efik | Democratic systems, influential kingdoms |

**Where people go wrong.** **Saif is Kanuri, Bayajidda is Hausa** — the classic swap. Kisra is a separate legend, also from Baghdad. **348 languages, 374 ethnic groups** — more peoples than tongues. **North Central**, not South South, is the most diverse zone. The Ezza/Agila statement is a **negative**: if an MCQ says Ezza defeated Agila, it is false.

**In the exam.** "Which two factors are used to identify groups?" (Culture and language.) "State four indicators of pre-colonial intergroup relations." Essay: the nature of intergroup relations, structured by the four indicators with two named examples each.

## Part 3 — The Slave Trade and Its Abolition (Week 3)

**Slaves** are persons who were bought, stolen, captured in wars and pawned, with expectations to render services to their master, who exercises full power over them, even their life and death. **The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade** is the inhuman buying and selling of humans from Africa across the Atlantic to the Americas and Europe to serve as slaves at both the plantations and homes of their masters. **Phillip Curtin** calls it "the largest intercontinental migration in western History, spanning a period of 300 years in which the Africans in mass numbers crossed the Atlantic". **The Asiento** is the Spanish system granting the right to trade in slaves in the New World.

**The triangular trade: Africa (source); Americas (destination); Europeans (organisers).** The crossing is the **Atlantic or Middle Passage**.

**Portuguese origins.** The trade began with the **Portuguese in the 15th century** after they developed maritime capabilities; landing ashore in Africa, they **seized people** for work in **Portugal and Spain**. **The Old World was divided into two halves by the Pope: Africa ceded to Portugal, the Americas ceded to Spain.** Portuguese sailors were motivated by the desire for **new markets**, and significantly improved **seafaring techniques and ship designs** (the **caravel**). **Portugal dominated world trade for nearly 200 years** through technology and access to **fabrics, spices and gold**. Their coastal trade affected the **growth of brass casting in Benin** and the **emergence of trading in the Lagos Lagoon**. When **Christopher Columbus** reached the Americas and sugar plantations were established, European traders realised they could make enormous profits from African labour. Africans were taken to **North America, Central America, South America and the Caribbean**, for **gold and silver mines** and plantations growing **cotton, sugar and tobacco**.

**The Asiento in sequence.** 1. **Before 1600 the Portuguese were the chief slave traders in West Africa**, taking Africans to **Brazil** or selling them to **Spanish settlers** in the Americas. 2. The Asiento **later became transferable**, and other Europeans, including the Portuguese, began to get the licence. 3. As the slave population swelled they could mobilise and rebel, leading to the **uprising in Puerto Rico in 1527**.

**Who led, century by century.** 1. **Portuguese** — chief traders before 1600. 2. **17th century: the Dutch** joined the Portuguese as leading slave-trading nations. 3. **18th century: the British** were the biggest, their ships carrying **more than half** of the total slaves taken from Africa. 4. **19th century:** European nations were no longer active, but **Europeans who had settled in the New World** organised a large part of the trade. Hook: **P-D-B-S.**

**Conduct of the trade.** It began as **direct attacks** by Europeans; raiding later became unsafe as Africans defended themselves, so they **switched to trade**, since they also needed gold and other commodities, and the Portuguese began **exchanging manufactured goods for slaves and African commodities**. **Nigerian chiefs forced Europeans to pay tax on each slave** at the trade ports. The journey took **5 to 8 weeks** in very terrible conditions. The space allotted to each slave was **6ft by 1ft by 4 inches**, so that a ship carried **between 451 and 600 slaves**. Slaves suffered **smallpox, dysentery and ophthalmia**. At first the victims were **coastal people**; later raiders travelled into the interior, and **by the end of the 18th century the stretch of the Niger River had been combed for slaves**, who arrived at the coast exhausted.

**The numbers.** **No one knows the number of Africans sold.** **Estimates have it that over 15 million reached the Americas**, and **Paul Lovejoy notes that over 7 million died in transit**; a high percentage also died during the middle passage, and many were killed during the brutal process of obtaining them.

**The named scholars.** 1. **Curtin** — the definition and the 300 years. 2. **Lovejoy** — the 7 million, and: "many communities relocated as far from the slavers' route as possible. In the process, their technological and economic development was hindered as they devoted their energy to hiding and defending themselves." 3. **Wole Soyinka** — Africa's underdevelopment is the outcome of "the twin evils of slavery and colonialism inflicted by the Western World." 4. **Walter Rodney** — shares this view. 5. **Washington Alcott** — the trade caused the forced removal of millions "including a large percentage of skilled tradesmen and women from a range of occupations who were making their contribution to African societies." 6. **Claude Ake** — "it was mainly because the development of productive forces stagnated at a certain stage in African history that the colonisers were able to subordinate the continent."

**Four implications for Africa:** 1. **depopulation**; 2. **destruction of Africa's socio-economic and political structures**; 3. **unbridled warfare**; 4. **insecurity leading to underdevelopment**.

**The end of the trade.** 1. A **movement emerged in Britain, the United States and parts of Europe** to abolish it. 2. The economic motive: **rapid economic and technological development in the metropole** removed the need for slave labour; Europe needed **Africans to stay in Africa to grow cash crops** for its industries and to be a **ready market for finished goods**. 3. **The British began to seize slave ships since 1792** and repatriated them to **Sierra Leone**. 4. **In 1833 a bill to abolish slavery was sponsored by Thomas Fowell Buxton, a British parliamentarian.** 5. Buxton advocated that **for the slave trade to stop, it must stop from Africa** — whose implication was the **annexation of Lagos in 1861**.

**Where people go wrong.** **1792 seizures, 1833 Buxton's bill, 1861 annexation** — three events, in that order. **15 million arrived; 7 million died in transit.** **Curtin gave the definition; Lovejoy gave the 7 million and the relocation quotation.** **Soyinka and Rodney blame the twin evils; Ake blames stagnated productive forces.**

**In the exam.** "According to Paul Lovejoy, how many died in transit?" (Over 7 million.) "Who sponsored the 1833 bill?" (Thomas Fowell Buxton.) "The space allotted to each slave was ___." (6ft by 1ft by 4 inches.) Essay: "The end of the slave trade ushered in colonialism" — use the cash-crop and market motive.

## Part 4 — Legitimate Trade, Company Rule and Colonial Rule (Week 3)

**"Legitimate" trade** is trade with Europe in commodities rather than people: **palm oil, ivory and other "legitimate" commodities**.

|---|---|
| By 1840 | The **Bight of Benin** was the last centre of the slave trade in West Africa; **Lagos the leading slave port** |
| 1849 | **Beecroft appointed** for control and supervision — **the beginning of Britain's direct influence in Nigeria** |
| 1851 | Britain signed a treaty with **Kosoko** to end the slave trade; his refusal brought the **bombardment** |

*The course never states the year the charter was revoked. Do not invent one. (Not in the course material: it took effect on 1 January 1900.) Note also that "1884, after the Berlin Conference" is chronologically tight, since the conference ran into 1885 — give 1884 as the course does.*

**The Lagos sequence.** Rulership was disputed between **Kosoko and Akitoye**. Britain treated with Kosoko in 1851; he refused; Lagos was bombarded; **on 29 December 1851 Akitoye regained the throne and Kosoko fled to Epe**. Immediately after the bombardment more ships came to buy palm oil and other produce — the rise of legitimate trade. Annexation followed in **1861**.

**Company rule.** The **United African Company** was created by amalgamation under **Sir George Goldie-Taubman**, later known as the **National African Company** after buying **two rival French firms**. Big companies that took charge of the Delta trade: **the West African Company, Alexander Miller Bros, Mr James Pinnock, and the Central African Company of London**. The **RNC operated as both a trading company and an administrator** and **paid dividends solely from trade profits**.

**The RNC's four monopoly tactics:** 1. **bought competing companies**; 2. **chased them with high taxes**; 3. **monopolised the supply of imported goods**; 4. **manipulated the prices of African produce**.

**Three named critics:** 1. **Harry Johnston**; 2. **Ajayi Crowther**; 3. **Hoenigsberg**. The criticisms did little damage because the RNC had the favour of the **Colonial Secretary, Salisbury**.

**Four causes of the RNC's decline:** 1. **alliances of opponents of the RNC**; 2. **appointment of Sir Ralph Moor as Consul of the ORP in 1891**; 3. **price war**; 4. **smuggling** — plus the two 1895 events above.

**The Oil River Protectorate's seven districts:** 1. **Bonny**; 2. **Forcados**; 3. **Old Calabar**; 4. **New Calabar**; 5. **Benin**; 6. **Brass**; 7. **Opobo**. There was a **request for the amalgamation of the ORP and RNC and opposition to it**; the ORP was **renamed the Niger Coast Protectorate in 1893**.

**Where people go wrong.** **1881 refused, 1882 capital raised but still blocked, 1886 charter granted** — the slides' own quiz answer is **1886**. **Salisbury favoured the RNC; Chamberlain's appointment weakened it.** **Hewett did the 1884 treaty tour; Moor was ORP Consul from 1891.** **1861 is a double date** (annexation and Laird's death). **UAC then NAC then RNC**, Goldie-Taubman throughout.

**In the exam.** "In what year did the Royal Niger Company receive its Royal Charter?" (1886.) "Name the seven districts of the Oil River Protectorate." Essay: trace the events from the end of the slave trade to company rule, walking the timeline.

## Part 5 — Constitutional Development, 1914 to 1954 (Week 4)

**The Nigerian Council, 1914.** **Lord Lugard set it up in 1914.** It **performed only advisory functions**, was made up of **36 members, including six unofficial members**, and **laid the foundation for constitutional development in Nigeria**. It is not a constitution — it is what came before the first one.

| | Clifford 1922 | Richards 1947–51 | Macpherson 1951 | Lyttleton 1954 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Author | **Sir Hugh Clifford** (took over 1919) | **Sir Arthur Richards** | **Macpherson**, Governor from 1948 | **Sir Oliver Lyttleton**, a **Colonial Secretary, not a governor** |
| Innovation | **First written constitution; the elective principle** | **Regionalism** | **Wide consultation; House of Representatives** | **Federalism** |
| Legislature | Council of **45**: **26 official (Europeans), 15 nominated, 4 elected Nigerians (3 Lagos, 1 Calabar)** | Council of **45**, of whom **28 were Nigerians, 4 elected**; a regional House of Assembly in each of the three regions | **House of Representatives, 136 members**, made supreme | Centre and regions each with law-making jurisdiction over allocated areas |
| Chambers | Lagos and the South under legislative-council law; **the North under proclamation** | **Bicameral in the north**, unicameral elsewhere | **Bicameral in the Western and Northern Regions** | **Judiciary regionalised** |
| Lagos | **Lagos Colony a separate unit** | Limited franchise for Lagos and Calabar | **Lagos removed from the Western Region, made an independent area** | **Lagos made a Federal Capital Territory** |

**Clifford, 1922.** Political division into three units: **Lagos Colony, Southern Provinces, Northern Provinces**. **Cameroon became part of Nigeria.** **Significance (three):** 1. **it introduced the elective principle**; 2. **this led to the emergence of political parties**; 3. **it encouraged the establishment of newspapers**. **Criticism (four):** 1. **the Governor had veto power**; 2. **there was no local input in the drafting**; 3. **the council was predominantly white**; 4. **it laid the foundation for disintegration, as Northern Nigeria was not represented in the council**.

**The interlude, 1926–1943.** 1. **Sir Graham Thompson**, took over in **1926**, with **no concessions to his credit**. 2. **Sir Donald Cameron**, Governor **1930–1936**: **abolished the office of the Lieutenant Governor**; **abolished the provincial court**; **introduced the doctrine of separation of power**. 3. **Bernard Bourdillon**, took over in **1935**: **prepared the ground for a new constitution**; **appealed to the northern leaders to join the legislative council**. *The slide's own dates overlap — Cameron to 1936, Bourdillon from 1935. Reproduce as given.*

**Richards, 1947.** It **introduced regionalism**, provided **bicameral legislation in the north** while others were unicameral, and kept a **central legislative council for the whole of Nigeria**. **Significance (three):** 1. **provision for a regional House of Assembly in each of the three regions**; 2. **of the 45 members in the Council, 28 were Nigerians, of whom 4 were elected**; 3. **it tried to unite the country by bringing the north and south under one legislative body**. The **Executive Council was still one at the centre**, and **in 1949 two Nigerians were appointed to it**. **Criticism (two):** 1. **the locals were not consulted in drafting a constitution to govern them**; 2. **it remained a limited franchise for Lagos and Calabar**.

**Macpherson, 1951.** He **went into wide consultations with Nigerians, avoiding the mistakes of his predecessors**. The Legislative Council was renamed the **House of Representatives**, with **136 members**, and **made supreme**; **members had to go through the regional legislature to be selected**. The **Executive Council** comprised **Chief Commissioners (3, one for each region), the Attorney General, the Financial Secretary, the Director of Medical Services, and the Director of Education**. **Nigerians were made ministers but without ministries or portfolios.** The **Central Executive Council was renamed the Council of Ministers**, constituted with **the Governor as Prime Minister and twelve ministers**. **Merit:** it **led to the formation of more political parties (AG, NPC)**. **Demerit:** it **did not last beyond three years because of Enahoro's motion for independence**.

**Lyttleton, 1954.** It was argued at the **House of Commons** that **the three regions could not work effectively in a closely knit structure like the existing one**. It **introduced the federal system**, sharing power between central and regional governments; **Lieutenant Governors in the regions were to be addressed as Governors**; **the regions and the centre had jurisdiction in law-making over areas allocated to them**; and it **provided for the position of Premier as the leader of the government in the regions**. **Significance (four):** 1. **the judiciary was regionalised**; 2. **Lagos was made a Federal Capital Territory**; 3. **the ministers were to control the departments in their ministries**; 4. **revenue allocation was based on derivation**.

**Where people go wrong.** **Both the Clifford and Richards councils have 45 members** — the composition, not the total, is the answer. **Four elected members appears in both**; under Clifford it is **3 Lagos, 1 Calabar**. One innovation each: **E-R-C-F, "Elective, Regional, Consultative, Federal."** **Macpherson took Lagos out of the Western Region; Lyttleton made it the FCT.** **136 = Macpherson's House; 45 = Clifford and Richards councils; 36 = the 1914 Nigerian Council.** **Bicameralism moves**: the north only under Richards, the Western and Northern Regions under Macpherson. **Lyttleton alone was a Colonial Secretary, not a governor.**

**In the exam.** "Who devised the first written constitution of Nigeria?" (Clifford, 1922.) "Which constitution introduced the federal system?" (Lyttleton, 1954.) "How many members were in the Macpherson House of Representatives?" (136.) Essay: significance and criticisms of the Clifford Constitution — three and four, numbered.

## Part 6 — Political Parties and the Quest for Independence (Week 4)

**1. NNDP.** Formed **1923**; **based in Lagos**; **founded the Lagos Daily News**; **founded by Herbert Macaulay**. It **won all three seats allocated to Lagos in 1923, 1928 and 1933**.

**2. NYM.** Formed **1935**. **Ernest Ikoli and Samuel Akisanya** were famous party leaders. It **began as the Lagos Youth Movement** and became the NYM after **Azikiwe, Awolowo, Abayomi** and others joined. **In 1938 the party won all elective seats from the NNDP.** **Internal party disputes led to its destruction in 1944.** **Aims (three):** 1. **to fight for the improvement of Yaba Higher College by getting it affiliated to a British University**; 2. **to gain independence for Nigeria**; 3. **to work towards the unity of Nigeria**. **Achievements (four):** 1. **it was the first nationalist movement with a national outlook**; 2. **it promoted national unity and consciousness**; 3. **it established a newspaper called the Daily Service**; 4. **it set the machinery in motion for the political emancipation of Nigeria**.

**3. NCNC.** Formed **August 1944**. **The first president was Herbert Macaulay and the first General Secretary was Nnamdi Azikiwe; Azikiwe became President after Macaulay died.** **Aims (four):** 1. **to achieve self-rule for Nigeria**; 2. **to spread political education**; 3. **to arouse political consciousness**; 4. **to provide a means of expression for Nigerians**. **Achievements (four):** 1. **it helped in the struggle for self-government in Nigeria**; 2. **it introduced and published the West African Pilot**; 3. **it raised funds to send a protest delegation against the 1946 constitution to London**; 4. **it formed the first regional government of the Eastern Region**.

**4. Action Group.** Formerly **Egbe Omo Oduduwa**, formed by **Awolowo, the Ooni and Ajasin in 1945**. **The AG was formed in Ibadan in 1951.** **Aims (two):** 1. **to mobilise Western Nigerians to forestall the NCNC's control of the Western Region**; 2. **to cooperate with other nationalist parties to win independence for Nigeria**.

**5. NPC.** **A conservative party established in 1951**, which **emerged from a cultural organisation, Jamiyya Mutanen Arewa**. Founders: **the late Sir Alhaji Ahmadu Bello, the Sardauna of Sokoto (President), Tafawa Balewa, Yahaya Gusau, and Aminu Kano**. **Objectives (three):** 1. **to unite the peoples of Northern Nigeria**; 2. **to retain northern regional autonomy**; 3. **to enhance the power of traditional Muslim rulers**.

**Criticism of Nigerian political parties (four):** 1. **ethnocentric syndrome**; 2. **lack of political ideology**; 3. **weakened the central government**; 4. **sacrificed national interest for ethnic interest**.

**How parties advanced independence (four):** 1. **establishment of newspapers**; 2. **sensitisation of Nigerians**; 3. **protest**; 4. **call for independence**.

**Newspapers:** NNDP — **Lagos Daily News**; NYM — **Daily Service**; NCNC — **West African Pilot**.

**Where people go wrong.** **1951 is a triple year**: the AG, the NPC, and the Macpherson Constitution that provoked them. **Egbe Omo Oduduwa (1945) is the ancestor; the AG (1951) is the party.** **Macaulay founded the NNDP and was the NCNC's first president; Azikiwe was its first General Secretary.** **The NYM died in 1944, the year the NCNC was born.** *Contradiction: the week 11 deck says the NNDP joined forces with the NYM in the late 1930s to form the NCNC, while week 4 dates the NCNC to August 1944 — use 1944.* *Second contradiction: the week 11 deck calls Balewa's party the "Northern People's Party"; the correct name in week 4 is the **Northern People's Congress**.*

**In the exam.** "Who founded the NNDP and in what year?" (Macaulay, 1923.) "Which newspaper did the NYM establish?" (Daily Service.) "The NPC emerged from which cultural organisation?" (Jamiyya Mutanen Arewa.)

## Part 7 — Independence, the First Republic and the Civil War (Week 5)

**Nigeria achieved independence from British colonial rule on 1 October 1960.** **The national question** then emerged: **What is Nigeria? Who are Nigerians? How do we develop a meaningful national identity?** Structurally, **the regional system posed a challenge to national consciousness, and access to national power came from first holding power in the region, which promoted the fear of domination.**

Efforts to bring Nigerians closer **politically, economically and culturally**, through a **strong central state and a state-run economy focused on development**, failed because of five named political vices: 1. **official corruption**; 2. **rigged elections**; 3. **ethnic baiting**; 4. **bullying**; 5. **thuggery**.

**Art and the national question.** Three writers: 1. **Chinua Achebe, *Things Fall Apart* (1958)**; 2. **Amos Tutuola, *The Palm-Wine Drinkard* (1952)**; 3. **Wole Soyinka, *A Dance of the Forest* (1960)**. **The plays and the universities had a multi-ethnic make-up, which fostered unity.** Three sculptors who **drew inspiration from Nigeria's pre-colonial past and used traditional design motifs**: 1. **Uche Okeke**; 2. **Susanne Wenger**; 3. **Felix Idubor**.

**Economic development.** **The expansion of formal education was part of the plan.** **The First National Development Plan (FNDP) of 1962–1968 focused on agriculture, industry and education.** Between **1960 and 1966**: 1. **agriculture 63.4% fell to 55.6% (GNP)**; 2. **manufacturing 3.6% to 6.2% (GNP)**; 3. **mining 0.9% to 4.8% (GNP)**; 4. **distribution of goods 9.1% to 14%**; 5. **national income grew at a 5% average**; 6. **per capita increased from $48.1 million to $53.8 million** (*the slide's wording is garbled — a per-capita figure cannot be in millions; the examinable numbers are 48.1 and 53.8*). **The military coup of 1966 led to the end of development planning efforts.**

**Development corporations.** **Richards's constitution was the first to introduce regionalism**, adopted during the First Republic: **Western Nigeria Development Corporation, Eastern Nigeria Development Corporation, Northern Nigeria Development Corporation**. Comparative advantage: **West — cocoa, timber; East — palm oil; North — groundnut. The regions grew at their own pace.**

**Politics of the First Republic — nine named crises:** 1. **election of 1959**; 2. **fear of dominance**; 3. **quota system**; 4. **disproportionate project allocation**; 5. **Western region crisis**; 6. **creation of the Mid-Western region**; 7. **the census crisis of 1962–3**; 8. **the 1964 election crisis, UPGA vs NNA**; 9. **the Western Region election of 1965**.

**The first coup, 15 January 1966.** Led by the **"five majors" — Nzeogwu, Ifeajuna, Okafor, Anuforo and Ademoyega**. They claimed **tribalism and corruption** as reasons. **Several northern leaders and military officers were killed, a number of notable politicians in the west, and very few easterners.** **Many people consider it an Igbo coup.** **The coup failed** as the leaders could not seize power, and power devolved to the commanding officer of the army, **Major General Aguiyi-Ironsi** (*one deck calls him Johnson, the other John — write Aguiyi-Ironsi*).

**Ironsi.** His government was poised to **restore law and order, maintain essential services, eradicate regionalism and tribalism, and end corruption**. He **appointed military governors to the regions, one of whom was Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu, who headed the eastern region**. **Ironsi's gaffes (four):** 1. **the plotters killed very few easterners; the majority were northerners and some Yorubas**; 2. **Ironsi released the coup plotters**; 3. **he surrounded himself with Igbo advisers, accelerating the promotion of Igbo officers**; 4. **Decree No. 34** (the Unification Decree).

**The countercoup, 29 July 1966.** **A group of northern NCOs and officers** struck; **Ironsi was captured and killed in Ibadan**; **Lieutenant Colonel Yakubu Gowon was selected as Head of State** and **immediately announced the repeal of Decree 34**; **Ojukwu and Gowon faced off on seniority**; Igbos were targeted in the north, which led to a **pogrom**.

**The pogrom and secession.** **Between 80,000 and 100,000 easterners were massacred in the north**, which sparked revenge killings of northerners in the East and a **mass return of Igbos to the East**. **This led to Ojukwu's quest for secession; Gowon was determined to keep the status quo.** Negotiations followed, **one of which was the Aburi Accord**. As negotiations failed, **Ojukwu declared the East's independence on 30 May 1967, resulting in war.**

**Three reasons the Federal Government could not allow secession:** 1. **belief in the practicability of Nigerian unity**; 2. **fragmentation of Nigeria into small states**; 3. **the prospect of Biafra leaving with oil-producing states**. The war, **sometimes called the Biafran War, lasted two and a half years**. Immediately after the declaration, **Gowon declared a state of emergency and announced the creation of states**.

**State creation and the squeeze.** **Twelve states were created out of the existing regions. Three states were carved out of the Eastern region, and only one was predominantly Igbo.** The design was to **isolate the Igbos** and stiffen the Biafran economy by two measures: 1. **blockade of the coastline**; 2. **currency change** (dated to **January 1968** in the week 9 case study). The twin policy caused **malnutrition, inflation and economic strangulation**, which was believed would lead to an internal uprising **but instead led to international sympathy and support for Biafra**.

**International intervention.** **Ojukwu's Biafra used pictures of starving children as proof of genocide**, and the propaganda proved effective; **international support helped prolong the war**. 1. **The OAU refused to treat the war as a civil war but rather as a war between two independent nations.** 2. **The US and UK chose to be neutral.** 3. **The USSR supported the FMG and supplied aircraft and advisers.** 4. **Four OAU member states formally recognised Biafra in 1968: Tanzania, Gabon, Ivory Coast and Zambia.** 5. **France and Portugal provided Biafra with supplies and logistics.** 6. **Israel also shared its sentiments for Biafra.** 7. **China showed support but with no serious material support.** 8. **The Catholic Church and the International Red Cross provided humanitarian aid to Biafrans.**

**The end.** **By January 1970 Biafra had been overrun by federal troops, leading to its collapse. Ojukwu eventually fled to Ivory Coast, and Effiong surrendered to Gowon in Lagos on 12 January 1970.** **The war had taken the lives of up to 3 million Nigerians, leaving another 3 million displaced.** **Gowon declared "no winner, no vanquished"** and that there were **"no winners or losers in the war between brothers"**.

**The 3 Rs — reconstruction, reconciliation and rehabilitation.** **Reconciliation** sought to bridge the divides between ethnic groups, emphasising forgiveness and dialogue; **reconstruction** focused on rebuilding war-torn infrastructure; **rehabilitation** provided humanitarian aid and livelihood support. **The National Rehabilitation Commission was established to handle reconstruction and rehabilitation, primarily focusing on the Eastern States.** *The week 5 deck orders them reconstruction–reconciliation–rehabilitation; the week 9 summary orders them reconciliation–reconstruction–rehabilitation. The three words are identical.* **The course's verdict: the 3 Rs failed**, hindered by **currency exchange and property disputes**, and **the Igbos have continually suffered marginalisation owing to the war to this day**. **Rwanda provides a working template** for post-war management.

**Where people go wrong.** **15 January 1966 (first coup), 29 July 1966 (countercoup), 29 July 1975 (against Gowon)** — July 29 appears twice, nine years apart. **30 May 1967 to 12 January 1970** dates the war. **80,000–100,000 is the pogrom; up to 3 million is the war dead, plus 3 million displaced.** **The USSR backed the Federal Government; France, Portugal, Israel and China leaned to Biafra; the US and UK were neutral.**

"What are the 3 Rs?" Essay: causes of the civil war, from the national question through the First Republic crises and the two coups to the failure of Aburi.

## Part 8 — The Military in Nigerian Politics (Week 5)

**The first military coup in Africa occurred in Egypt in 1952.** **Nigeria has seen six coups and two attempted coups.** **The Nigerian military inherited the British tradition of professionalism and neutrality at all times**, but **the post-Independence era witnessed the politicisation of the military**. **Nigeria experienced its first coup on 15 January 1966.** *The deck then numbers eight events; the two that failed are Dimka (1976) and Orkar (1990), so "six coups and two attempted coups" is consistent with the list of eight.*

**Coup 1 — the named dead:** 1. **the Prime Minister, Abubakar Tafawa Balewa**; 2. **the Sardauna of Sokoto**; 3. **Finance Minister, Festus Okotie-Eboh**; 4. **Premier of the Western Region, Ladoke Akintola**; 5. **Commandant, Second Brigade, Brigadier Zakariya Maimalari**; 6. **Commandant, First Brigade, Brigadier S. A. Ademulegun**; 7. **Commandant of the Nigeria Defence Academy, R. A. Shodeinde**; 8. **"and lots more"** (the slide's own words). The coup was **mainly carried out by officers of Igbo extraction**; the plotters **perceived it as a revolution** and **attributed it to the bigotry of politicians in the First Republic**. **Nwafor Orizu, the acting President of the Senate, voluntarily transferred power to Aguiyi-Ironsi**, who **suspended the constitution and established an interim federal military government**.

**Coup 2 — four reasons for the countercoup:** 1. **the fear of Igbo domination in the north**; 2. **Ironsi's compromising management of the first coup plotters**; 3. **Ironsi's policies, e.g. the Unification Decree**; 4. **the need to exert revenge for the first coup.** **Gowon led the new government until 1975.**

**Coup 3.** At the **end of the civil war in 1970, Gowon promised to terminate the military on 1 October 1976**; **in 1974 he suddenly announced a postponement** of the transition. Officers **led by Colonel Joseph Nanven Garba** overthrew him on **29 July 1975** in a **bloodless coup**.

**Coup 4.** **Dimka assassinated Murtala Muhammed**, citing **perceived corruption and maladministration**, and **was arrested without trial**. The coup **failed as it did not have the support of senior military officers**; **Lieutenant-General Obasanjo assumed office as head of state** and **returned power to the civilian government of Shehu Shagari**.

**Coup 5.** Buhari was **the General Officer Commanding the 3rd Armoured Division in Jos**. The Shagari government was widely criticised for being **"inept and corrupt"**, which was the primary reason cited for the coup.

**Coup 6.** Babangida was **Chief of Army Staff** and said the coup became imperative because of the **"rigid and uncompromising" nature of the Buhari regime**. It was bloodless because **Buhari was not in Lagos and his vice president, Idiagbon, was out of the country**. **Buhari was detained in Benin City until 1988; Idiagbon was placed under house arrest for 3 years.** Babangida **shifted the handover date on three different occasions and ruled Nigeria until 1993**.

**Coup 7.** The plotters **attacked Dodan Barracks, where Babangida was present, but he escaped through a back route**. **Orkar seized control of the Federal Radio Corporation of Nigeria (FRCN) and announced the secession of the southern part of Nigeria from the north.** The declaration was quashed and **the coup was put down within 10 hours**. **A total of 42 men, including military personnel and civilians, were arrested, convicted and summarily executed in July 1990.**

**Coup 8.** Following the **annulment of June 12**, Babangida **handed over power to an interim government with Ernest Shonekan as interim president**. **On 17 November 1993, just three months into Shonekan's reign, Abacha launched a coup**, citing the **stagnant nature of the Shonekan government and his inability to manage the democratic process**.

**The end of military rule.** **In 1998, General Sani Abacha died in mysterious circumstances**; many believed he had **stepped on too many toes** following his **arrest of Olusegun Obasanjo** and **Abiola's continued detention**. **After Abacha's death power was transferred to Abubakar Abdulsalam, who supervised the shortest transition to democracy.** **Olusegun Obasanjo came from prison to contest the presidential election against Olu Falae in 1999, which he won to become elected president of the Fourth Republic.**

**Pros of military rule (six):** 1. **they kept Nigeria as one**; 2. **infrastructural development**; 3. **the relocation of the FCT to Abuja**; 4. **creation of states**; 5. **bold policy decisions like NYSC and ECOWAS**; 6. **intentional foreign policy thrust under Murtala Muhammed**.

**Cons (eleven):** 1. **the military was responsible for the civil war**; 2. **corruption and impoverishment**; 3. **lack of checks and balances**; 4. **uncoordinated policy thrust**; 5. **lacked the requisite administrative acumen**; 6. **wanton killings and violations of human rights**; 7. **suppression of the press**; 8. **coups leading to economic uncertainty**; 9. **mismanagement of the oil boom**; 10. **stooges of IGOs like the IMF**; 11. **lack of respect for the rule of law and the judiciary**.

**Verdict.** **Awolowo alluded to the fact that the best military government cannot be better than the worst civilian government.** Like slavery and colonialism, **military rule destroyed existing structures of progress** and left **a legacy of criminality, abuse of the rule of law, and underdevelopment**.

**Where people go wrong.** **Nzeogwu led but did not rule; Ironsi ruled but did not plot.** **Garba led the 1975 plot; Murtala took power.** **Dimka killed Murtala; Obasanjo took power.** **Buhari appears as 1983 coup-maker and 1985 coup victim.** **Egypt 1952 is Africa's first coup, not Nigeria's.**

## Part 9 — Nigeria's Modern Economic Realities (Week 6)

**Nigeria is the most populous and the largest country in Africa.** **According to the 2006 census the population stands at 140,431,790** (*the slide writes "140,431,790 million" — an extraction error*). **By 2015 it was estimated at 180 million persons**, **first in Africa and the sixth most populated nation in the world, after China, India, the USA, Indonesia and Brazil.** Resources: **crude oil, gold, tin, iron ore, niobium, lead, zinc, limestone, salt**, plus arable land producing **palm oil, cocoa, groundnut, beans, melon, corn and rice**. **Agriculture was the hub of Nigeria's economy before the advent of oil in commercial quantity**; with oil, the focus shifted from an **agriculture-dependent** to a **crude-oil-dependent** nation.

**At independence.** **Before independence, agriculture accounted for over fifty per cent of GDP**, and **in 1960 it absorbed over 75% of the workforce**. **Development Corporations played a significant role in the regional political structure.** **GDP jumped from 1.89% in 1960 to 4.89% in 1965**, and **Nigeria was tipped to be one of the 20 largest economies in the world by 1980, ahead of Malaysia**.

**Post-civil-war sequence.** 1. The coups and the war **truncated economic progress as the military government destroyed the economic structure on which the regional political system was built**. 2. **By the 1970s there was an oil boom, and the government did not save for rainy days.** 3. **Soon after there was a crunch in the oil market**, agriculture having been abandoned for easy oil money. 4. **The nation struggled in the mid-1980s, and austerity measures were introduced during the Buhari regime.** 5. **The IMF pressured African countries, including Nigeria, to adopt the Structural Adjustment Programme.**

**The SAP era.** 1. **SAP further weakened the economy, as the currency began a downward movement since then.** 2. **SAP destroyed the manufacturing base as the economy was flooded with imported goods, which made local products uncompetitive due to price and quality.** 3. **There was another oil boom in the 90s, but the then Head of State, Sani Abacha, looted the treasury and kept the funds in foreign banks.**

**Democracy and the economy.** **In 1999, following the return to democracy, GDP grew from 0.58% to 6.59%.** **Under President Olusegun Obasanjo, Nigeria got debt relief of $20 billion and foreign reserves jumped above $64 billion.** **Following the death of President Yar'Adua, his Vice, Goodluck Jonathan, took over; the savings of the previous administration were expended with reckless abandon, and reserves dropped to $34 billion in 2015**, even though **oil prices stood at over $100 for most of that administration**. **Owing to shale oil production in the US, in 2014 the US — the largest buyer of Nigeria's crude — announced its reduced purchase.** **Olivier Jakob**, a Swiss-based consultant, said: "It's a very plausible scenario that one day the U.S. won't need to import crude oil from Nigeria; the U.S. is awash with light crude. Nigeria crude may need to be placed at a discount to go to new markets in Asia." **India replaced the US as the major buyer**, and **the budget has been in deficit, with borrowing to balance it**.

**Causes of Nigeria's economic woes:** 1. **over-dependence on crude oil as a major source of revenue for the government**; 2. **the level of corruption**; 3. **in the banking sector, insider abuses, non-adherence to the corporate governance code and too many non-performing loans; in the capital market, round-tripping and other corrupt practices, which have led to crashes of share prices and the economic meltdown**; 4. **poor economic policies**; 5. **the spate of violence and oil pipeline vandalisation**; 6. **lack of economic planning and policy direction, untimely implementation of the national budget, and unguided utterances**. *Contradiction: the lecture's summary slide says "Five causes of economic problems in Nigeria were identified", but the causes slide carries six bullets. If five are demanded, merge the two corruption bullets.*

**Solutions (ten):** 1. **economic diversification**; 2. **economic tutelage from a metropolitan state**; 3. **curb corruption**; 4. **provide electricity to boost manufacturing**; 5. **build industrial parks and clusters**; 6. **boost local refining capacity**; 7. **improve the political-economic structure of the country**; 8. **reduce the cost of governance**; 9. **effective tax collection**; 10. **invest in tourism**.

**Conclusion.** **Nigeria's economic problems will worsen if nothing is done to diversify away from crude oil; renewable energy is gradually becoming popular, hence less need for crude. One of Nigeria's most vital resources is its people, and the government should invest more in Nigerians.**

**The pre-colonial economy (week 6 summary; no slides survive).** **Nigeria's indigenous trade system thrived between 1500 and 1800 AD.** **The indigenous economies were shaped by geographical factors**, with regions specialising in **agriculture, trade and craftsmanship**; **communities traded to exchange goods they produced for those they needed**; **the Niger River played a pivotal role in trade growth**; **international markets attracted traders from various regions**; **the apprenticeship system played a vital role in passing down skills and knowledge**; and **colonial intrusion transformed the precolonial economy into the Western capitalist economic model**.

**Where people go wrong.** **1.89% to 4.89% is 1960–65; 0.58% to 6.59% is from 1999.** **$20bn debt relief, $64bn reserve peak, $34bn in 2015.** **140,431,790 is 2006; 180 million is 2015.** **Over 50% of GDP and over 75% of the workforce are different measures — do not swap the percentages.**

## Part 10 — Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (Week 7)

**Human rights** are **rights inherent to all human beings, regardless of race, sex, nationality, ethnicity, language, religion, or any other status.** **Article 1 of the UN Declaration of Human Rights:** "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. They are endowed with reason and conscience and should act towards one another in the spirit of brotherhood." **A human rights abuse is anything that harms someone's human rights. They include harm to people, communities, and the environment.**

**Democracy** (week 7 summary) is **the government of the people, by the people, and for the people**, the most legitimate form of governance in modern times, emphasising **free and egalitarian societies built around strong institutions rather than individuals or groups**. **The rule of law ensures that a nation is governed by laws rather than arbitrary decisions by government officials.** **Its three key principles: 1. supremacy of law; 2. equality before the law; 3. protection of individual rights through the courts.** **Nigeria is classified as a hybrid state on the Democracy Index**, because of its challenges in meeting the criteria of a full democracy.

**Where the rights live.** **Human rights are contained in Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution, Sections 33 to 46.** **The African Charter on Human and People's Rights also guarantees that rights are enforceable in Nigerian courts of law.** **The 1979 Constitution introduced Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy and Fundamental Human Rights**, though **there are still shortcomings and inhibitions in the 2009 Rules**.

**The five rights named:** 1. **freedom of religion**; 2. **freedom of speech**; 3. **right to education**; 4. **right to freedom of movement**; 5. **right to life**. More generally the course lists **the right to life and liberty, freedom from slavery and torture, freedom of opinion and expression, the right to work and education, and many more**.

**The five common human rights abuses in Nigeria:** 1. **extra-judicial killing**; 2. **rape**; 3. **freedom of expression** (its violation); 4. **child marriage**; 5. **domestic violence**.

**1. Extra-judicial killing.** **The Nigerian security forces, particularly the police and military, have been implicated in numerous cases of extrajudicial killings, torture, and excessive use of force**, and the **#EndSARS protests in October 2020 brought international attention to police brutality**. **Four named examples: law enforcement officials; non-state actors; death sentences; deaths in custody due to the use of force or failure to protect the right to life.**

**2. Rape.** **Five named challenges:** 1. **social stigma**; 2. **underreporting of cases**; 3. **inadequate legal frameworks**; 4. **lack of access to justice**; 5. **limited support services for survivors**. **There were about 367,000 women and gender-related issues in Nigeria in 2021.**

**3. Freedom of expression.** The government has been criticised for **restricting freedom of speech and press, including freedom of press and media and online media**; **critics reported being subjected to threats, intimidation, arrest, detention and sometimes violence**; **security services detained and harassed journalists**, sometimes for reporting on **political corruption and security**; **the government controlled much of the electronic media through the National Broadcasting Commission (NBC)**, which is **responsible for monitoring and regulating broadcast media**; and **it used regulatory oversight to restrict press freedom, notably by clamping down on television and radio stations**.

**4. Child marriage.** **A discriminatory practice affecting mostly girls and prohibited under international law.** It violates international human rights law if a State fails to: 1. **establish a minimum age of marriage**; 2. **enforce it**; 3. **provide for and enforce the same minimum age of marriage for boys and girls**; 4. **act with due diligence to prevent it**; or 5. **if it involves public officials in it**. **43% of girls in Nigeria are married before their 18th birthday, and 16% are married before the age of 15 (Aliogo, 2023).**

**5. Domestic violence.** **The Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act was signed into law in 2015**, criminalising various forms of abuse and providing **mechanisms for reporting, prosecution and support services for survivors**. **Four remaining challenges:** 1. **underreporting due to social stigma**; 2. **inadequate enforcement of laws**; 3. **limited awareness and education**; 4. **the need for more accessible support services**.

**National Human Rights Commission figures: 1,701,519 cases of rights violations in 2021; 1,287,280 complaints in 2020.**

**Conclusion.** **A nation's attitudes towards human rights are indicators of the values that its political and other leaders have espoused regarding human life. Human rights are not validated by the number of international treaties that countries have signed; the international documents are just standards for the additional safeguards individuals demand from the state. Every government agency has to enforce the norms established by the Constitution, and penalties should likewise be imposed regardless of socioeconomic class.**

**Where people go wrong.** **Chapter IV = rights; Chapter III = citizenship.** **1979 introduced the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles; 1999 holds Chapter IV; the 2009 Rules still fall short.** **VAPP is 2015, and it is about domestic violence, not child marriage.** **43% before 18, 16% before 15.** **1,701,519 is 2021; 1,287,280 is 2020.** **"Hybrid state" is the Democracy Index label — not "flawed democracy".**

**In the exam.** "Human rights are contained in which chapter and sections of the 1999 Constitution?" (Chapter IV, Sections 33 to 46.) "State five common human rights abuses in Nigeria." "When was the VAPP Act signed into law?" (2015.) "How many violations did the NHRC receive in 2021?" (1,701,519.)

## Part 11 — Citizenship and Civic Responsibilities (Week 8)

**Citizenship** is **the state of being a citizen, as well as the rights, obligations and advantages that come with it.** You have **the right to live, work, vote and pay taxes** in a nation if you have citizenship there. **Citizenship is usually granted in the nation where you were born; if you are an immigrant from another nation, you must apply.** **Civic responsibility** refers to **the duties and obligations that citizens are expected to fulfil for the betterment of their society and the functioning of their country**, outlined in **various laws, policies and cultural expectations**. **Naturalisation** is **the process by which an individual becomes a legal citizen of a specific country — the formal act of granting citizenship to a foreign national who meets the predetermined eligibility criteria established by the country's laws and regulations.**

**History.** **Citizenship is often confused with nationality, which is of a wider scope than citizenship.** **Before the date of independence there were no Nigerian citizens.** **Since 1979 there have been significant changes in the law**, namely **the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1979, the Nigeria Citizenship Act 1960, and the Nigerian Citizenship Act 1961** (the slide's own order; chronologically it is 1960, 1961, 1979).

**Chapter three of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended) provides extensively for citizenship.** **Three routes:** 1. **by birth**; 2. **by registration**; 3. **by naturalisation**.

**By birth**, a person is a Nigerian citizen if: 1. **both parents are Nigerians**; 2. **either parent is a Nigerian**; 3. **any one of the grandparents is a Nigerian**.

**By registration**, open to: 1. **a non-Nigerian woman who is married to a Nigerian**; 2. **every person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparent is a citizen of Nigeria**. The applicant must prove to the President: 1. **that he or she is a person of good character**; 2. **that he or she has shown a clear intention of the desire to be domiciled, live or reside in Nigeria**; 3. **that he or she has taken the prescribed Oath of Allegiance to Nigeria contained in the Seventh Schedule to the 1999 Constitution**.

**By naturalisation**, one fulfils the requirements **stipulated in section 27 of the 1999 Constitution**, after which **a certificate of grant of citizenship by naturalisation is issued**. This is the route for **any foreigner who has lived for a long time in Nigeria**.

**Dual citizenship.** **The Nigerian Constitution allows dual citizenship for a foreigner who is a citizen of another country by birth to also be a citizen of Nigeria, by registration or by naturalisation. A citizen by birth can also acquire the citizenship of another country without renouncing his Nigerian citizenship.**

**Renunciation.** **A citizen of Nigeria who is of full age has the right to renounce and abandon his Nigerian citizenship and take up the citizenship of any other country of his choice, by making the prescribed declaration to that effect.** **The President may refuse to register such a declaration where it was made: 1. when Nigeria is involved in a war; or 2. contrary to public policy.**

**Loss of citizenship.** **The President may deprive a person who is not a Nigerian by birth of Nigerian citizenship in the following circumstances. 1. If the person is sentenced to a term of imprisonment of not less than 3 years within a period of 7 years after becoming a citizen of Nigeria by naturalisation. 2. Persons who acquired Nigerian citizenship by naturalisation or by registration may lose it if: A. the person is disloyal to Nigeria by act or speech; B. the person traded with, engaged, or associated with any business with an enemy country at war with Nigeria; C. assisted the enemy country at war with Nigeria in any manner; D. unlawfully communicated with such enemy country to the detriment of, or with the intention to cause damage to, Nigeria.** Hook: **"3 inside 7."**

**The six civic responsibilities:** 1. **obey the law**; 2. **protection of public property**; 3. **payment of taxes**; 4. **rendering national service**; 5. **loyalty**; 6. **respect for others' rights**.

**Section 24 of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended) — it shall be the duty of every citizen to: A. abide by this Constitution, respect its ideals and its institutions, the National Flag, the National Anthem, the National Pledge, and legitimate authorities; B. help to enhance the power, prestige and good name of Nigeria, defend Nigeria and render such national service as may be required; C. respect the dignity of other citizens and the rights and legitimate interests of others and live in unity and harmony and in the spirit of common brotherhood; D. make positive and useful contribution to the advancement, progress and well-being of the community where he resides; E. render assistance to appropriate and lawful agencies in the maintenance of law and order; and F. declare his income honestly to appropriate and lawful agencies and pay his tax promptly.** **Civic responsibility is not only a legal obligation but also a moral duty that promotes active citizenship.**

**The judiciary (week 8 summary; no slides).** **The Nigerian judiciary is often regarded as the third branch of government, alongside the executive and legislative branches. Its historical development includes pre-colonial indigenous judicial systems and the British colonial influence, which led to the establishment of a hierarchical court system. It interprets the constitution and ensures the rule of law.**

**Where people go wrong.** **Chapter III citizenship, Chapter IV rights, Section 24 duties, Section 27 naturalisation, Seventh Schedule oath** — five references, five jobs, and the biggest source of lost marks in the course. **A citizen by birth cannot be deprived of citizenship.** **3 years' imprisonment within 7 years, not the reverse.** **Registration is for a foreign wife or an adult born abroad with a Nigerian grandparent; naturalisation is for long residence under section 27.** **The two refusal grounds for renunciation are war and public policy — nothing about age, education or destination.**

**In the exam.** "State the three ways of acquiring Nigerian citizenship." "When may the President refuse to register a renunciation?" (War; contrary to public policy.) "Civic responsibilities are contained in which section?" (Section 24.)

## Part 12 — Reorientation Strategies (Week 9)

**Reorientation strategies** refer to **government authorities, institutions or programmes aimed at reshaping or redirecting people's attitudes, beliefs or behaviours towards a particular purpose or set of ideals**, through **public awareness campaigns, educational programmes, skill development, counselling, and community outreach**.

**The six reorientation efforts:** 1. **National Orientation Agency (NOA)**; 2. **Operation Feed the Nation**; 3. **Austerity Measures**; 4. **War Against Indiscipline**; 5. **War Against Indiscipline and Corruption**; 6. **Mass Mobilisation for Self-reliance, Social Justice and Economic Recovery (MAMSER)**. *WAIC is listed but never explained anywhere in the source — learn it as a name on the list.*

**NOA.** **The successor of the Directorate for Social Mobilisation — MAMSER — established by Decree 100 of 1993.** It is **a government agency established to create and sustain public awareness, understanding and support for government policies, programmes and initiatives**, founded with **the mandate to reorient Nigerians towards embracing positive values, patriotism, national unity and development**. **Its eight programmes:** 1. **Nationwide Attitudinal Reorientation Programme for Students in Tertiary Institutions**; 2. **Implementation of National Ethics and Integrity Policy (Secondary Schools)**; 3. **Campaign Against Open Defecation**; 4. **Building a People of Peace (Inter-Faith Peaceful Coexistence)**; 5. **Nationwide Sensitisation on the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)**; 6. **Sensitisation on COVID-19**; 7. **Sensitisation on Routine Immunization**; 8. **Change Begins With Me: Patronise Made in Nigeria Products and Services**.

**Operation Feed the Nation.** **An agricultural initiative launched in Nigeria in 1976–80, introduced by the military government of General Olusegun Obasanjo**, with the aim of **addressing the country's food security challenges and reducing dependence on food imports**. **Urban dwellers were encouraged to garden undeveloped building plots.** The programme involved **awareness campaigns, training programmes, and the provision of inputs such as improved seeds, fertilizers and farm implements**, and stressed **sustainable agricultural practices and the conservation of natural resources**. **Its six programmes:** 1. **Agricultural Development Project (ADP), 1976**; 2. **Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme (ACGS), 1977**; 3. **River Basin Development Programme (RBDA), 1978**; 4. **Rural Banking System (RBS), 1978**; 5. **Green Revolution (GR), 1980**; 6. **Directorate for Food, Roads, and Rural Infrastructure (DFRRI), 1986**. **Four reasons it fell short:** 1. **the resources (seedlings, insecticides, tractors) were not provided as promised**; 2. **activities were not effectively performed**; 3. **farmers were sceptical of new methods and administrative officials did not do the job expected of them**; 4. **the manpower needed to expand farming operations did not materialise.** Still, it **played a significant role in raising awareness about agriculture and food production and laid the foundation for subsequent agricultural development programmes and policies.**

**Austerity measures** are **strict, frugal economic policies used by the government to manage public debt, typically undertaken during periods of economic hardship or budget deficits.** **Three waves:** 1. **after the oil boom of the 1970s, during Buhari's military regime**; 2. **the Structural Adjustment Programme of Babangida also introduced austerity**; 3. **from 2015, to plug the budget deficit arising from low oil prices and low oil output.** **Six examples implemented in Nigeria:** 1. **fiscal consolidation**; 2. **reduction of recurrent expenditure**; 3. **subsidy reforms**; 4. **revenue diversification**; 5. **debt management**; 6. **rationalisation of subsidies and social programs.** **Three main types:** 1. **higher taxes (revenue generation) to fund government spending**; 2. **revenue generation plus lower government spending**; 3. **lower taxes plus lower government spending.** **Austerity has continually failed because the government has not addressed the issue of waste in government and lower government spending, and the bulk of the burden is often shifted to the masses to carry.**

*Contradiction: the slide says austerity was introduced "during Buhari's military regime", but the mid-lesson quiz on the next slide answers **B) Babangida**, explaining that it was specifically under Babangida's SAP that these measures were further developed and implemented. In an MCQ, follow the quiz and answer **Babangida**; in an essay, say Buhari's regime introduced austerity in the mid-1980s and Babangida's SAP deepened it.*

**War Against Indiscipline.** **A mass mobilisation programme organised by the military dictatorship of Buhari with the aim of correcting social maladjustments**, aiming to **instil discipline, moral values and a sense of national pride among Nigerians**. **Four principles promoted:** 1. **punctuality**; 2. **respect for queues**; 3. **environmental cleanliness**; 4. **adherence to traffic regulations**. **Three methods:** **public sensitisation; enforcement of discipline through penalties; public works projects to improve infrastructure and cleanliness.** **The WAI achieved remarkable success as the WAI Brigade were quick to deal decisively with anyone who failed to act in accordance with the dictates of the state**, and **its utmost criticism was the high-handedness of the WAI Brigade**. **The programme ended in September 1985.**

**MAMSER.** **An initiative launched by the Nigerian government in 1987 following the recommendation of the Public Bureau headed by Samuel Cookey.** **Its primary goal was to mobilise and empower Nigerians to actively participate in nation-building, promote self-reliance, achieve social justice and drive economic recovery.** **MAMSER played a key role in the emergence of cooperative societies playing a key role in the Nigerian workplace**, and was **eventually phased out, its functions and activities absorbed into other government agencies and initiatives**.

**Why reorientation fails:** 1. **corruption**; 2. **lack of political will**; 3. **poor leadership**; 4. **exemplary leadership** (the slide's own word — read it as the absence of it); 5. **improper planning**. **There is a need for a declaration of a state of emergency on reorienting Nigerians on the values of trust, sycophancy, legitimate work and moral conscience** (again the slide's wording; the week 9 summary rephrases it as **trust, hard work, and moral conscience**).

**Where people go wrong.** **NOA 1993 (Decree 100), MAMSER 1987, OFN 1976–80, WAI ended September 1985.** **NOA succeeded MAMSER, not the reverse.** **Obasanjo ran OFN; Buhari ran WAI; Babangida ran SAP; Cookey's Public Bureau recommended MAMSER.** **DFRRI is 1986 yet is listed under OFN — give it as the slides do.**

**In the exam.** "The NOA was established by which decree and in what year?" (Decree 100 of 1993.) "Who introduced Operation Feed the Nation?" (Obasanjo, 1976–80.) "When did WAI end?" (September 1985.) "List the six programmes under OFN with their years."

## Part 13 — Pop Culture and Nigerian Popular Music (Week 10)

**Popular culture** refers to **the traditions and material culture of a particular society**; in the modern West, to **cultural products such as music, art, literature, fashion, dance, film, cyberculture, television and radio consumed by most of a society's population**. A second definition: **those types of media that have mass accessibility and appeal**. **The term "popular culture" was coined in the mid-19th century** and refers to **the cultural traditions of the people, in contrast to the "official culture" of the state or governing classes.**

**Popular music** refers to **all contemporaneous music with broad, immediate and implicitly transient attractiveness that appeals to a mass audience. Such music is quite receptive to people since they are familiar with its idioms and does not require guided listening.** It **primarily attracts urban dwellers, particularly the youth, who seek social entertainment and dancing experiences, and predominantly thrives in pubs and nightclubs.**

**Three named causes of the rise of popular culture:** 1. **scholars trace the origins of popular culture to the creation of the middle class generated by the Industrial Revolution**; 2. **people who were configured into working classes and moved into urban environments began creating their own culture and distinct lifestyle**; 3. **after the end of World War II, innovations in mass media led to significant cultural and social changes in the West.** **The rise of popular culture coincided with the rise of capitalism**, and its meaning **began to merge with mass culture, consumer culture, image culture, media culture, and culture created by manufacturers for mass consumption.**

**The evolution of Nigerian popular music has been delimited into four epochal stages.**

**First Wave: 1950–67.** **Deeply traditional**, seeing the evolution of **Juju, Apala and Nigerian Highlife**. Artists: 1. **I.K. Dairo and his Blue Spots**; 2. **Haruna Ishola and his Group**; 3. **E.C. Arinze**; 4. **Sammy Akpabot and His All Stars**; 5. **Godwin Omabuwa and his Sound Makers**; 6. **Ganiyu Kale and His Guinea Mambo Orchestra**; 7. **Sefiu Ayan**; 8. **Kasumu Adio**; 9. **Ayinla Omowura**; 10. **Bobby Benson**; 11. **Jim Lawson and the Mayor's Dance Band**; 12. **Dan Maraya**. **External influences: Ghanaian Highlife, Caribbean calypso and Mambo.** The era was **popular for its bands and stage performances**, and its music **influenced dressing, the pattern of social gathering, and also indicated class**.

**Second Wave: 1967–80.** **Highlife regained popularity in the 1970s** with 1. **Victor Olaiya**; 2. **Osadebe**; 3. **Oliver De Coque**; 4. **Oriental Brother**; 5. **Sunny Okosun**; 6. **Victor Uwaifo**; 7. **Orlando Owoh**; 8. **Christie Essien Igbokwe**. **Afrobeat also became mainstream during this epoch, with Fela becoming a household name; he addressed social ills with his brand of music.** **The Nigerian Civil War particularly affected Highlife music**, which in Nigeria had gained popularity as **feel-good and upper-class music**, whereas **in Ghana, from where it was borrowed, it was a genre that addressed social problems**. **Juju was waxing strong with Ebenezer Obey and King Sunny Ade blazing the trail; it was a song for the upper class.** **Fuji emerged as a fusion of Apala and Ajisari, drew major influences from Islam and was mostly for the lower class**, with **Sikiru Ayinde Barrister and Ayinla Kollington** its famous musicians.

**Third Wave: 1980–90.** **New sounds like Waka, Yo Pop and Afro Juju were introduced.** **Island Records signed Sunny Ade; Virgin Records signed Ebenezer Obey.** Pairings: **Salawa Abeni — Waka; Segun Adewale — Yo Pop; Shina Peters — Afro Juju; Wasiu Ayinde Marshall — Fuji**, which continued to grow. There was **a yearning to appeal to an international audience**, leading to fusion of **Reggae, Calypso, rap and R&B**. **A lot of sounds came out of Ajegunle, with Junior and Pretty leading the charge with popular songs like *Bolanle* and *Monika*.** Artists of the identity struggle: **Orits Wiliki, Blackky (Rosie), Majek Fashek, Mike Okri, Ras Kimono, Evi Edna Ogholi.** **There was a dwindling of Nigerian music at this time as the international record labels left, and Nigerian music was struggling for identity because it was sounding more American than Nigerian.**

**Fourth Wave: 1992–till present.** **The quest to sound more American than Nigerian continued**, and **the artists appealed to the youth as there was a rarity of local languages in their lyrics.** Groups: **Remedies, Plantashun Boiz, Styl Plus, P-Square and Mo'Hits Crew.** **Over time, Nigerian pop culture began to infuse local languages and styles into their craft to create a unique sound and style.** The week 11 deck adds **Burna Boy, Wizkid, Davido and Rema** as current names drawing positive attention to Nigeria.

*Note: the third wave ends in 1990 and the fourth begins in 1992; the slides leave 1990–92 unassigned. Reproduce the dates as given.*

**Where people go wrong.** **The second wave starts in 1967, not 1970** — the boundary is the civil war. **Fuji = Apala + Ajisari, Islamic, lower class; Juju = upper class.** **Island signed Sunny Ade; Virgin signed Obey.** **Highlife came from Ghana, where it addressed social problems.** **"Popular culture" was coined in the mid-19th century.**

**In the exam.** "Into how many epochal stages has Nigerian popular music been delimited?" (Four, with the dates.) "Fuji is a fusion of which two genres?" (Apala and Ajisari.) Essay: trace the four waves, with dates, genres, artists and one social observation each.

## Part 14 — Police Brutality, EndSARS and Youth Political Awakening (Week 10)

**#EndSARS is a decentralised social movement and series of mass protests against police brutality in Nigeria.** The agitation began with **a call for the disbanding of the Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS), a unit of the police, which has been attributed to a host of unlawful activities, especially against youths.** **The protest began as a hashtag on Twitter in 2017 and metamorphosed into a crisis in October 2020.** **The immediate cause of the crisis was a shooting captured on video in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian.** **Nigerian youth make up roughly 60% of the population**, and if not effectively utilised this population **could potentially lead to uprisings like the 2011 Arab Spring**. **In October 2020 Nigerian youths rose to protest the continuous operation of SARS; the protest lasted three weeks and ended with the Nigerian army shooting at unarmed protesters.**

**Two named scholars.** **Moses Ochonu** argues that **the Nigerian Police have not been decolonised**: in the colonial period they were about **"suppression of revolts"** rather than protection, and **"brute force was the primary operating philosophy of the police."** **Abimbola Oyarinu** posits that SARS began during a moment of turmoil for Nigeria — **"the falling and failing economy after the oil boom, the depreciating naira, the Nigerian civil war, the Structural Adjustment Programme, and poor leadership"** — all of which **culminated in creating an environment ripe for insecurity and violent crimes.**

**Macaulay.** **Tagged the father of Nigerian nationalism**, one of the key figures who helped lay the foundation of modern Nigeria. **During the 1920s**, when Nigeria started to witness political agitation for self-rule, he was **among the first generation of Nigerian nationalists** who protested British policies on **water rates, land issues, and the management of railway finances**. **In 1923 he founded the NNDP, Nigeria's first political party, whose members were the first to sit in the legislative council**; it **held sway until the late 1930s, when it joined forces with the Nigerian Youth Movement to form the NCNC** (see the contradiction note in Part 6).

**Awolowo.** **The first premier of the Western Region, 1954 to 1960**, and for some **Nigeria's foremost federalist**, who **advocated for federalism as the only basis for equitable national integration**. **While he led the Action Group, Awolowo was at the forefront of the campaign for a federal constitution, which was introduced in the 1954 Lyttleton Constitution.** **As the leader of the opposition he frequently challenged the policies of the Balewa-led government.** He was **one of the country's leading social democratic politicians**, standing for **economic and social development, especially in the Western region**.

**Azikiwe.** **A leading figure of modern Nigerian nationalism** who spent the better part of his life working to end British control **both as a journalist and a politician**. **He served as the last governor-general of Nigeria from 1960 to 1963, as well as the country's first elected president from 1963 to 1966 during the first republic.** **He joined politics in 1944 and later co-founded the NCNC.** **He inspired the Zikist movement, a radical revolutionary and multi-ethnic youth body which championed the Nigerian struggle against imperialism and the belief that Nigerians and indeed Africans should manage their own affairs.**

**Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti.** **She stood against British colonial rule, demanding the rights of citizens in the pre-independence era**, and **as a revolutionary used her influence to mobilise women and organise resistance against colonialism**, earning the moniker **"Lioness of Lisabi"**. **She mobilised market women for events such as picnics and festivals and, in 1953, organised a conference in Abeokuta to discuss women's welfare, which gave birth to the Federation of Nigerian Women's Societies (FNWS).** **On 18 February 1977 hundreds of soldiers raided the Lagos residence of Fela Kuti, her son, who was also a known activist; the 76-year-old Ransome-Kuti was thrown from a second-storey window, sustaining injuries that led to her death on 13 April 1978.** **Cheryl Johnson-Odim** described her as **"the only Nigerian woman of her time to rank as such a national figure or have the international exposure and connections of Ransome-Kuti."**

**Fela.** **Nigeria's most famous musician**, who **used words as a form of protest and resistance and also as a means of reflecting on and commenting on political events**. **As he rose to prominence throughout the 1970s, he caused a change in the relationship between music as an art form and Nigerian socio-political discourse**, and **is widely credited as the creator of Afrobeat in the early 1970s, which rapidly became the most avidly followed African style of music.**

**Achebe.** **One of the most acclaimed novelists of his generation**, famous for **unsentimental depictions of the social and psychological disorientation that follows the imposition of Western customs and values upon traditional African society**. **His first novel, *Things Fall Apart* (1958), often considered his masterpiece, is the most widely read book in modern African literature.** **He openly supported the secessionist Biafra movement and took a position as head of the Biafra Broadcasting Service at great risk to his life and career.** **After the civil war he became active with the left-leaning People's Redemption Party (PRP), and in 1983 became the party's deputy national vice president.** In ***The Trouble with Nigeria*** he wrote: **"The Nigerian problem is the unwillingness or inability of its leaders to rise to the responsibility and to the challenge of personal example, which are the hallmarks of true leadership."**

**Soyinka.** **As a 33-year-old graduate in 1967 he was imprisoned by the Nigerian military government for 22 months for speaking out against the Nigerian civil war**, and **in 1994 he fled the country after the dictatorship in power charged him with treason for criticising the military junta.** **Since the return to civilian rule in 1999 he has continued to activate political discourse around the needs of the average Nigerian and the corruption of the polity.** **He received the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1986, the first African to do so**, and in his acceptance speech **focused the world's attention on the continuing injustice of white rule in South Africa and dedicated the award to the imprisoned Nelson Mandela.**

**Gani Fawehinmi.** Full name **Ganiyu "Gani" Oyesola Fawehinmi**. **He started practising in Lagos in 1965** and, **often representing those who couldn't afford his services, became known as "the people's lawyer", taking on a great number of pro bono cases.** **He played the role of human rights campaigner for 40 years, constantly challenging military rulers and defending their victims; his brushes with the authorities earned him frequent detentions and beatings, and on one occasion his books were seized and his library set on fire.** **He believed in media freedom, contributed avidly to newspapers, and often took on journalists' cases, the most notable being the still unsolved case of Dele Giwa, a magazine editor who died in a parcel bombing in 1986.** **He sought to re-open the Dele Giwa case and called on the federal attorney general to be removed for his indifference to corruption, all from his sick bed, and died in 2009 after a long battle with cancer.**

**Kanu.** **One of the most celebrated Nigerian sportsmen**, whose **stern contribution won the 1996 Olympic Gold medal for Nigeria, beating Brazil and Argentina on the road to Gold.** **He won the African Footballer award, the Premier League, the UEFA Champions League and the UEFA Cup**, and **is a UNICEF ambassador whose foundation has helped a lot of Nigerian children who had heart complications.**

**The diaspora (week 11 summary; no slides).** 1. **The Nigerian diaspora significantly contributes to national development, primarily through remittances.** 2. **Historical roots include pre-modern migrations, the slave trade, and contemporary economic migration.** 3. **Remittances have become a key source of foreign exchange and a major contributor to Nigeria's GDP.** 4. **Nigerians in the United States have notable educational and professional achievements.** 5. **Strategies are needed to harness the diaspora's potential for development.** 6. **Addressing challenges related to criminal activities within a minority of the diaspora is crucial.** The summary names **"Japa"** as the concept for the contemporary emigration experience.

**Where people go wrong.** **Azikiwe held two offices back to back — last governor-general, then first elected president.** **Awolowo's office was regional.** **Funmilayo was injured in February 1977 but died in April 1978, aged 76.** **Soyinka: 1967 imprisonment (22 months), 1986 Nobel, 1994 exile.** **1986 is a double year: Soyinka's Nobel and the Dele Giwa bombing.**

## Part 16 — Social Vices: Cultism, Kidnapping and Armed Robbery (Week 12)

**Social vices are actions that stray from accepted moral norms.** **According to Asmau**, the vices common among young men and women are **prostitution, indecent dressing, robbery, cultism, pocket-picking, drug addiction, exam fraud, hooliganism, thuggery, gambling, smoking, premarital sexual activity, and rape.**

**Social vices can be classified into two: crime and deviance.** **While both terms are used interchangeably, there are subtle differences: committing a crime violates social laws, while deviant behaviour violates social norms and rules.** The slides add: **crime specifically refers to actions prohibited by law that can result in legal consequences such as fines or imprisonment, while deviance is a broader term encompassing any behaviour, belief or condition that departs from societal norms or expectations.** Hook: **crime breaks LAW, deviance breaks NORMS, and deviance is the wider circle.**

**Four theories of deviance:** 1. **Structural Functionalism** — **deviant behaviour plays a constructive role in society because it brings the different parts of the population together to agree on what is acceptable and what is not**; 2. **Social Strain Typology** — deviant behaviour is based on **two criteria: a person's motivations or adherence to cultural goals, and a person's belief in how to attain her goals**; 3. **Conflict Theory** — deviance is **a consequence of material inequality**; 4. **Labelling Theory** — deviance is **often a consequence of having a deviant-like label applied to a person.**

**Three theories of crime:** 1. **Rational Choice Theory** — **criminals weigh cost or risk and benefits when deciding whether to commit crime or not**; 2. **Strain Theory** — **social structures within society may pressure citizens to commit crime**; 3. **Social Learning Theory** — **criminal behaviour can be acquired by observing and imitating others.** *Strain appears on both lists: as Social Strain Typology under deviance, as Strain Theory under crime.*

**Cultism.** **Campus cultism may be traced back to the early 1950s, when Prof. Wole Soyinka and some of his friends founded the Pirates Confraternity as a social group at the University of Ibadan.** The friends named: **Ralph Opara, Ikhehare Aig-Imokhuede, Sylvanus Egbuchie, Nathaniel Oyelola, Pius Oleghe, and Olumuyiwa Awe** — seven founders including Soyinka. **The founding beliefs of the confraternity were patriotic and philanthropic, as it was not intended to be a secret cult.** **Its three major goals: to resist foreign norms nonviolently but intellectually and successfully; to resurrect the period of chivalry; and to find a lasting solution to the issues of tribalism and ethnicity.** **Over time such organisations grew to deadly proportions. Today there are about forty-five (45) secret cults operating in our tertiary institutions of learning, all equipped with an elaborate hierarchy, insignia and distinct attire.**

**Six activities of cultists:** 1. **meeting in very odd places like cemeteries, valleys or abandoned buildings**; 2. **the use of dangerous weapons such as knives, daggers, guns, axes and arrows against erring members or non-members**; 3. **hand warfare, burglary, killing, thuggery, assault, torture**; 4. **taking of hard drugs, drinking of human blood**; 5. **acid bath**; 6. **extravagant spending on non-profit ventures.**

**Nine reasons people join cult groups:** 1. **inherent instincts in human beings to inflict pain, punishment and even death on fellow human beings**; 2. **hostile and uncaring parenting**; 3. **failing or expelled students seeking to set the college on rampage**; 4. **rivalry and competition as a result of seeking for girls**; 5. **protection from boys of rival cult groups**; 6. **protection from victimisation from lecturers, and peer influence**; 7. **militarisation of the Nigerian polity**; 8. **erosion of educational standards**; 9. **societal decadence.**

**Punishment for cultism.** **Cultism poses a significant threat to social harmony and stability. It leads to the loss of lives, disrupts academic pursuits, and fosters a culture of fear within affected communities, hence it is classified as a crime.** 1. **Anyone who allows cult meetings in their residence, or in a structure that belongs to them, is regarded as committing a felony and will receive a 3-year sentence.** 2. **The law stipulates a jail term of 21 years for anyone found to be a member of a cult group or who solicits for members of an unlawful society.** These are **the only two legal penalties stated anywhere in the course**, and the statute is not named. Hook: **"Host 3, Join 21."**

**Kidnapping** involves **the unlawful abduction and detention of individuals, usually for financial gain or other motives such as ransom or revenge. It is a heinous crime that inflicts trauma on victims and their families, creates fear in society, and discourages investment and economic development.** **In 2018 there were 838 reported kidnap cases in Nigeria; in 2022 the cases had risen to 4,600**, which the slide calls **"an indication that kidnap cases had quadrupled"** (*838 to 4,600 is closer to five and a half times; give both figures, and the course's word if the question demands it*).

**Two causes of kidnapping:** 1. **unemployment** — **"some frustrated graduates, after searching for jobs for up to three or more years, decide to engage in this atrocity for the sole aim of survival"**; 2. **the political factor** — **"many politicians engage the services of kidnappers to deal with their political opponents and their political power, popularity and economic muscles."**

**Ten effects of kidnapping:** 1. **humanitarian crisis**; 2. **loss of lives**; 3. **economic impact**; 4. **security concerns**; 5. **negative image**; 6. **ransom culture**; 7. **deterioration of social trust**; 8. **escalation of violence**; 9. **impact on education**; 10. **human trafficking.** It also **undermines personal security, disrupts businesses and tourism, and puts tremendous pressure on law enforcement agencies.**

**Armed robbery** refers to **the use of weapons to steal from individuals, homes, businesses or vehicles. This crime endangers lives and property and often involves violence and brutality.** It **creates fear among citizens, reduces trust in law enforcement, and hampers economic growth.** **Prison records show that armed robbery cases in 2013 were 11,858; in 2014 it reduced to 10,249.**

**Six causes of armed robbery:** 1. **socio-economic factor**; 2. **corruption**; 3. **weak law enforcement**; 4. **drug abuse**; 5. **proliferation of firearms**; 6. **societal factor.**

**Five effects of armed robbery:** 1. **loss of lives and injuries**; 2. **psychological impact**; 3. **economic consequences**; 4. **disruption of daily life**; 5. **erosion of trust.**

**Conclusion.** **Kidnapping, cultism, armed robbery and gang violence are still on the increase in Nigeria due to the government's inability to curb it. The six geo-political zones are facing different versions of kidnapping, with Boko Haram insurgency and herdsmen attacks in the North East and North West, militants in the Niger Delta region or the South-South and South East, and criminal opportunists committing kidnapping for ransom.** **Corruption is also linked to kidnapping, with politicians using youths as political thugs**, and **the relationship between cult groups and other criminal groups engaged in armed banditry is not in doubt, because access to small arms and light weapons motivates interaction between cult groups and armed robbers who terrorise residential areas in their bid to raise funds.** The week 12 summary adds the **Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC)** as the agency created to fight corruption, and lists the vices as **bribery, corruption, cultism, examination malpractice, kidnapping, and armed robbery.**

**Where people go wrong.** **Crime breaks law, deviance breaks norms** — not synonyms. **Structural Functionalism says deviance is constructive**; it sounds wrong, but it is the course's answer. **Strain sits on both theory lists.** **Soyinka founded the Pirates Confraternity, and it was not intended to be a secret cult.** **3 years for hosting, 21 for membership** — reversing these is the most expensive error in week 12. **838 and 4,600 are kidnapping; 11,858 and 10,249 are armed robbery — armed robbery fell while kidnapping rose.**

**In the exam.** "Distinguish between crime and deviance." "State four theories of deviance and three theories of crime." "What is the penalty for being a member of a cult group?" (21 years.) "How many secret cults operate in Nigerian tertiary institutions?" (About 45.)
